*Every Technical Term, in Plain English*

**Companion to:** all six reference documents

## 1. Studying texts

**textual criticism** — Comparing surviving copies of a text to work out what the earliest version said. Not 'criticism' in the sense of attacking it.

**source criticism** — Working out what earlier written sources an author used.

**form criticism** — Studying the small units (sayings, miracle stories) and how they were shaped by oral retelling before being written down.

**redaction** — Editing. A redactor is an editor who reshaped an existing text.

**redaction criticism** — Studying what an editor changed, added, or cut — and what that reveals about their agenda.

**exegesis** — Drawing the meaning out of a text — reading what it says.

**eisegesis** — Reading your own meaning into a text. The opposite of exegesis, and always an accusation.

**hermeneutics** — The rules you use for interpreting a text. Your method, not your conclusion.

**stylometry** — Statistical analysis of writing style — word frequency, sentence length — used to test whether one person wrote two texts.

**provenance** — Where an object or text came from, and its chain of custody.

**philology** — Close study of language and word meanings in historical texts.

**consensus** — What most specialists in a field currently accept. Always ask: consensus among whom?

**critical scholar** — Someone studying the Bible as a historical document, without assuming it's divinely inspired. Includes many believers.

**minimalist** — A scholar who thinks very little of the Hebrew Bible's history is historically reliable.


## 2. Translation

**formal equivalence** — Word-for-word translation. Follows the original sentence structure closely, even when it reads awkwardly.

**functional equivalence** — Thought-for-thought translation. Renders the meaning in natural English, which means someone already decided what it means.

**dynamic equivalence** — Older name for functional equivalence — thought-for-thought translation.

**Textus Receptus** — The 16th-century Greek New Testament based on a few late manuscripts. The basis of the KJV.

**critical text** — The modern reconstructed Greek New Testament, built by comparing all known manuscripts. Behind most modern translations.

**Johannine Comma** — The explicit Trinity wording at 1 John 5:7-8. In the KJV; absent from every Greek manuscript before the 14th century.

**complementarian** — The view that men and women have different God-given roles, with male authority in church and marriage.

**almah** — Hebrew for a young woman of marriageable age. Not the specific word for virgin, which is betulah.

**arsenokoitai** — A rare Greek compound in 1 Corinthians 6:9, probably coined from the Greek of Leviticus 20:13. Its exact reference is debated.

**Sheol** — The Hebrew place of the dead — the grave. No fire, no torment. The KJV translates it 'hell', which misleads.

**Targum** — An ancient Aramaic translation-paraphrase of the Hebrew Bible.


## 3. Texts & versions

**Septuagint** — The Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures, made around 250 BCE. What most New Testament authors actually quote.

**Masoretic Text** — The standard Hebrew Bible text, preserved by Jewish scribes; our oldest complete copies are medieval.

**Vulgate** — Jerome's Latin translation, c. 400 CE. The standard Bible in the West for over a thousand years.

**Peshitta** — The standard Syriac (Aramaic) Bible of the Eastern churches.

**Torah** — The first five books — Genesis to Deuteronomy. Also means 'teaching' or 'law' generally.

**Pentateuch** — Greek term for the same first five books.

**Tanakh** — The Jewish Bible: Torah (law), Nevi'im (prophets), Ketuvim (writings). Same books as the Protestant Old Testament, different order.

**Talmud** — The vast rabbinic collection of law and debate, compiled roughly 200–600 CE.

**Mishnah** — The earliest layer of rabbinic law, c. 200 CE. The Talmud is built around it.

**midrash** — Rabbinic interpretation that fills gaps and draws lessons from the biblical text.

**codex** — A book with bound pages, as opposed to a scroll.

**uncial** — A manuscript written in all capital Greek letters. Generally the older ones.

**minuscule** — A manuscript in lowercase cursive Greek. Generally later, medieval.

**papyrus** — Writing material made from reed. The earliest New Testament fragments are papyri.

**recension** — An officially revised and standardized edition of a text.

**interpolation** — Words inserted into a text by a later hand, presented as if original.

**canon** — The official list of books counted as scripture.

**apocryphal** — Outside the canon. Not necessarily fake — just not included.

**patristic** — Relating to the Church Fathers, the Christian writers of roughly 100–500 CE.


## 4. Who wrote what

**pseudepigraphy** — Writing under a famous person's name. Common in the ancient world; whether it counted as honest is disputed.

**pseudonymous** — Written under someone else's name — not by the person it claims.

**deutero-Pauline** — 'Second' Paul. The letters attributed to Paul that most critical scholars think he didn't write.

**undisputed letters** — The seven letters nearly all scholars accept Paul actually wrote: Romans, 1–2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, Philemon.

**Documentary Hypothesis** — The theory that the Torah was woven together from four earlier written sources (labelled J, E, D, P).

**neo-documentarian** — A modern scholar who still defends a version of the four-source theory. Mostly North American and Israeli.

**Synoptic Problem** — The question of who copied whom among Matthew, Mark, and Luke.

**Markan priority** — The majority view that Mark was written first and Matthew and Luke used it.


## 5. Theology

**eschatology** — Beliefs about the end — death, judgement, the end of the world.

**realized eschatology** — The view that the promised end has already arrived in some sense — eternal life available now rather than later.

**parousia** — The second coming of Christ. Greek for 'arrival' or 'presence'.

**apocalyptic** — A genre and worldview: the present age is corrupt, God will intervene catastrophically and soon. Not the same as 'apocalypse means disaster'.

**kerygma** — The core preached message of the earliest Christians — the basic outline repeated in sermons.

**Christology** — The theology of who Jesus is. 'High' Christology treats him as divine; 'low' as human or exalted-but-created.

**homoousios** — Greek: 'same substance'. The word Nicaea chose to say the Son is the same being as the Father.

**Arianism** — The view that the Son was created by the Father and therefore not co-eternal. The losing side at Nicaea.

**free will** — The disputed kind of control over action needed for genuine agency or moral responsibility. Philosophers disagree about whether it requires alternative possibilities, ultimate sourcehood, or neither.

**free choice (project working term)** — In this corpus, selecting among presented options without assuming the stronger metaphysical claim of libertarian free will. This is a project distinction, not a universally standardized philosophical usage.

**libertarian free will** — The view that genuine freedom is incompatible with determinism; libertarian accounts usually require the agent to be an appropriate source of the action and commonly require a real ability to do otherwise.

**compatibilism** — The view that free will or moral responsibility can coexist with determinism. Different compatibilist theories define the required freedom differently.

**theological fatalism** — The argument that infallible divine foreknowledge makes future human acts necessary in a way that threatens libertarian freedom.

**open theism** — A Christian view on which the future includes genuinely open free decisions, so God does not have exhaustive settled foreknowledge of those undetermined choices. Open theists usually define omniscience as knowing every truth that can be known.

**omniscience** — Complete knowledge; in classical theism this normally includes exhaustive knowledge of the actual future, though open theists dispute that future free contingents are settled truths available to be known.

**omnipotence** — Maximal divine power, usually formulated as the power to do anything logically possible rather than the power to make contradictions true.

**omnibenevolence** — Perfect or unsurpassable goodness attributed to God.

**Oriental Orthodox** — The family including the Coptic, Syriac, Armenian, Ethiopian, Eritrean, and Malankara Orthodox churches. They accept the first three ecumenical councils and did not accept the Council of Chalcedon (451); they are distinct from Eastern Orthodox churches.

**Council of Chalcedon** — The 451 CE council that defined Christ as one person in two natures, divine and human. Its formula was accepted by the churches that became Eastern Orthodox and Roman Catholic and rejected by the churches now called Oriental Orthodox.

**miaphysitism** — The Christological language associated with the Oriental Orthodox tradition: Christ has one united incarnate nature that is fully divine and fully human. It should not simply be equated with Monophysitism, which implies the humanity is absorbed or denied.

**theodicy** — An argument defending God's goodness given the existence of suffering.

**skeptical theism** — The reply that we shouldn't expect to understand God's reasons, so suffering isn't evidence against God.

**typology** — Reading an Old Testament person or event as a preview of a New Testament one.

**allegory** — Reading a text as standing for something else entirely, beyond its plain sense.

**quadriga** — The medieval four-layer reading method: literal, allegorical, moral, and future/heavenly senses.

**sensus plenior** — 'Fuller sense'. The claim that God intended a deeper meaning the human author didn't know about.

**accommodation** — The idea that God spoke in terms the original audience could understand, even if inexact.

**inerrancy** — The doctrine that the Bible contains no errors of any kind, including historical and scientific.

**dispensationalism** — A Protestant system dividing history into eras with different rules. Popular in American evangelicalism.

**covenant theology** — A Protestant system organizing scripture around a series of covenants rather than eras.

**proof text** — A verse quoted to support a position, usually stripped of its context.

**unfalsifiable** — Structured so that no possible evidence could count against it. Usually a weakness, not a strength.

**properly basic** — A belief that's rational to hold without arguing for it — like trusting your memory.


## 6. History & archaeology

**stele** — An upright stone slab with an inscription. Plural: stelae.

**etiology** — A story explaining why something exists or has its name — an origin story.

**Deuteronomistic history** — Joshua through 2 Kings, read as one work shaped by the theology of Deuteronomy.

**Second Temple period** — c. 516 BCE to 70 CE — between the rebuilding and the destruction of the Temple. When most distinctive first-century Jewish beliefs formed.

**prefect** — The Roman governor of a minor province. Pilate's actual title.

**Bronze Age collapse** — The roughly 1200 BCE breakdown of palace civilizations across the eastern Mediterranean.

**low chronology** — Finkelstein's downward redating of Israelite archaeological layers, which shrinks the united monarchy.


## 7. Contested specifics

**Testimonium Flavianum** — The passage in Josephus describing Jesus. Partly altered by Christian copyists; how much is disputed.

**mythicism** — The claim that Jesus never existed as a person. A fringe position among historians, including atheist ones.

**Ioudaioi** — The Greek word in John usually translated 'the Jews'. Whether it should be 'Judeans' is heavily contested.

**gematria** — Assigning number values to Hebrew letters and reading meaning into the totals.

**mitzvot** — Commandments. Singular: mitzvah.

**eleph** — The Hebrew word usually read as 'thousand', which some argue meant a clan or squad instead.

**minimal facts** — Habermas's method: build a resurrection case only from claims most critical scholars grant.


## 8. Islamic sources

**sira** — The traditional biographies of Muhammad, written 100–200 years after his death.

**hadith** — Reports of Muhammad's sayings and actions, collected and graded for reliability.

**isnad** — The chain of transmitters attached to a hadith, used to judge how reliable it is.
