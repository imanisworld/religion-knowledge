Ancient explanations · medical history · source limits

When Illness Was Explained by Gods and Spirits

About the evidence: These comparisons use accessible ancient texts, translations, historical scholarship and medical histories. Some original-language texts, primary Mari tablets and academic chapters have not been examined directly. The interpretations that depend on them remain qualified.

Question: Before germ theory, how did people explain disease, seizures, disability, and suffering? When did divine punishment, spirits, ritual impurity, and natural causes appear in the same tradition? What changed when infectious causes could be tested?

Short answer: Some ancient writings explicitly attribute illness or plague to divine anger or spirits. Other ancient writings describe contagion, physical treatment, physicians, and objections to treating illness as a consequence of sin. These explanations overlapped. Nineteenth-century germ theory greatly expanded knowledge about the mechanisms of infectious diseases; it did not create the first natural account of illness.

Some original-language and scholarly sources remain unchecked; the study identifies those uncertainties where they matter.

01

Terms and evidence rules

⌄
  • God of the gaps is a modern label for inferring divine action because an observed event lacks an identified natural explanation. It does not describe every ancient appeal to a god. A writer can give a theological explanation of purpose or responsibility while recognizing an observable medical or contagious process.
  • Divine punishment, spirit affliction, ritual impurity, contagion, and bodily causes are distinct explanatory categories. Record them separately; some texts combine them.
  • A text describes its author's or narrator's account. It does not establish the actual cause of an ancient patient's condition or prove that everyone in a region shared that account.
  • A modern diagnosis cannot be established from a short ancient narrative. “Seizure-like symptoms” describes what a narrative reports; it does not confirm epilepsy.
  • Germ theory applies to infectious disease and its microbial causes. Cancer, inherited disorders, injuries, many mental health conditions, and other illnesses require their own causal accounts.
02

Ancient texts: what is actually attributed to whom?

⌄

Deuteronomy 28:21–22, Hebrew Bible covenant curses

What the source says
In covenant curses, Yahweh threatens disobedient Israel with disease, fever, and other afflictions.
What this establishes
Illness can function as covenant punishment in this passage.
What it does not establish
The actual causes of an ancient epidemic; that every illness was viewed as punishment.

Numbers 16:46–50, priestly narrative

What the source says
A plague is linked to divine wrath; Aaron offers incense and makes atonement; the plague ends.
What this establishes
A ritual response is presented as effective within the story.
What it does not establish
That incense biologically cured or stopped an epidemic.

Mark 9:17–29, first-century Gospel

What the source says
A boy is described as convulsing, falling, becoming rigid, and foaming at the mouth; a spirit is expelled.
What this establishes
The narrative gives a spirit-based explanation for seizure-like symptoms.
What it does not establish
A clinical diagnosis or independent medical verification of the event.

John 9:1–3, first-century/early-Christian Gospel narrative

What the source says
The disciples ask whether a man's blindness from birth resulted from his or his parents' sin; Jesus rejects both attributions. In the usual punctuation he then presents the blindness as an occasion for God's works to be revealed; some scholars attach that clause to v. 4 instead, which no longer presents the man's congenital blindness explicitly as existing for that purpose; the purpose clause instead concerns the work to be done (Poirier 2006).
What this establishes
The text challenges an automatic sin-to-disability explanation.
What it does not establish
A biological explanation of congenital blindness, or a rejection of every theological connection between illness and God.

Job 42:7, read with Job 4:7–8 and 8:4–6, Hebrew wisdom literature

What the source says
Job's friends connect suffering to wrongdoing; at the end Yahweh rebukes the friends for not speaking rightly of (or to) him; the Hebrew preposition allows either.
What this establishes
The wider book challenges a straightforward equation between suffering and deserved punishment.
What it does not establish
That Job 42:7 alone specifies which of the friends' arguments Yahweh rejected; a single, uniform biblical theory of disease.

Sirach 38:1–15, Jewish wisdom literature, early second century BCE

What the source says
The author praises physicians, medicinal plants, pharmacists, prayer, repentance, and sacrifice, and says that whoever sins against the Maker will be defiant toward the physician (v. 15). That reading of v. 15 follows the Hebrew. The Greek tradition instead says, in effect, may the sinner fall into the physician's hands, which treats the physician as a punishment; the Greek is generally harsher toward physicians (Zapff 2011).
What this establishes
Medical treatment and religious devotion are combined in a Jewish wisdom work composed around 180 BCE.
What it does not establish
That religious people rejected physicians, or that the author knew microbial causes.

Attribution: The six rows are descriptions of primary texts, not endorsements of their causal or miraculous claims. Verse translations linked above are English translations; any later argument depending on a Hebrew or Greek word must check its original-language context.

03

Naturalistic criticism already existed in antiquity

⌄

**Hippocratic treatise, On the Sacred Disease, generally situated within the late-fifth/fourth-century BCE Hippocratic corpus.** The anonymous author challenges treating the “sacred disease” (commonly associated with epilepsy) as uniquely divine, disputes purifications and incantations as an explanation for cures, and argues for bodily causes. His physiological model is not modern neurology. The work does not reject every possible reference to the divine: it argues the disease is no more divine than others.

Directly relevant to the user's observation — verified against the English text (20 October 2026): In §1, the author accuses practitioners of claiming success if patients recover while explaining deaths in terms that excuse the practitioners and blame the gods. Francis Adams translates the recovery clause as "if the person should recover, theirs would be the honor and credit" and later says "as if the gods, and not they, were to blame". See the Perseus *De morbo sacro* §1 text and the MIT Classics edition, which reproduces the same English translation. "Credit if they recover, gods if they die" is a paraphrase, not a verbatim quote. This is an ancient author's polemical accusation about practitioners, not evidence that every religious healer acted this way or that Hippocrates himself authored the treatise. The Greek critical wording and wider frequency of this practice have not been independently verified.

  • Primary witness: *On the Sacred Disease* §1–2, Francis Adams translation, Perseus.
  • Secondary context: The Cambridge Ancient History, vol. 12, “Medicine” (Cambridge University Press; chapter abstract), on the anonymous and multi-author character of the Hippocratic corpus. Abstract-level check only; do not treat a particular sentence in this chapter as page-verified.

Mesopotamian medicine. Long before modern germ theory, Mesopotamian traditions included diagnostic descriptions, treatment knowledge, and specialists whose roles shifted over time. Scholars usually describe two: the āšipu (exorcist and ritual specialist) and the asû (physician). Markham Geller [CRITICAL/HISTORY OF MEDICINE], “A New Understanding of the Origins of Ancient Babylonian Medicine,” International Medical Historical Review 8, no. 2 (2024; published in Chinese), describes exorcism and medicine as distinct areas of expertise, with diagnosis and prognosis open to practitioners of either. Source scope: UCL abstract only. Other summaries describe the two roles overlapping by the first millennium BCE (French Ministry of Culture glossary), so this was not a rigid priest/doctor split. JoAnn Scurlock, “Mesopotamian Beginnings for Greek Science?” (Oxford, 2018), pp. 35–46, is not cited in support here: its pages have not been read, and its reported discussion of contagion remains unverified.

Early evidence of contagion without microbes. Yitzhaq Feder [CRITICAL], “Tum’ah: Ritual Impurity or Fear of Contagious Disease?” (2019; updated 2026), discusses eighteenth-century BCE letters from Mari, from the reign of Zimri-Lim (c. 1775–1761 BCE on the conventional chronology), describing separation of infected servants and restrictions on sharing vessels or furniture. The letters also coexist with explanations involving divine touch, curses, and spirits. Article with translations and source notes. The standard study of one of these tablets is Edward Neufeld, "The Earliest Document of a Case of Contagious Disease in Mesopotamia (Mari Tablet ARM X, 129)," *Journal of the Ancient Near Eastern Society* 18 (1986).

Conclusion ⟨INFERENCE⟩: “Ancient people did not know germs, so they simply blamed gods” is too sweeping. Available evidence shows more than one causal framework, sometimes in the same society or text. Whether a particular writer used divine language to fill a knowledge gap or to make a moral/theological claim must be decided case by case.

04

Ritual impurity is a separate category

⌄

Leviticus 13–14 directs priests to assess a condition called ṣāraʿat (often spelled tzara'at) and regulate separation and restoration. The biblical category cannot be equated straightforwardly with Hansen's disease, the modern condition often called leprosy; the ancient descriptions do not fit one modern diagnosis.

There is interpretive disagreement about the purpose of separation. Yitzhaq Feder [CRITICAL] emphasizes ancient awareness of contagious conditions. In “Tzaraat in Light of Its Mesopotamian Parallels” (2017), Feder also notes that the priestly text explicitly states a religious rationale for excluding affected people from the camp, which should not be automatically replaced by a modern public-health rationale. Both arguments appear in Feder's study and his contagion study.

Important distinction: Ritual “unclean” does not automatically mean infectious, immoral, or medically dangerous. Mapping ritual statuses directly onto modern pathogens produces anachronisms.

05

What nineteenth-century science changed

⌄
  • 1854 — cholera transmission: Physician John Snow used deaths, interviews, and water-source patterns around London's Broad Street pump to argue for waterborne cholera transmission. He did so before identifying its specific bacterium. The outbreak was already declining when the pump handle was removed on 8 September 1854, as Snow himself noted (John Snow Archive, MSU). CDC, “150th Anniversary of John Snow and the Pump Handle” (2004).
  • Nineteenth-century microbiology: Experimental work by multiple researchers, including Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch, supplied methods for connecting particular microorganisms with particular infectious diseases. This was cumulative rather than one discovery that suddenly explained all illness. National Research Council, *Science, Medicine, and Animals*, “A Theory of Germs” (2004).
  • 1882 — tuberculosis: Koch reported the bacterial agent of tuberculosis, providing evidence for a specific infectious cause. CDC, “Centennial: Koch's Discovery of the Tubercle Bacillus” (1982).

What follows ⟨INFERENCE⟩: A microbial mechanism can independently explain transmission of a particular disease. Whether someone also interprets suffering spiritually is a separate theological judgment. Scientific explanation of disease does not, on its own, settle every metaphysical claim about a deity.

06

How this relates to “God of the gaps”

⌄

The claim “we cannot explain this disease, therefore a god or spirit caused it” commits an argument from ignorance unless independent evidence supports the supernatural cause.

But compare two distinct propositions:

  1. Mechanism claim: “A spirit produced this boy's convulsions.” Mark 9 places a spirit in the narrative as an agent. Historical inquiry can document the claim; medicine cannot diagnose an ancient individual from the narrative.
  2. Theological interpretation: “God grants knowledge to physicians.” Sirach 38 affirms doctors and medicine while retaining a religious account of ultimate meaning. Germ theory does not logically falsify that belief.

Assessment ⟨INFERENCE⟩: The evidence supports studying how supernatural explanations and bodily explanations coexist, compete, and change. It does not support inferring a coordinated effort by priests or biblical writers to invent diseases as instruments of social control. Such a motive would require specific dated institutional evidence.

07

Consequences to investigate, without assuming the answer

⌄
  • Blame and stigma: Did any identifiable community punish, shun, or deny treatment to ill or disabled people specifically because it viewed their symptoms as sin or possession? A biblical rule, by itself, does not establish how widely it was enforced.
  • Care versus ritual: Where did prayer, purification, exorcism, herbal treatment, physician care, quarantine, and public health operate together, and where were they alternatives?
  • Seizures and mental illness: Compare particular historical descriptions with what medical historians can responsibly infer. Do not retroactively assign schizophrenia, epilepsy, psychosis, or other diagnoses to named biblical figures.
  • Power and explanation: In which documented cases did clergy, healers, rulers, physicians, or patients gain or lose authority as diagnostic explanations changed?
  • Modern implications: Identify evidence from specific times and institutions for medical delay, rejection, or complementarity. Do not assume an ancient passage caused a modern practice without a transmission chain.

Louise J. Lawrence [CRITICAL/BIBLICAL STUDIES], Sense and Stigma in the Gospels: Depictions of Sensory-Disabled Characters (2013), chapter 5, pp. 98–123, “Sense, Seizures, and Illness Narratives”, is a promising modern interpretive study of the boy with seizures. Abstract examined; chapter's full argument requires a page-level audit before stronger claims are attributed to it.

09

Sources and evidence limits

⌄

Texts checked: The biblical passages discussed above were compared using available translations and textual notes. The Francis Adams English translation of On the Sacred Disease §1, Feder's studies and public histories of germ theory were also consulted.

Limits of access: The Hebrew, Greek and Akkadian texts have not received a full critical-edition comparison. The primary Mari tablets were not examined. The studies by Scurlock and Lawrence and a Cambridge ancient-medicine chapter were available only in part or at the abstract level; no conclusion here depends on treating their unread pages as verified. This survey has not undergone an outside specialist review.

Limits of inference: An ancient account can document how a writer explained illness without proving the patient's diagnosis, the disease's physical cause or the beliefs of an entire society. Where texts or translations disagree, the disagreement remains part of the evidence.

Evidence status reflects checks of accessible translations and sources; historical interpretations and unanswered source questions remain identified.
