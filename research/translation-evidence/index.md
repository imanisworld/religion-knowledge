This ledger separates categories and tests discrete cases, rather than repeating that introduction.

## How to investigate a claim that a translation was changed

1. **Name the verse and English editions or years.** Comparing KJV with NRSVue is not necessarily evidence an English committee edited the same Greek or Hebrew source.
2. **Read the actual Hebrew, Aramaic, or Greek text** and its grammatical context. The New Testament letters attributed to Paul are **Greek**, not originally Hebrew.
3. **Classify the difference:** word sense, syntax, speaker framing, punctuation, manuscript variant, or documented committee revision.
4. **Find the translator's explanation if published:** prefaces, marginal notes, footnotes, handbook, publisher's official change log. Otherwise label motive **unknown** and distinguish plausible rationale from documented reason.
5. **Show the consequences and remaining limits:** what impression changes for readers, what the larger chapter supports, and what no text evidence can establish.
6. **Check scholarly objections and alternative readings.** An ancient manuscript does not arrive with modern quotation marks and every ambiguity pre-resolved.

### 2 Corinthians 12:16 — Speaker framing {#paul-deceit}

- **Original-language phrase:** ὑπάρχων πανοῦργος δόλῳ ὑμᾶς ἔλαβον
- **Difference:** KJV presents a first-person statement; ESV adds “you say”; God's Word frames it as a question.
- **Why/how:** The surrounding verses (12:17–18) challenge an allegation that Paul exploited the Corinthian church. The UBS Handbook explains the implied accusation.
- **What it means:** The English framing can make Paul sound like he confessed deceit or like he rejected an accusation. The Greek has neither an explicit “you say” nor a question in verse 16.
- **Evidence status:** Strong contextual reading; intent not proven
- **Source trail:** [Greek word analysis](https://biblehub.com/text/2_corinthians/12-16.htm); [English parallel translations](https://www.biblegateway.com/verse/en/2%20Corinthians%2012%3A16); [UBS translators’ handbook (1993)](https://tips.translation.bible/tip_term/translation-commentary-on-2-corinthians-1216/).

### Romans 3:7 — Hypothetical speaker {#paul-lie}

- **Original-language phrase:** ἐν τῷ ἐμῷ ψεύσματι … τί ἔτι κἀγὼ … κρίνομαι
- **Difference:** Literal versions retain “my lie”; NIV introduces the argument with “Someone might argue.”
- **Why/how:** The passage develops objections in Romans 3:5–8. Greek εἰ means “if,” and τί means “why”: a conditional plus a question already exist.
- **What it means:** This is no demonstrated confession that Paul lied. The translation makes an inferred argumentative speaker explicit.
- **Evidence status:** Greek grammar verified; attribution contextual
- **Source trail:** [Greek word analysis](https://biblehub.com/text/romans/3-7.htm); [Verse translations](https://biblehub.com/romans/3-7.htm); [SIL translators’ notes](https://tips.translation.bible/tip_term/sil-translators-notes-on-romans-37/).

### Genesis 3:16 — Committee revision {#genesis-desire}

- **Original-language phrase:** אֶל־אִישֵׁךְ
- **Difference:** ESV 2016 used “contrary to your husband”; the 2025 revision restored “for your husband.”
- **Why/how:** Crossway says the restoration better preserves interpretive options for Hebrew ’el; it also replaced the contrastive “but” with “and.”
- **What it means:** A reading about antagonism between woman and man became less compulsory in English. The committee's documented reasoning can be examined; a hidden motive cannot be assumed.
- **Evidence status:** Documented by publisher
- **Source trail:** [Crossway’s 2025 explanation and change list](https://www.crossway.org/articles/esv-bible-translation-update/); [Hebrew text](https://biblehub.com/text/genesis/3-16.htm).

### Isaiah 7:14 — Lexical choice and reception {#isaiah-almah}

- **Original-language phrase:** הָעַלְמָה / παρθένος
- **Difference:** Hebrew ‘almah is rendered “young woman” in some translations, “virgin” in others. The ancient Greek Septuagint uses parthenos, which Matthew 1:23 quotes.
- **Why/how:** The Hebrew noun specifies a young woman rather than explicitly asserting virginity; the Greek and later Christian reading connect the verse to Jesus' birth.
- **What it means:** Read the Hebrew prophecy in its eighth-century BCE context and separately examine its use in Matthew. A religious interpretation should not be mistaken for an unambiguous Hebrew lexical requirement.
- **Evidence status:** Original terms documented; later interpretation debated
- **Source trail:** [Hebrew Isaiah 7:14](https://biblehub.com/text/isaiah/7-14.htm); [Jewish Bible Quarterly study (2013)](https://www.jewishbible.org/articles/isaiah-7-14b-in-new-christian-bible-translations/); [Matthew 1:23 Greek](https://biblehub.com/text/matthew/1-23.htm).

### Genesis 1:1–3 — Syntax / sentence division {#genesis-beginning}

- **Original-language phrase:** בְּרֵאשִׁית
- **Difference:** Traditional “In the beginning God created…” contrasts with “When God began to create…”
- **Why/how:** The debate concerns how to construe bĕrēʾšît, the opening clause, and its relationship to verses 2–3. Rashi and modern scholars have argued for the dependent-clause translation.
- **What it means:** Neither translation alone proves previous human populations or earlier creations. A 2025 peer-reviewed study argues that the missing article does not rule out “in the beginning.”
- **Evidence status:** Genuine grammatical debate
- **Source trail:** [Benjamin D. Sommer (JTS)](https://www.jtsa.edu/torah/an-anthology-of-beginnings/); [Benjamin Kantor, JBL 144 (2025)](https://doi.org/10.15699/jbl.1444.2025.2).

### 1 John 5:7–8 — Manuscript variation {#johannine-comma}

- **Original-language phrase:** ὁ πατήρ, ὁ λόγος, καὶ τὸ ἅγιον πνεῦμα (later expanded reading)
- **Difference:** The KJV contains the explicit heavenly-witnesses clause naming Father, Word, and Holy Spirit; modern critical editions exclude it from their base text.
- **Why/how:** This is a text-critical issue: the clause is absent from early Greek witnesses and is principally documented in later Latin and some very late Greek witnesses.
- **What it means:** Calling this merely a modern “mistranslation” conceals the earlier manuscript history. The variant affects whether this passage explicitly states a Trinitarian formula; it does not alone determine the theology of the Trinity.
- **Evidence status:** Strong manuscript evidence; development history studied
- **Source trail:** [Galiza and Reeve, Andrews University Seminary Studies (2018)](https://digitalcommons.andrews.edu/auss/vol56/iss1/6/); [English parallel verses](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20John%205%3A7-8&version=KJV%3BNRSVUE).

### Genesis 37:35 / Psalm 9:17 / Numbers 16:33 — Vocabulary and semantic range {#sheol-hell}

- **Original-language phrase:** שְׁאוֹל
- **Difference:** The KJV variously translates sheʾol as “grave,” “hell,” and “pit.” The previous Translations document wrongly claimed it used “hell” throughout.
- **Why/how:** Translators chose English equivalents for an ancient term associated with the realm of the dead; modern readings of “hell” can import later associations not contained in every Hebrew use.
- **What it means:** Compare the term across several contexts before concluding any one verse describes eternal fiery punishment.
- **Evidence status:** Verified factual correction
- **Source trail:** [KJV Sheol usage concordance](https://www.kingjamesbibledictionary.com/StrongsNo/H7585/grave/0); [Genesis 37:35](https://biblehub.com/text/genesis/37-35.htm); [Psalm 9:17](https://biblehub.com/text/psalms/9-17.htm).



## Young woman, virgin, and young man across biblical contexts

**CHECKED 2026-10-20 · Representative biblical passages, not a complete concordance.** A socially recognized *young woman* did not correspond to a universal numeric age or an anatomical virginity test. Ancient Israel, Hellenistic/Greek societies, and Roman-period Christian communities must be distinguished. Marriage, betrothal, sexual conduct, and family authority could shape the social category, but each verse's wording and genre establish what is actually being claimed.

| Passage | Language and word | Who | What the description does |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Genesis 24:16, 43](https://biblehub.com/text/genesis/24-16.htm) | Hebrew: נַעֲרָה / בְּתוּלָה / עַלְמָה (*na‘arah / betulah / ‘almah*) | Rebekah | The narrator calls her a young woman and betulah, then explicitly says no man has known her; later she is called almah. The information about sex comes from the whole description, not almah alone. |
| [Exodus 2:8](https://biblehub.com/text/exodus/2-8.htm) | Hebrew: עַלְמָה (*‘almah*) | Moses' sister | She acts as an older sibling assisting the infant Moses. Her sexual history is not part of this episode. |
| [Isaiah 7:14–16](https://biblehub.com/text/isaiah/7-14.htm) | Hebrew: הָעַלְמָה (*ha-‘almah*) | The woman in the Immanuel sign | The sign operates within Isaiah's account of the eighth-century BCE political crisis. The Hebrew noun does not independently establish a miraculous conception. |
| [Isaiah 62:5](https://biblehub.com/text/isaiah/62-5.htm) | Hebrew: בָּחוּר / בְּתוּלָה (*bachur / betulah*) | Young man and maiden in a marriage image | The male is identified by youth; the female by betulah, a term associated with virginity. The verse is a poetic metaphor for Zion, not a universal lexical rule. |
| [Deuteronomy 22:13–21](https://www.thetorah.com/article/deuteronomys-uncompromising-demand-for-womens-sexual-fidelity) | Hebrew: בְּתוּלִים (*betulim*) | A bride whose sexual status is disputed | A legal case addresses accusations about a bride's prior sexual experience with potentially severe sanctions. No matching routine test of a groom's virginity appears in this law. |
| [Luke 1:27; 1:34](https://biblehub.com/text/luke/1-27.htm) | Greek: παρθένος / ἐμνηστευμένην (*parthenos / emnēsteumenēn*) | Mary | Luke explicitly describes betrothal and Mary's claim not to have sexual relations with a man. The narrative, not a dictionary gloss alone, supplies the sexual-virginity claim. |
| [Matthew 19:20](https://biblehub.com/text/matthew/19-20.htm) | Greek: νεανίσκος (*neaniskos*) | A young male interlocutor | The word identifies youth; the scene concerns wealth, commandments, and discipleship, not his sexual experience. |
| [1 Corinthians 7:34](https://biblehub.com/text/1_corinthians/7-34.htm) | Greek: ἄγαμος / παρθένος (*agamos / parthenos*) | Unmarried women | Paul distinguishes or juxtaposes unmarried and virgin women, depending on disputed punctuation and syntax; marriage and devotion are the chapter's concerns. |
| [Revelation 14:4](https://biblehub.com/text/revelation/14-4.htm) | Greek: παρθένοι (*parthenoi*) | A group of male followers of the Lamb | The Greek applies parthenoi to men and links purity to avoiding sexual 'defilement' with women. The apocalyptic symbolism does not establish a universal everyday rule. |

### Direct contrasts between female and male labels

- In **Isaiah 62:5**, *bachur* describes the young man; *betulah* describes the bride/maiden. The difference in lexical focus is visible, although the verse is a metaphor, not a demographic survey.
- In **Genesis 24:16 and 43**, *almah* names Rebekah as a young woman; *betulah* plus the explicit clause **“no man had known her”** establishes the narrator's claim of sexual inexperience. Do not derive all three statements from *almah* alone.
- In **Matthew 19:20**, *neaniskos* names a young man without an accompanying inquiry into his sexual history. By contrast, **Deuteronomy 22:13–21** explicitly regulates accusations about a bride's virginity. This is a selected legal-literary comparison, not a survey of all biblical requirements for male sexual conduct.
- **Revelation 14:4** applies **παρθένοι** (*parthenoi*, “virgins”) to a group depicted as male. This is an unusual usage embedded in a disputed apocalyptic image of 144,000 followers, not a general virginity requirement imposed on men. It also describes women as a potential source of defilement. Scholars read the imagery as literal celibacy, a symbolic pure community, or battle/angelic purity (e.g., Ruben Zimmermann, “Nuptial Imagery in the Revelation of John,” *Biblica* 84 [2003]; Clarissa Breu, “Female Seed as a Metaphor,” *JSNT* [2022]). [Zimmermann](https://www.bsw.org/index-html/vol-84-2003/nuptial-imagery-in-the-revelation-of-john/197/article-p157.html) · [Breu](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0142064X221110685).
- **Earlier male abstinence and celibacy references exist:** [1 Samuel 21:4–5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Samuel+21%3A4-5&version=NRSVUE) describes temporary abstinence before eating sacred bread; [Matthew 19:12](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+19%3A12&version=NRSVUE) depicts forgoing marriage for the kingdom; [1 Corinthians 7:7–9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Corinthians+7%3A7-9&version=NRSVUE) commends singleness for those who can accept it. None is directly equivalent to the bride-virginity accusation and sanction in Deuteronomy 22:13–21. A word being used for men once does **not** establish symmetrical sexual regulation across biblical texts.


### Historical frame versus modern English

- **Hebrew biblical narratives and prophetic texts** (preserving settings and compositions from different periods): *almah* often marks a young woman in a social life stage; no single modern age bracket or universal virginity conclusion follows. Isaiah 7:14 belongs to a narrative set in the **eighth century BCE**, whereas the text's literary growth and final compilation need separate historical analysis.
- **Ancient Greek usage and the Septuagint**: *parthenos* could mark a girl/maiden or sexual virginity. In some Greek societies being a maiden was defined in relation to marriage and transition from her father's household, but **Classical Athens is not interchangeable with ancient Judah**.
- **First-century Christian texts**: Luke's Mary is explicitly betrothed and presented as sexually inexperienced by the larger story. Paul distinguishes married and unmarried life; Revelation applies *parthenoi* to men in symbolic imagery.
- **Modern English**: “young woman” usually communicates approximate age and gender, whereas “virgin” commonly asserts sexual inexperience. Unmarried status and sexual experience are separate facts.

**Interpretive boundary:** A word meaning “young woman” does not prove she was sexually experienced; a word commonly glossed “virgin” does not by itself specify every social rule for women. These texts document gendered vocabulary and particular asymmetries. A universal claim about every ancient woman or man requires a different evidence base.

## Interpretation limits

**⟨DOCUMENTED⟩** The Greek or Hebrew wording and comparison of editions are checkable. The publisher's own account establishes its *stated reason* for a committee revision, not a hidden motive. The UBS Handbook explains why a translation may explicitly add an implied interlocutor in 2 Corinthians 12:16.

**⟨INFERENCE⟩** Differences between gendered power readings, perceived Pauline honesty, and doctrinal claims can be analyzed as *effects* on a reader. Effects alone do not prove deliberate deception by translators. Nor does a modern translation reliably preserve the source's ambiguity by merely being more literal.

**Distinct source histories:** Romans 3:7 and 2 Corinthians 12:16 involve speaker/argument interpretation; Genesis 3:16 a documented translation revision; Isaiah 7:14 a contested lexical/reception history; Genesis 1:1 a Hebrew syntax dispute; 1 John 5:7–8 a manuscript issue; and Sheol lexical range. Do not collapse these into a single category called “removed from the Bible.”

**Open expansion criteria:** add more cases only when source wording, two actual English renderings, the mechanism, a credible explanation, the reader consequence, and uncertainty are documented. Avoid unsourced count claims about *most* or *all* translations.
