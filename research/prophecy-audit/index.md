# Biblical Prophecy — Evidence Audit

**Checked:** October 20, 2026 · **Status:** 17 researched cases: 11 source-reviewed in Batch 1 and 6 new Matthew-formula studies awaiting their internal control pass. External specialist review remains on hold until the defined collection is complete. This is not a count of all biblical prophecies.

The Bible's prophetic literature includes warnings, oracles about current political crises, predictions, poetic laments, promises of restoration, and retrospective historical narratives. A later author calling an earlier passage **fulfilled** establishes that the later author made that interpretation. It does not by itself establish that the earlier author predicted the later event.

This module complements [Translations](../../Translations.md), [Historical Framework](../../Historical_Framework.md), and the [primary research corpus](../../README.md) without duplicating or modifying their earlier audits. Its structured source-of-record is [records.json](records.json). [The claim inventory](inventory.json) separately records which quotations have been researched and which are candidate leads.

## Research method

For each **claimed prophecy or fulfillment**, record these six layers separately:

1. **Earlier original words:** Hebrew, Aramaic or Greek; textual variants; whether the claim requires a particular translation.
2. **Original setting and genre:** audience, political and social circumstances, original apparent referent, and whether the passage is a warning, forecast, lament, or retrospective statement. Date of events and date of textual composition are separate questions.
3. **Later reuse or claimed fulfillment:** where it is quoted, and whether quotation, literary typology, selective allusion, or a new theological reading is taking place.
4. **Historical evidence:** independently dated records, archaeology, external testimony, and gaps. A later believer's own narrative is evidence of belief and narrative construction, not independent confirmation of the event it narrates.
5. **Competing interpretations:** identify historically grounded Jewish, Christian, and historical-critical readings; distinguish debates with evidence from guesses.
6. **Findings:** report *original text*, *later interpretation*, and *historical corroboration* distinctly. A universal pass/fail score conflates incompatible questions.

An important biblical contrast: [Deuteronomy 18:22](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy+18%3A22&version=NRSVUE) criticizes an unfulfilled pronouncement, whereas [Jeremiah 18:7–10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Jeremiah+18%3A7-10&version=NRSVUE) states that threats may change if behavior changes. Jonah 3 illustrates conditional warning.

## Passage audits

### Isaiah 7:10–17 — The young woman and Immanuel {#isaiah-7-14}

- **Original language:** הָעַלְמָה (ha-‘almah; the young woman)
- **Earlier situation:** Isaiah addresses King Ahaz during a military crisis in Judah; the child functions as a sign within a near-term political timetable (see 7:15–16).
- **Later claim / event:** Matthew quotes the Greek-scripture form using parthenos and applies it to Mary's conception of Jesus.
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** Retrospective messianic rereading, with a Hebrew-to-Greek lexical change.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** The connection is explicit in Matthew. The Hebrew noun alone does not require a miraculous conception; the supernatural claim is part of Matthew's narrative and not independently established.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Original-context reading strong; historical miracle unverified
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [Hebrew Isaiah 7:14](https://biblehub.com/text/isaiah/7-14.htm); [Greek Matthew 1:23](https://biblehub.com/text/matthew/1-23.htm); [TheTorah: Jewish origins of Christmas story](https://www.thetorah.com/article/the-jewish-origins-of-the-christmas-story); [Yale Bible Study: Matthew ancestry and birth](https://yalebiblestudy.org/courses/the-gospel-of-matthew/lessons/ancestry-and-birth-study-guide/).

### Hosea 11:1–4 — Out of Egypt I called my son {#hosea-11-1}

- **Original language:** כִּי נַעַר יִשְׂרָאֵל (when Israel was a boy)
- **Earlier situation:** Hosea recalls the historical Exodus story and addresses Israel as God's wayward child; its verbs point back to a past relationship.
- **Later claim / event:** Matthew cites Hosea 11:1 immediately after narrating the family's flight into Egypt (Matthew 2:14–15), although their return from Egypt is narrated later (2:19–21).
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** Matthew identifies Jesus with Israel by typology. Seong-Kwang Kevin Kim (2019) notes that the quotation about coming out is placed before Matthew narrates the return and offers a larger Israel/Egypt pattern to explain the application.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** Hosea describes Israel's past Exodus. Matthew's quotation and retelling are explicit but do not independently confirm the family’s travel. The timing of the quotation within Matthew is a real interpretive issue.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Earlier reference to Israel explicit; later typological use explicit
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [Hebrew Hosea 11:1](https://biblehub.com/text/hosea/11-1.htm); [Matthew 2:15](https://biblehub.com/text/matthew/2-15.htm); [TheTorah: Hosea 11 and Israel as a child](https://www.thetorah.com/article/israel-is-a-rebellious-child-yhwh-is-a-parent-but-not-a-person); [Yale Bible Study: Matthew and new Exodus](https://yalebiblestudy.org/courses/the-gospel-of-matthew/lessons/introduction-to-the-course-12/); [Seong-Kwang Kevin Kim (2019), The Use of Hosea 11:1 in Matthew 2:15](https://journal.kci.go.kr/ksots/archive/articleView?artiId=ART002505074).

### Jeremiah 31:15–17 — Rachel weeping for her children {#jeremiah-31-15}

- **Original language:** רָחֵל מְבַכָּה (Rachel is weeping)
- **Earlier situation:** Rachel personifies grief for Israelite exile; the very next lines promise that the children will return from enemy territory.
- **Later claim / event:** Matthew quotes the lament when narrating Herod's killing of Bethlehem's boys.
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** A poetic lament in an exile-restoration context is given a new narrative referent.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** The reuse is explicit and the original exile context can be verified. Matthew's particular massacre narrative is not independently established by this scriptural quotation.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Different historical referents documented; event evidence limited
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [Hebrew Jeremiah 31:15](https://biblehub.com/text/jeremiah/31-15.htm); [Matthew 2:17–18](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+2%3A17-18&version=NRSVUE); [TheTorah: Jewish origins of Christmas story](https://www.thetorah.com/article/the-jewish-origins-of-the-christmas-story); [TheTorah: Rachel weeps in Ramah](https://www.thetorah.com/article/rachel-weeps-in-ramah-of-all-the-patriarchs-god-listens-only-to-her).

### Micah 5:2–5 (English numbering) — A ruler from Bethlehem {#micah-5-2}

- **Original language:** בֵּית־לֶחֶם אֶפְרָתָה (Bethlehem Ephrathah)
- **Earlier situation:** Micah looks toward a ruler emerging from David's ancestral setting amid political insecurity, with hopes of peace and protection for Israel.
- **Later claim / event:** Matthew explicitly quotes Micah as identifying the Messiah's Bethlehem connection; Luke also places the birth in Bethlehem but supplies a different journey narrative and does not use the same fulfillment formula.
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** The earlier passage is a genuine future-oriented royal oracle, later understood christologically.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** Micah contains a forward-looking Davidic royal oracle. The Bethlehem association may refer to Davidic origins as well as a locality; the two later nativity accounts do not supply independent contemporary corroboration of Jesus' precise birthplace.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Earlier royal oracle clear; Bethlehem birth details unverified independently
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [Hebrew Micah 5:2](https://biblehub.com/text/micah/5-2.htm); [Matthew 2:5–6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+2%3A5-6&version=NRSVUE); [TheTorah: why Bethlehem](https://www.thetorah.com/article/the-jewish-origins-of-the-christmas-story); [Yale Matthew study guide](https://yalebiblestudy.org/courses/the-gospel-of-matthew/lessons/ancestry-and-birth-study-guide/).

### Zechariah 9:9–10 — The king riding a donkey {#zechariah-9-9}

- **Original language:** עַל־חֲמוֹר וְעַל־עַיִר (on a donkey, and on a colt)
- **Earlier situation:** Zion is told to welcome a humble, victorious king, contrasted with military power and associated with the end of war in the next verse.
- **Later claim / event:** Mark and John describe one colt/donkey; Matthew names both a mother donkey and colt while citing Zechariah. What the pronoun 'them' refers to in Matthew 21:7 is also debated.
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** There are competing explanations: Matthew may have read Zechariah's poetic parallelism as two animals (Yale's account); Tucker Ferda (2020) argues Matthew combined Zechariah 9:9 with Genesis 49:10–11; Adam Kubiś (2018) defends a historically two-animal scene. Zechariah's Hebrew parallel phrases need not refer to two mounts.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** Matthew's two-animal version is a textual fact. Its relationship to the one-animal accounts and the event itself is debated, so the Gospel quotation alone does not independently establish the exact incident.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Textual reuse explicit; exact event details not independently established
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [Hebrew Zechariah 9:9](https://biblehub.com/text/zechariah/9-9.htm); [Matthew 21:4–7](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+21%3A4-7&version=NRSVUE); [Yale on Matthew 21](https://yalebiblestudy.org/courses/the-gospel-of-matthew/lessons/stories-of-the-end-study-guide/); [Tucker S. Ferda (2020), Journal of Theological Studies](https://doi.org/10.1093/jts/flaa088); [Adam Kubiś (2018), Biblical Annals](https://doi.org/10.31743/ba.2018.8.2.04).

### Isaiah 52:13–53:12 — The suffering servant {#isaiah-53}

- **Original language:** עַבְדִּי (my servant; Isaiah 52:13)
- **Earlier situation:** The servant poem belongs to Isaiah 40–55's exile-and-restoration setting. Isaiah 49:3 explicitly calls the servant Israel, but Isaiah 49:5 also describes a servant commissioned to restore Israel. This complicates a simple identification; views include collective Israel, an individual representative, or an idealized servant.
- **Later claim / event:** New Testament texts apply servant phrases to Jesus' healings, suffering, death, and perceived redemptive role.
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** Later interpretation identifies the servant with Jesus; interpretations of the original figure include collective Israel and individual or idealized servants.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** New Testament identification of the servant with Jesus is explicit. Even if Jesus' execution is accepted historically, it does not independently prove that the poem originally referred to him or that its theological portrayal of suffering was an advance prediction.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Servant identity debated; New Testament application documented
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [Hebrew Isaiah 52:13](https://biblehub.com/text/isaiah/52-13.htm); [Hebrew Isaiah 49:3](https://biblehub.com/text/isaiah/49-3.htm); [Acts 8:32–35](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts+8%3A32-35&version=NRSVUE); [Yale: Second Isaiah suffering servant](https://yalebiblestudy.org/courses/second-isaiah/lessons/arm-of-god-study-guide/); [TheTorah: suffering servant interpretive reception](https://www.thetorah.com/article/the-skin-of-the-metzora-and-the-heart-of-the-messiah); [Hebrew Isaiah 49:5, servant commissioned to restore Israel](https://biblehub.com/text/isaiah/49-5.htm).

### Psalm 22 — My God, why have you forsaken me? {#psalm-22}

- **Original language:** כָּאֲרִי (Masoretic 'like a lion') / כארו (Naḥal Ḥever variant): Hebrew Psalm 22:17, often 22:16 in English Bibles
- **Earlier situation:** Psalm 22 is an anguished prayer of a suffering speaker who ultimately voices praise, not a prose forecast naming a future crucifixion.
- **Later claim / event:** Jesus' cry and the distribution of garments are narrated with language that evokes Psalm 22; Christians also connect the debated hands-and-feet reading to crucifixion.
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** The evangelists echo an earlier lament, including its opening cry and imagery. The Masoretic text has 'like a lion'; a Naḥal Ḥever fragment supports a spelling with final waw; the Greek ὤρυξαν is 'they dug'. Translating the verbal tradition as 'pierced' is interpretive and disputed.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** Hopkin (2005) argues that the ancient fragment supports the verbal reading; Strawn (2000) stresses the lion imagery and textual difficulties; NET translation notes challenge 'pierce' as a gloss. Competing textual readings do not by themselves show that Psalm 22 originally predicted Jesus' crucifixion.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Ancient reading variation documented; prophetic status is interpretive
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [Masoretic Hebrew Psalm 22:17](https://biblehub.com/text/psalms/22-17.htm); [Shon D. Hopkin (2005), BYU Studies](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/byusq/vol44/iss3/9/); [Brent A. Strawn (2000), Journal of Biblical Literature](https://doi.org/10.2307/3268408); [NET Bible notes, Psalm 22](https://classic.net.bible.org/passage.php?passage=Psa+22%3A16-17); [Matthew 27:46](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+27%3A46&version=NRSVUE); [John 19:24](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+19%3A24&version=NRSVUE).

### Isaiah 40:3–5 — The voice in the wilderness {#isaiah-40-3}

- **Original language:** קֹול קֹורֵא בַּמִּדְבָּר (a voice calls: in the wilderness...)
- **Earlier situation:** Second Isaiah (Isaiah 40–55) is commonly situated in the sixth-century BCE exilic context. Hebrew poetry pairs 'in the wilderness' with 'in the desert'; the voice calls for a processional road prepared for YHWH's return to the people.
- **Later claim / event:** Gospel authors use the Greek textual tradition so that the voice belongs to John the Baptist in the wilderness.
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** The Septuagint does not reproduce the Hebrew 'in the desert' parallel phrase in the second line, enabling 'in the wilderness' to describe the crying voice. The Gospels apply that Greek reading to John the Baptist. The shift involves the Greek version's wording as well as punctuation.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** Hebrew parallelism and Greek translation structure support distinct construals; the New Testament application is explicit. The earlier text does not name John the Baptist.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Literary and translation shift documented
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [Hebrew Isaiah 40:3](https://biblehub.com/text/isaiah/40-3.htm); [Greek Mark 1:3](https://biblehub.com/text/mark/1-3.htm); [David M. Freidenreich, TheTorah (2026): Hebrew and Greek compared](https://www.thetorah.com/article/a-voice-cries-out-in-the-wilderness-isaiah-translated-and-improved).

### Jeremiah 46:13–26 — Babylon's predicted conquest of Egypt {#jeremiah-46-egypt}

- **Original language:** לָבוֹא נְבוּכַדְרֶאצַּר ... לְהַכּוֹת אֶת־אֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם (Nebuchadnezzar coming to strike Egypt)
- **Earlier situation:** The oracle imagines a destructive Babylonian invasion and devastation of Egypt after Babylon's military rise.
- **Later claim / event:** Later interpreters may regard partial raids as fulfillment or understand the oracle as revised in light of events.
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** A concrete military forecast, testable with Egyptian and Babylonian evidence and the book's editorial history.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** Babylon fought Egypt and conducted raids; Egyptian independence continued until Persian conquest. Dan'el Kahn argues parts of the present prophetic text were revised with later Egyptian civil-war knowledge.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Predicted full conquest not documented; partial attacks documented; textual layers debated
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [Hebrew Jeremiah 46:13](https://biblehub.com/text/jeremiah/46-13.htm); [Dan'el Kahn (2022), TheTorah](https://www.thetorah.com/article/nebuchadnezzar-fails-to-conquer-egypt-so-jeremiahs-prophecy-was-updated).

### Ezekiel 29:8–16; 29:17–21 — Egypt uninhabited for forty years {#ezekiel-29-egypt}

- **Original language:** אַרְבָּעִים שָׁנָה (forty years)
- **Earlier situation:** The prophecy predicts devastation, dispersion, and lack of inhabitants for forty years, associated with Egyptian and Babylonian power.
- **Later claim / event:** The forty-year desolation can be read as a literal timetable or as a rounded/rhetorical period. Ezekiel 29:17–21 offers Egypt as compensation for Nebuchadnezzar's inadequate reward from his campaign against Tyre; historical conquests require outside evidence.
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** Specific forecast compared to independent historical records, with attention to whether numbers are literal or rhetorical.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** No independently verified Egyptian history supports forty years of the entire country being uninhabited. Babylonian attacks occurred, but the available record does not establish complete depopulation on the prophesied scale. Interpretive debate remains over the imagery and intended literalness.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Literal fulfillment unsupported by known independent record
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [Hebrew Ezekiel 29:11](https://biblehub.com/text/ezekiel/29-11.htm); [Ezekiel 29:17–21](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezekiel+29%3A17-21&version=NRSVUE); [Dan'el Kahn (2022), Egyptian and Babylonian evidence](https://www.thetorah.com/article/nebuchadnezzar-fails-to-conquer-egypt-so-jeremiahs-prophecy-was-updated).

### Jonah 3:4–10; Jeremiah 18:7–10 — Forty days until Nineveh's overthrow {#jonah-3-warning}

- **Original language:** עֹוד אַרְבָּעִים יֹום (yet forty days)
- **Earlier situation:** The story has Jonah announce impending destruction; the city's response leads the narrator to say God does not bring the announced disaster.
- **Later claim / event:** Sometimes claimed as failed prediction or defended as a conditional warning.
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** A teaching story about judgment and mercy, paired with Jeremiah's explicit principle that warnings may be conditional.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** The narrative itself says the announced disaster did not occur. It gives a reason within the story; this does not independently establish that Jonah preached to historical Nineveh or that its repentance happened.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Non-occurrence and conditional logic explicit within the narrative
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [Hebrew Jonah 3:4](https://biblehub.com/text/jonah/3-4.htm); [Jonah 3:10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Jonah+3%3A10&version=NRSVUE); [Jeremiah 18:7–10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Jeremiah+18%3A7-10&version=NRSVUE).


### No surviving identifiable scriptural quotation — He will be called a Nazarene {#nazorean-matthew-2-23}

- **Original language:** Ναζωραῖος κληθήσεται (Greek Matthew 2:23, 'he will be called a Nazarene'); no corresponding Hebrew prophecy text is known
- **Earlier situation:** Matthew refers to what 'the prophets' said in the plural, but no extant verse in the Hebrew Bible makes this exact statement. Proposed connections include the Hebrew 'branch' (netser) in Isaiah 11:1, the Nazirite tradition, or a broader motif of social contempt; each has lexical or contextual obstacles.
- **Later claim / event:** Matthew says Joseph's family settled in Nazareth so that the words spoken through the prophets might be fulfilled.
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** A fulfillment claim without a securely identifiable prior passage. An association with an older scriptural theme is plausible, but reconstructing an exact lost quotation or the author's motive exceeds the evidence.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** Matthew 2:23 is verifiable. The lack of an extant matching oracle prevents word-for-word comparison or independent confirmation that 'Nazarene' was predicted; proposed Hebrew wordplays remain hypotheses.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Fulfillment claim explicit; original quoted source unidentified
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [Matthew 2:23 with NET translation notes](https://classic.net.bible.org/verse.php?book=Mat&chapter=2&verse=23); [Matthew 2:23 Greek text](https://biblehub.com/text/matthew/2-23.htm); [Isaiah 11:1 (netser) comparison](https://biblehub.com/text/isaiah/11-1.htm).

### Isaiah 8:23–9:1 (Hebrew numbering; English Isaiah 9:1–2) — Great light in Galilee {#isaiah-9-galilee-matthew-4}

- **Original language:** הָעָם הַהֹלְכִים בַּחֹשֶׁךְ רָאוּ אוֹר גָּדוֹל ('people walking in darkness saw a great light')
- **Earlier situation:** The Isaiah passage names the territories of Zebulun and Naphtali in northern Israel after devastation associated with imperial warfare in the eighth century BCE; its original horizon is hope after crisis. Its precise literary layering and royal expectation are debated.
- **Later claim / event:** Matthew quotes the passage after Jesus relocates to Capernaum and begins his ministry in Galilee, identifying his activity with the light reaching the north.
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** The application is explicit; Matthew adapts the geographical and metaphorical imagery to Jesus. The earlier oracle does not name Jesus or specify a first-century Capernaum ministry.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** The Hebrew text, its historical north-Israel setting, and the textual correspondence with Matthew are checkable. Matthew's report of the ministry cannot be treated as independent proof of supernatural fulfillment simply because Matthew supplies the citation.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Earlier northern-restoration imagery and later application documented; supernatural forecast unverified
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [Isaiah 9:1–2 and Matthew 4:14–16](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah+9%3A1-2%2CMatthew+4%3A14-16&version=NIV); [Hebrew Isaiah 9:2](https://biblehub.com/text/isaiah/9-2.htm); [Richard Beaton, Isaiah's Christ in Matthew's Gospel (2002), research program](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/18889/sample/9780521818889ws.pdf).

### Isaiah 53:4 — He took our infirmities {#isaiah-53-4-healing}

- **Original language:** חֳלָיֵנוּ הוּא נָשָׂא וּמַכְאֹבֵינוּ סְבָלָם ('our sicknesses he bore, our pains he carried')
- **Earlier situation:** Isaiah's fourth servant poem depicts a sufferer bearing illness and pain, interpreted within the larger exile-restoration discourse. The ancient Greek version renders a related clause using 'sins', making the textual tradition crucial.
- **Later claim / event:** Matthew quotes Isaiah 53:4 after reporting Jesus' healings and exorcisms, drawing attention to physical sickness in the wording; this is a narrower use than later applications to Jesus' death.
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** A particular use of Isaiah 53 distinct from the broader servant-identity case already in this audit (isaiah-53). Matthew's phrasing is closer to the Hebrew's physical-illness sense than the old Greek version's 'sins'. Rikk Watts (2015) connects it to Matthew's wider theme of ending Israel's afflictions.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** The verbal source and Hebrew–Septuagint difference are independently inspectable. Matthew's literary explanation for healings does not independently establish that the reported miracles occurred, nor prove an original forecast of Jesus.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Explicit citation and identifiable translation choice; specific theological significance debated
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [Isaiah 53:4 / Matthew 8:17 parallel](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+8%3A17%2CIsaiah+53%3A4&version=NIV); [Hebrew Isaiah 53:4](https://biblehub.com/text/isaiah/53-4.htm); [Greek Septuagint comparison](https://intertextual.bible/text/isaiah-53.4/lxx-isaiah-53.4); [Rikk Watts (2015), Journal for the Study of the New Testament](https://doi.org/10.1177/0142064X15595940).

### Isaiah 42:1–4 — The quiet servant bringing justice {#isaiah-42-servant-matthew-12}

- **Original language:** הֵן עַבְדִּי ... מִשְׁפָּט לַגּוֹיִם יוֹצִיא ('Here is my servant ... he will bring justice to the nations')
- **Earlier situation:** Isaiah's servant is characterized as chosen and Spirit-supported, bringing justice without destructive force; the servant's identity across Isaiah 40–55 is debated, and the text belongs to an exile/restoration horizon.
- **Later claim / event:** Matthew quotes a lengthy adapted version to interpret Jesus' healing and instruction, including his restraint and the prospect of justice or hope among the nations.
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** Explicit later identification with Jesus. The Greek wording used in Matthew differs in places from both the standard Hebrew and the old Greek Isaiah text; it is an interpretive quotation rather than an exact unedited reproduction.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** The passage's servant/justice language exists earlier, and Matthew's theological application is documented. Richard Beaton (2000) argues that the servant's justice is integral to Matthew's compassionate portrayal of Jesus; the original identity and fulfillment standard are debatable.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Text-to-text use direct; earlier servant identity and fulfillment interpretation disputed
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [Hebrew Isaiah 42:1–4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah+42%3A1-4&version=NRSVUE); [Matthew 12:17–21 and Isaiah 42:1–4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+12%3A17-21%2CIsaiah+42%3A1-4&version=NIV); [Richard Beaton (2000), Matthew's use of Isaiah 42](https://doi.org/10.1177/0142064X0002207502); [Richard Beaton, Isaiah's Christ in Matthew's Gospel (2002)](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/18889/sample/9780521818889ws.pdf).

### Psalm 78:1–8 (especially 78:2) — I will open my mouth in parables {#psalm-78-parables-matthew-13}

- **Original language:** אֶפְתְּחָה בְמָשָׁל פִּי ('I will open my mouth with a parable')
- **Earlier situation:** Psalm 78, associated by its superscription with Asaph, introduces a poetic instruction that retells Israel's past and teaches coming generations. In its own context it describes the psalmist's mode of teaching rather than a named future person's preaching.
- **Later claim / event:** Matthew cites the line to explain Jesus' choice to teach in parables. Matthew's longer wording mentions what was hidden since the foundation of the world, whereas Hebrew Psalm 78:2 speaks about things 'from of old'.
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** A later literary application of ancient wisdom and teaching language, not an unambiguous predictive oracle. Some Greek manuscripts name Isaiah as the 'prophet' in Matthew 13:35; most do not. The source remains Psalm 78:2.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** The older psalm and Matthew's citation are directly comparable; the choice of literary quotation does not independently prove historicity of every speech of Jesus. NET and NABRE textual notes document the rare Matthew 'Isaiah' variant.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Earlier retrospective instruction and later reuse established; minor NT textual variant noted
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [Psalm 78:2 / Matthew 13:35 parallel](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm+78%3A2%2CMatthew+13%3A35&version=NABRE); [Hebrew Psalm 78:2](https://biblehub.com/text/psalms/78-2.htm); [NET textual note Matthew 13:35](https://classic.net.bible.org/passage.php?passage=Mat+13%3A35); [Yale Bible Study on Matthew's parables](https://yalebiblestudy.org/courses/the-gospel-of-matthew/lessons/controversies-and-parables-study-guide/).

### Zechariah 11:12–13 (and debated allusions to Jeremiah 18–19; 32) — Thirty silver pieces attributed to Jeremiah {#zechariah-11-silver-matthew-27}

- **Original language:** שְׁלֹשִׁים כָּסֶף ('thirty [pieces of] silver', Zechariah 11:12); Matthew says 'through Jeremiah'
- **Earlier situation:** Zechariah 11 presents a symbolic shepherd drama about the rejection of a shepherd and the contemptuous thirty-silver payment. It mentions disposing of the money in the house of YHWH, but not the purchase of a potter's field.
- **Later claim / event:** Matthew's Judas narrative specifies thirty silver pieces, money thrown into the temple, and the purchase of a potter's field; Matthew 27:9 calls the quotation Jeremiah although its closest wording is Zechariah 11:12–13.
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** The discrepancy is explicit. Some scholars posit composite imagery from Jeremiah 19 or 32; Maarten J. J. Menken (2002) argues Zechariah 11:13 is the main source and Matthew's redaction explains the altered wording and selected narrative details.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** The source attribution in Matthew is demonstrably different from the best matching earlier text. A correction based solely on Matthew's own claim that Judas acted this way would be circular; the earlier source independently exists, but the thirty-coin event is not independently verified by that correspondence.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Zechariah source and Jeremiah attribution verifiable; precise cause of misattribution debated
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [NRSVUE Zechariah 11:12–13 vs Matthew 27:9–10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Zechariah+11%3A12-13%3B+Matthew+27%3A9-10&version=NRSVUE); [Zechariah 11:12 Hebrew](https://biblehub.com/text/zechariah/11-12.htm); [Maarten J.J. Menken (2002), Biblica 83](https://www.bsw.org/biblica/vol-83-2002/the-old-testament-quotation-in-matthew-27-9-10-textual-form-and-context/238/article-p309.html); [Jeremiah 32:6–9 for comparison](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Jeremiah+32%3A6-9&version=NRSVUE).

## Open inventory and stopping rule

**Batch 1:** 11 starter records, already internally reviewed. **Batch 2:** the six additional dedicated studies covering all ten classic Matthew formula quotations, with four earlier cases linked. The new entries are researched but still need a source-control review. Neither batch means "every prophecy done."

Next nonduplicative batches, with each source passage + later claim as the canonical match key:

- **Matthew:** the ten formula quotations are now indexed and researched; next enumerate and review the five separately tracked non-formula candidates and remaining quotations/allusions.
- **Other New Testament authors:** Mark, Luke, John, Acts, Paul, Hebrews and Revelation; include quotations and explicitly labeled allusions.
- **Hebrew Bible predictions:** Assyria, Babylon, Egypt, Persian period, restoration and conflict oracles, tested against independent records.
- **Apocalyptic / end-time claims:** Daniel, Jesus' kingdom imminence sayings, Revelation and later receptions, tracking dating debates and genre.
- **Jewish and Christian messianic expectations:** catalog standards and interpretive differences without implying any single modern community speaks for all Judaism or Christianity.

There is no defensible single total for “all prophecies” until the inclusion rule is defined. One passage may be reused for multiple alleged fulfillments; one later author can quote several originals. Neither count is interchangeable with the number of predictive oracles.
