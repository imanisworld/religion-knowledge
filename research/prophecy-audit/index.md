# Biblical Prophecy — Evidence Audit

**Checked:** October 20, 2026 · **Status:** first audited batch, 11 cases. This is not a complete inventory or a count of all biblical prophecies.

The Bible's prophetic literature includes warnings, oracles about current political crises, predictions, poetic laments, promises of restoration, and retrospective historical narratives. A later author calling an earlier passage **fulfilled** establishes that the later author made that interpretation. It does not by itself establish that the earlier author predicted the later event.

This module complements [Translations](../../Translations.md), [Historical Framework](../../Historical_Framework.md), and the [primary research corpus](../../README.md) without duplicating or modifying their earlier audits. Its structured source-of-record is [records.json](records.json).

## Research method

For each **claimed prophecy or fulfillment**, record these six layers separately:

1. **Earlier original words:** Hebrew, Aramaic or Greek; textual variants; whether the claim requires a particular translation.
2. **Original setting and genre:** audience, political and social circumstances, original apparent referent, and whether the passage is a warning, forecast, lament, or retrospective statement. Date of events and date of textual composition are separate questions.
3. **Later reuse or claimed fulfillment:** where it is quoted, and whether quotation, literary typology, selective allusion, or a new theological reading is taking place.
4. **Historical evidence:** independently dated records, archaeology, external testimony, and gaps. A later believer's own narrative is evidence of belief and narrative construction, not independent confirmation of the event it narrates.
5. **Competing interpretations:** identify historically grounded Jewish, Christian, and historical-critical readings; distinguish debates with evidence from guesses.
6. **Findings:** report *original text*, *later interpretation*, and *historical corroboration* distinctly. A universal pass/fail score conflates incompatible questions.

An important biblical contrast: [Deuteronomy 18:22](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy+18%3A22&version=NRSVUE) criticizes an unfulfilled pronouncement, whereas [Jeremiah 18:7–10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Jeremiah+18%3A7-10&version=NRSVUE) states that threats may change if behavior changes. Jonah 3 illustrates conditional warning.

## Passage audits

### Isaiah 7:10–17 — The young woman and Immanuel {#isaiah-7-14}

- **Original language:** הָעַלְמָה (ha-‘almah; the young woman)
- **Earlier situation:** Isaiah addresses King Ahaz during a military crisis in Judah; the child functions as a sign within a near-term political timetable (see 7:15–16).
- **Later claim / event:** Matthew quotes the Greek-scripture form using parthenos and applies it to Mary's conception of Jesus.
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** Retrospective messianic rereading, with a Hebrew-to-Greek lexical change.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** The connection is explicit in Matthew. The Hebrew noun alone does not require a miraculous conception; the supernatural claim is part of Matthew's narrative and not independently established.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Original-context reading strong; historical miracle unverified
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [Hebrew Isaiah 7:14](https://biblehub.com/text/isaiah/7-14.htm); [Greek Matthew 1:23](https://biblehub.com/text/matthew/1-23.htm); [TheTorah: Jewish origins of Christmas story](https://www.thetorah.com/article/the-jewish-origins-of-the-christmas-story); [Yale Bible Study: Matthew ancestry and birth](https://yalebiblestudy.org/courses/the-gospel-of-matthew/lessons/ancestry-and-birth-study-guide/).

### Hosea 11:1–4 — Out of Egypt I called my son {#hosea-11-1}

- **Original language:** כִּי נַעַר יִשְׂרָאֵל (when Israel was a boy)
- **Earlier situation:** Hosea recalls the historical Exodus story and addresses Israel as God's wayward child; its verbs point back to a past relationship.
- **Later claim / event:** Matthew describes Jesus returning from Egypt and explicitly presents Hosea 11:1 as fulfilled.
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** Typology: Matthew portrays Jesus reenacting the history of Israel.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** Hosea 11:1 is retrospective in its own context, rather than a straightforward advance forecast of a future messiah. The Egypt trip is narrated in Matthew; this quotation alone cannot independently corroborate the journey.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Earlier reference to Israel explicit; later typological use explicit
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [Hebrew Hosea 11:1](https://biblehub.com/text/hosea/11-1.htm); [Matthew 2:15](https://biblehub.com/text/matthew/2-15.htm); [TheTorah: Hosea 11 and Israel as a child](https://www.thetorah.com/article/israel-is-a-rebellious-child-yhwh-is-a-parent-but-not-a-person); [Yale Bible Study: Matthew and new Exodus](https://yalebiblestudy.org/courses/the-gospel-of-matthew/lessons/introduction-to-the-course-12/).

### Jeremiah 31:15–17 — Rachel weeping for her children {#jeremiah-31-15}

- **Original language:** רָחֵל מְבַכָּה (Rachel is weeping)
- **Earlier situation:** Rachel personifies grief for Israelite exile; the very next lines promise that the children will return from enemy territory.
- **Later claim / event:** Matthew quotes the lament when narrating Herod's killing of Bethlehem's boys.
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** A poetic lament in an exile-restoration context is given a new narrative referent.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** The reuse is explicit and the original exile context can be verified. Matthew's particular massacre narrative is not independently established by this scriptural quotation.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Different historical referents documented; event evidence limited
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [Hebrew Jeremiah 31:15](https://biblehub.com/text/jeremiah/31-15.htm); [Matthew 2:17–18](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+2%3A17-18&version=NRSVUE); [TheTorah: Jewish origins of Christmas story](https://www.thetorah.com/article/the-jewish-origins-of-the-christmas-story); [TheTorah: Rachel weeps in Ramah](https://www.thetorah.com/article/rachel-weeps-in-ramah-of-all-the-patriarchs-god-listens-only-to-her).

### Micah 5:2–5 (English numbering) — A ruler from Bethlehem {#micah-5-2}

- **Original language:** בֵּית־לֶחֶם אֶפְרָתָה (Bethlehem Ephrathah)
- **Earlier situation:** Micah looks toward a ruler emerging from David's ancestral setting amid political insecurity, with hopes of peace and protection for Israel.
- **Later claim / event:** Matthew uses the passage to locate the Messiah's birthplace; Luke also sets Jesus' birth in Bethlehem without presenting the same formal quotation.
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** The earlier passage is a genuine future-oriented royal oracle, later understood christologically.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** An earlier written Bethlehem association is documented. A Bethlehem birth is asserted in two infancy narratives but not independently confirmed by them; the distinctive accounts require their own source criticism.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Earlier prediction explicit; historical birth detail not independently verified
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [Hebrew Micah 5:2](https://biblehub.com/text/micah/5-2.htm); [Matthew 2:5–6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+2%3A5-6&version=NRSVUE); [TheTorah: why Bethlehem](https://www.thetorah.com/article/the-jewish-origins-of-the-christmas-story); [Yale Matthew study guide](https://yalebiblestudy.org/courses/the-gospel-of-matthew/lessons/ancestry-and-birth-study-guide/).

### Zechariah 9:9–10 — The king riding a donkey {#zechariah-9-9}

- **Original language:** עַל־חֲמוֹר וְעַל־עַיִר (on a donkey, and on a colt)
- **Earlier situation:** Zion is told to welcome a humble, victorious king, contrasted with military power and associated with the end of war in the next verse.
- **Later claim / event:** The Gospels depict Jesus entering Jerusalem on a donkey. Matthew mentions both a donkey and its colt, reflecting the Hebrew poetry's parallel phrases.
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** Deliberate literary and symbolic enactment of a royal oracle is strongly supported within the Gospel narratives.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** The text-to-text association is observable; the details are related by texts invested in fulfillment. Hebrew parallelism need not predict two separate animals ridden at once.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Textual reuse explicit; exact event details not independently established
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [Hebrew Zechariah 9:9](https://biblehub.com/text/zechariah/9-9.htm); [Matthew 21:4–7](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+21%3A4-7&version=NRSVUE); [Yale: stories of the end](https://yalebiblestudy.org/courses/the-gospel-of-matthew/lessons/stories-of-the-end-study-guide/); [TheTorah: Hebrew donkey parallelism](https://www.thetorah.com/article/he-tethers-his-donkey-to-the-vine-judah-exported-soreqa-wine).

### Isaiah 52:13–53:12 — The suffering servant {#isaiah-53}

- **Original language:** עַבְדִּי (my servant; Isaiah 52:13)
- **Earlier situation:** An unnamed suffering servant bears others' suffering in a collection about Israel's exile and restoration. Isaiah 49:3 explicitly calls the servant 'Israel'; the identity across the songs is debated.
- **Later claim / event:** New Testament texts apply servant phrases to Jesus' healings, suffering, death, and perceived redemptive role.
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** Later interpretation identifies the servant with Jesus; interpretations of the original figure include collective Israel and individual or idealized servants.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** The borrowing is explicit. The fact of Jesus' execution, if established historically, does not by itself independently establish the poem's claim of vicarious atonement or a supernatural prediction.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Servant identity debated; New Testament application documented
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [Hebrew Isaiah 52:13](https://biblehub.com/text/isaiah/52-13.htm); [Hebrew Isaiah 49:3](https://biblehub.com/text/isaiah/49-3.htm); [Acts 8:32–35](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts+8%3A32-35&version=NRSVUE); [Yale: Second Isaiah suffering servant](https://yalebiblestudy.org/courses/second-isaiah/lessons/arm-of-god-study-guide/); [TheTorah: suffering servant interpretive reception](https://www.thetorah.com/article/the-skin-of-the-metzora-and-the-heart-of-the-messiah).

### Psalm 22 — My God, why have you forsaken me? {#psalm-22}

- **Original language:** כָּאֲרִי (like a lion; MT 22:17) / כארו (older variant)
- **Earlier situation:** Psalm 22 is an anguished prayer of a suffering speaker who ultimately voices praise, not a prose forecast naming a future crucifixion.
- **Later claim / event:** Jesus' cry and the distribution of garments are narrated with language that evokes Psalm 22; Christians also connect the debated hands-and-feet reading to crucifixion.
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** Literary reuse of a lament alongside a genuine manuscript variant; a quotation or echo does not by itself establish advance prediction.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** The Masoretic text has 'like a lion'; a Nahal Hever witness and the ancient Greek version support an alternate verbal reading commonly translated 'pierced/dug'. Neither variant alone identifies Jesus as the original subject.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Ancient reading variation documented; prophetic status is interpretive
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [Hebrew Psalm 22:17](https://biblehub.com/text/psalms/22-17.htm); [Greek and Hebrew variant research, BYU Studies](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/byusq/vol44/iss3/9/); [Matthew 27:46](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+27%3A46&version=NRSVUE); [John 19:24](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+19%3A24&version=NRSVUE).

### Isaiah 40:3–5 — The voice in the wilderness {#isaiah-40-3}

- **Original language:** קֹול קֹורֵא בַּמִּדְבָּר (a voice calls: in the wilderness...)
- **Earlier situation:** In Second Isaiah, a highway is prepared through the wilderness for God's return and the restoration of exiles.
- **Later claim / event:** Gospel authors use the Greek textual tradition so that the voice belongs to John the Baptist in the wilderness.
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** Quotation and syntactic reinterpretation; punctuation/word grouping substantially affects the image.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** Ancient Hebrew and Greek versions do not carry modern punctuation, but their syntactic groupings support different readings. This is a specific demonstrable interpretive shift; it is not evidence John was named by Isaiah.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Literary and translation shift documented
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [Hebrew Isaiah 40:3](https://biblehub.com/text/isaiah/40-3.htm); [Mark 1:3](https://biblehub.com/text/mark/1-3.htm); [TheTorah: Isaiah translated and improved (2026)](https://www.thetorah.com/article/a-voice-cries-out-in-the-wilderness-isaiah-translated-and-improved).

### Jeremiah 46:13–26 — Babylon's predicted conquest of Egypt {#jeremiah-46-egypt}

- **Original language:** לָבוֹא נְבוּכַדְרֶאצַּר ... לְהַכּוֹת אֶת־אֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם (Nebuchadnezzar coming to strike Egypt)
- **Earlier situation:** The oracle imagines a destructive Babylonian invasion and devastation of Egypt after Babylon's military rise.
- **Later claim / event:** Later interpreters may regard partial raids as fulfillment or understand the oracle as revised in light of events.
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** A concrete military forecast, testable with Egyptian and Babylonian evidence and the book's editorial history.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** Babylon fought Egypt and conducted raids; Egyptian independence continued until Persian conquest. Dan'el Kahn argues parts of the present prophetic text were revised with later Egyptian civil-war knowledge.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Predicted full conquest not documented; partial attacks documented; textual layers debated
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [Hebrew Jeremiah 46:13](https://biblehub.com/text/jeremiah/46-13.htm); [Dan'el Kahn (2022), TheTorah](https://www.thetorah.com/article/nebuchadnezzar-fails-to-conquer-egypt-so-jeremiahs-prophecy-was-updated).

### Ezekiel 29:8–16; 29:17–21 — Egypt uninhabited for forty years {#ezekiel-29-egypt}

- **Original language:** אַרְבָּעִים שָׁנָה (forty years)
- **Earlier situation:** The prophecy predicts devastation, dispersion, and lack of inhabitants for forty years, associated with Egyptian and Babylonian power.
- **Later claim / event:** Readers have proposed historical and symbolic interpretations of the forty-year figure; the following verses discuss Egypt as payment for Nebuchadnezzar's failed labor at Tyre.
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** Specific forecast compared to independent historical records, with attention to whether numbers are literal or rhetorical.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** No independent evidence establishes that all of Egypt was uninhabited for forty years. Babylonian attacks occurred but Egypt remained an independent kingdom.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Literal fulfillment unsupported by known independent record
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [Hebrew Ezekiel 29:11](https://biblehub.com/text/ezekiel/29-11.htm); [Ezekiel 29:17–21](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezekiel+29%3A17-21&version=NRSVUE); [Dan'el Kahn (2022), Egyptian and Babylonian evidence](https://www.thetorah.com/article/nebuchadnezzar-fails-to-conquer-egypt-so-jeremiahs-prophecy-was-updated).

### Jonah 3:4–10; Jeremiah 18:7–10 — Forty days until Nineveh's overthrow {#jonah-3-warning}

- **Original language:** עֹוד אַרְבָּעִים יֹום (yet forty days)
- **Earlier situation:** The story has Jonah announce impending destruction; the city's response leads the narrator to say God does not bring the announced disaster.
- **Later claim / event:** Sometimes claimed as failed prediction or defended as a conditional warning.
- **Type of reuse or prediction:** A teaching story about judgment and mercy, paired with Jeremiah's explicit principle that warnings may be conditional.
- **Independent evidence and limitations:** The narrative itself says the announced disaster did not occur. It gives a reason within the story; this does not independently establish that Jonah preached to historical Nineveh or that its repentance happened.
- **Provisional conclusion:** Non-occurrence and conditional logic explicit within the narrative
- **Primary and scholarly sources:** [Hebrew Jonah 3:4](https://biblehub.com/text/jonah/3-4.htm); [Jonah 3:10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Jonah+3%3A10&version=NRSVUE); [Jeremiah 18:7–10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Jeremiah+18%3A7-10&version=NRSVUE).


## Open inventory and stopping rule

**Batch 1 is complete when:** eleven distinct claims have canonical records, links to underlying passages and scholarship, a published comparison, a source trail, search access, and CI validation. The status is *starter sample*, not "every prophecy done."

Next nonduplicative batches, with each source passage + later claim as the canonical match key:

- **Matthew's remaining fulfillment passages:** inventory every formula quotation and compare Hebrew/Greek originals; do not treat allusions and direct citations as the same type.
- **Other New Testament authors:** Mark, Luke, John, Acts, Paul, Hebrews and Revelation; include quotations and explicitly labeled allusions.
- **Hebrew Bible predictions:** Assyria, Babylon, Egypt, Persian period, restoration and conflict oracles, tested against independent records.
- **Apocalyptic / end-time claims:** Daniel, Jesus' kingdom imminence sayings, Revelation and later receptions, tracking dating debates and genre.
- **Jewish and Christian messianic expectations:** catalog standards and interpretive differences without implying any single modern community speaks for all Judaism or Christianity.

There is no defensible single total for “all prophecies” until the inclusion rule is defined. One passage may be reused for multiple alleged fulfillments; one later author can quote several originals. Neither count is interchangeable with the number of predictive oracles.

Explanations of how and why a later author adapted older material must be grounded in scholarship and acknowledged as interpretations. The absence of external corroboration is a limit on the conclusion, not proof that an event never occurred. Uncertain original dates and authorial identities remain open.
