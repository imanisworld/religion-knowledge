# Gender Over Time

**Module status:** Research module outside the nine-document core corpus. First-pass source scaffold, 2026-09-28.

This module tracks how societies have classified sex, gender, reproduction, social role, religious role, law, and family status across time. It is designed to prevent a common error: treating reproductive biology, bodily sex traits, social gender categories, religious rules, and legal classifications as though they were one thing.

## Method

Every case should separate these layers when the evidence permits:

1. **Reproductive biology** — reproductive anatomy, gametes, fertility, and reproductive role.
2. **Bodily sex traits** — chromosomes, gonads, hormones, internal/external anatomy, and secondary sex characteristics.
3. **Social gender category** — the society's own named or recognized categories.
4. **Religious/ritual role** — priesthood, sacred office, ritual restriction, purity status, or spiritual function.
5. **Law and social status** — property, inheritance, testimony, citizenship, dress, occupation, marriage, family, and state classification.
6. **Historical practice** — what people actually did, which may differ from texts or official rules.

For religious traditions, also keep this chain separate:

**text/doctrine → institutional rule → historical practice → modern practice**

Do not infer one layer from another.

## Terminology guardrails

- Use **intersex** or **variations in sex characteristics** for people whose sex traits do not all align with a single typical male or female pattern. "Unisex" is not the term for this.
- Do not treat **intersex**, **transgender**, **eunuch**, **third gender**, and **nonbinary** as synonyms.
- Do not translate a culture's category directly into a modern Western identity unless the scholarship supports that equivalence.
- **Two-Spirit** is a modern pan-Indigenous umbrella adopted in 1990; where evidence exists, prefer the nation/community-specific historical term and describe its own meaning.
- A category can be socially gendered without being a claim about anatomy.
- Eunuchs require their own track: castration status, social classification, court/religious role, and historical terminology.
- Claims that a culture recognized a specific number of genders must identify the culture, period, terms, and source. **Do not publish "seven genders" as a general fact until a culture-specific source verifies it.**

## Reproduction and the binary justification

This module does **not** begin with the conclusion that reproduction is a "lie." It tests a narrower historical claim:

> Does the existence of reproductive roles establish that every person must fit one of exactly two exhaustive social gender categories?

Those are different questions. The National Academies defines sex as a **multidimensional** construct involving multiple sex traits and notes that those traits are often assumed to align but may not. It separately defines gender as a multidimensional construct involving identity, expression, and social/cultural expectations. Cross-cultural cases can therefore be tested without denying ordinary reproductive biology.

The research task is to trace **when and where reproductive difference was used to justify an exhaustive two-gender social system**, and compare that argument with societies that recognized additional or alternative categories.

## Historical layer I — Ancient Near East to early Christianity

This pass adds cases where the evidence is specific enough to publish without pretending ancient categories map neatly onto modern identity labels.

| Case | Place / period | What is documented | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| *assinnu* in the cult of Ištar | Mesopotamia; evidence spans multiple cuneiform periods | Saana Svärd and Martti Nissinen conclude that the sources support unconventional gender performance and gender-boundary transgression associated with the *assinnu*. They explicitly reject treating castration, same-sex behavior, or a modern transgender identity as settled facts. | **Documented with major qualification** |
| Hatshepsut and masculine kingship imagery | Egypt, 18th Dynasty, c. 1473–1458 BCE | Hatshepsut was a woman who held the office of pharaoh. Official statues could show her in the idealized masculine form and regalia of kingship while inscriptions retained feminine grammar and her female personal name. This is evidence about gendered political representation, not a third-gender social category. | **Documented** |
| Eunuchs in Deuteronomy and Isaiah | Hebrew Bible | Deuteronomy 23:1 excludes a man with crushed or severed genitalia from the assembly, while Isaiah 56:3–5 promises covenant-keeping eunuchs a place and enduring name in God's house. The texts show different treatments of eunuchs inside the biblical corpus; they do not by themselves establish a social gender category. | **Direct textual contrast** |
| *tumtum* and *androgynos* in rabbinic law | Tannaitic/rabbinic Judaism | Rabbinic law explicitly discusses people who cannot be classified as definitely male or female in particular legal contexts. Scholarship warns that describing this simply as “six genders” is misleading: the categories are strongly body/legal-status based and the male/female binary remains structurally important. | **Documented with terminology qualification** |
| *galli* of Cybele / Magna Mater | Hellenistic and Roman world | Ancient and modern scholarship documents self-castration, feminine-coded clothing and appearance, and hostile Roman reactions to the *galli*. Some modern scholars interpret their social position as nonbinary or outside expected gender binaries, but that is an analytical interpretation rather than an ancient self-description. | **Documented + interpretive layer** |
| Nicaea Canon 1 and eunuch clergy | Christianity, Council of Nicaea, 325 CE | Canon 1 distinguishes involuntary/medical castration from voluntary self-castration: eunuchs made by illness, violence, or masters could remain or be admitted to clergy if otherwise worthy, while voluntary self-castration disqualified clergy. This is institutional regulation of bodily status, not evidence of gender identity. | **Direct textual / institutional rule** |

### What this changes

- **Mesopotamia:** publish gender-boundary transgression; do **not** publish “the *assinnu* were transgender eunuchs” as settled fact.
- **Egypt:** Hatshepsut shows that kingship imagery could be grammatically and visually gendered in different directions without erasing knowledge that the ruler was a woman.
- **Hebrew Bible:** keep eunuch status separate from claims about transgender or intersex identity.
- **Rabbinic Judaism:** replace the popular “six genders” slogan with the more defensible claim that rabbinic texts recognize multiple sex/body-status categories and legal ambiguity beyond definite male/female.
- **Rome:** distinguish what ancient sources say about the *galli* from modern scholarly theories about nonbinary identity.
- **Christianity:** Nicaea shows a church institution regulating castration status differently according to cause.

## Case record fields

Each mature case should answer, where evidence exists:

| Field | Question |
|---|---|
| Culture / polity | Which society, community, state, or religious tradition? |
| Time range | When did this classification or practice exist? |
| Local term | What did people call the category themselves? |
| Source language | Is the English label a translation or later analytical term? |
| Sex traits | Were anatomy or reproductive traits part of classification? |
| Social gender | How was the category socially understood? |
| Religious role | Was there a ritual, priestly, sacred, purity, or spiritual role? |
| Family / reproduction | Marriage, fertility, parenthood, inheritance, kinship? |
| Legal status | How did law or state administration classify the person/category? |
| Treatment | Prestige, ordinary integration, restriction, stigma, violence, criminalization, medicalization? |
| Change mechanism | Internal reform, conquest, colonization, missionization, state law, medicine, secularization, other? |
| Evidence type | Primary text, ethnography, archaeology, law, court record, medical record, later scholarship? |
| Confidence | High / medium / low / contested |
| Sources | Exact citations and locators |

## Next research standard

A case is ready to move from **research-pending** to **documented** only when the record has:

- a culture- and period-specific claim;
- at least one credible scholarly or primary source;
- local terminology where recoverable;
- a clear distinction between anatomy, reproductive role, social gender, religious role, and law;
- explicit treatment of disagreement or translation problems;
- no inference of historical continuity merely because a modern category looks similar.
