## Constitutional provisions

### Article I, §§3–4 and 6 — Conscience, nonpreference, and religious tests

Article I, §3 protects worship according to conscience, bars compelled support of worship or ministry, bars human interference with conscience, and bars legal preference for a religious establishment or mode of worship. Section 4 bars political or religious tests for public office or public trust other than the oath to support the federal and state constitutions. Section 6 bars religious or political tests for jurors.

- **Legal effect:** State constitutional conscience, noncompulsion, nonpreference, and no-religious-test protections.
- **Classification:** D — religion-limiting rule.
- **Status:** In force.
- **Confidence:** High.

### Article IX, §1 — Ministers and priests barred from the legislature

The Tennessee Constitution still prints a provision declaring ministers of the Gospel and priests of any denomination ineligible for a seat in either house of the legislature.

- **Legal effect as written:** Religion-based disqualification from legislative office.
- **Classification:** A — explicit religious legal basis.
- **Text-on-books status:** Still part of the published Constitution.
- **Operative enforceability:** Unenforceable under controlling federal constitutional law.
- **Confidence:** High.

In *McDaniel v. Paty*, 435 U.S. 618 (1978), the U.S. Supreme Court reversed a Tennessee judgment enforcing the clergy disqualification through the qualifications for a state constitutional convention and held that the disqualification violated the First Amendment as applied to the states.

### Article IX, §2 — Belief-in-God office qualification

Article IX, §2 states that a person who denies the being of God or a future state of rewards and punishments may not hold civil office in Tennessee.

- **Legal effect as written:** Express religious-belief qualification for civil office.
- **Classification:** A.
- **Text-on-books status:** Still printed in the Constitution.
- **Operative enforceability:** Unenforceable under controlling federal no-religious-test doctrine, including *Torcaso v. Watkins*, 367 U.S. 488 (1961).
- **Confidence:** High for the text and federal constitutional rule.

## Religious accommodation and liberty

### Tennessee Preservation of Religious Freedom Act — T.C.A. §4-1-407

Public Chapter 573 of 2009 enacted Tennessee's statutory protection against government substantially burdening a person's free exercise of religion, including burdens from generally applicable rules, unless the government demonstrates an essential compelling governmental interest and the least restrictive means.

The statute also provides a claim or defense and specified remedies. Later General Assembly enactments amended the section to add protections concerning governmental subpoenas or compelled testimony regarding clergy sermons; the core substantial-burden standard remains the statutory anchor.

- **Classification:** D.
- **Status:** In force; originally enacted in 2009 and later amended.
- **Confidence:** High for the enacted core standard; broader amendment history remains an expansion item.

## Historical or unenforceable rules

Article IX, §§1–2 are important text-on-books examples: the religious disqualifications remain printed in Tennessee's Constitution but cannot be enforced consistently with controlling federal constitutional law.

No separate historical-causation category is assigned here merely from the religious language of those provisions.

## Landmark cases

- *McDaniel v. Paty*, 435 U.S. 618 (1978) — Tennessee's clergy disqualification, as applied through the constitutional-convention qualification statute, violated the First Amendment.
- *Torcaso v. Watkins*, 367 U.S. 488 (1961) — states may not condition public office on a declaration of belief in God; relevant to Tennessee Article IX, §2.

## Research caution

The fact that a religious qualification remains printed in a state constitution does not establish that it is legally enforceable. Tennessee's Article IX disqualifications must be separated from the controlling federal constitutional rules that disable their enforcement.
