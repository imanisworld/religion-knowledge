## Constitutional provisions

### Article II, §5 — Freedom of religion

Montana provides that the State shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion or prohibiting the free exercise thereof.

- **Status:** In force.
- **Classification:** D — protects/limits religion.
- **Confidence:** High.

### Article X, §6 — Aid prohibited to sectarian schools

Montana bars state and local governmental bodies from making direct or indirect appropriations or payments from public funds for a sectarian purpose or to aid institutions controlled in whole or in part by a church, sect, or denomination.

- **Status:** The text remains in force.
- **Federal constraint:** *Espinoza v. Montana Department of Revenue*, 591 U.S. 464 (2020), held that Montana could not apply the provision to exclude otherwise eligible religious schools from the neutral scholarship program at issue solely because of religious status.
- **Classification:** D — religion-limiting funding rule, constrained by federal Free Exercise doctrine.
- **Confidence:** High.

## Education and public funding

The no-aid clause is Montana's central religion-and-school-funding provision. *Espinoza* does not erase Article X, §6; it limits discriminatory application of the clause where federal Free Exercise doctrine requires equal treatment.

## Religious accommodation and liberty

### Montana Religious Freedom Restoration Act — MCA §§27-33-101 through 27-33-105

Montana enacted a state RFRA through Senate Bill 215 in 2021.

The statute prohibits state action that substantially burdens a person's exercise of religion unless the burden is essential to further a compelling governmental interest and is the least restrictive means of furthering that interest. Montana agencies have subsequently cited the RFRA in administering state programs.

- **Enacted:** 2021.
- **Status:** In force.
- **Classification:** D.
- **Confidence:** High.

This corrects the research handoff's statement that Montana's RFRA status was unconfirmed.

## Historical or unenforceable rules

### Historical lineage of Article X, §6

The current 1972 no-aid clause has a predecessor in Montana's 1889 constitution. The *Espinoza* opinions discuss the national Blaine Amendment movement and the anti-Catholic prejudice surrounding many nineteenth-century “sectarian” funding restrictions. Justice Alito's concurrence expressly linked Montana's original no-aid provision to that prejudice.

The project keeps two historical layers separate:

- **1889 predecessor:** documented connection to the Blaine-era anti-Catholic/nativist political environment.
- **1972 readoption:** Montana's constitutional convention retained and adapted the no-aid rule in a later setting; the 1972 text should not simply be treated as if its delegates had the identical motive as nineteenth-century actors.

- **Causation assessment:** **Documented religious historical influence** for the 1889 lineage, with anti-Catholic/nativist religious conflict as an important causal factor; the motives for 1972 readoption are more mixed and require their own convention-history analysis.
- **Confidence:** High for Blaine-era lineage; medium for relative causal weight in the 1972 readoption.
