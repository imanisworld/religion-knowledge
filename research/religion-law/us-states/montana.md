## Constitutional provisions

### Article II, §5 — Freedom of religion

Montana provides that the State shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion or prohibiting the free exercise thereof.

- **Status:** In force.
- **Classification:** D — protects/limits religion.
- **Confidence:** High.

### Article X, §6 — Aid prohibited to sectarian schools

Montana bars state and local governmental bodies from making direct or indirect appropriations or payments from public funds for a sectarian purpose or to aid institutions controlled in whole or in part by a church, sect, or denomination.

- **Status:** The text remains in force.
- **Federal constraint:** *Espinoza v. Montana Department of Revenue*, 591 U.S. 464 (2020), held that Montana could not apply the provision to exclude otherwise eligible religious schools from the neutral scholarship program at issue solely because of religious status.
- **Classification:** D — religion-limiting funding rule, constrained by federal Free Exercise doctrine.
- **Confidence:** High.

## Education and public funding

The no-aid clause is Montana's central religion-and-school-funding provision. *Espinoza* does not erase Article X, §6; it limits discriminatory application of the clause where federal Free Exercise doctrine requires equal treatment.

## Religious accommodation and liberty

### Montana Religious Freedom Restoration Act — MCA §§27-33-101 through 27-33-105

Montana enacted a state RFRA through Senate Bill 215 in 2021.

The statute prohibits state action that substantially burdens a person's exercise of religion unless the burden is essential to further a compelling governmental interest and is the least restrictive means of furthering that interest. Montana agencies have subsequently cited the RFRA in administering state programs.

- **Enacted:** 2021.
- **Status:** In force.
- **Classification:** D.
- **Confidence:** High.

This corrects the research handoff's statement that Montana's RFRA status was unconfirmed.

## Historical or unenforceable rules

### Historical lineage of Article X, §6

The current 1972 no-aid clause has a predecessor in Montana's 1889 constitution. The *Espinoza* opinions discuss the national Blaine Amendment movement and the anti-Catholic prejudice surrounding many nineteenth-century “sectarian” funding restrictions. Justice Alito's concurrence expressly linked Montana's original no-aid provision to that prejudice.

**1972 source limitation:** Floor excerpts and votes were matched to convention transcript OCR and corroborated in independent later scholarship. The source reviewer has **not independently inspected the original Vol. VI page images or Vol. II committee reports**. The following quotes and conclusions about motives therefore remain provisional.

The project keeps two historical layers separate:

- **1889 predecessor:** documented connection to the Blaine-era anti-Catholic/nativist political environment.
- **1972 readoption:** Montana's constitutional convention retained and adapted the no-aid rule in a later setting; the 1972 text should not simply be treated as if its delegates had the identical motive as nineteenth-century actors.

- **1972 convention record (Committee of the Whole, March 11, 1972; provisional):**
  - **The split:** The Education and Public Lands Committee divided. The majority (Burkhardt) kept the 1889 text, citing "unequivocal support… for a strong public school system," education as a state function, and fear that any change "might endanger passage of the entire Constitution." The minority (Harbaugh) sought to drop "indirectly" and to protect federal funds for nonpublic schools.
  - **The outcome:** Loendorf's substitute retained "indirectly" and added a federal-funds exception (53–40). Campbell's *separate* broader "nonstate money" amendment failed 13–79; the section passed by voice vote. These votes were OCR-matched and corroborated by later scholarship, but independent inspection of the [original Vol. VI pp. 2007–2032](https://courts.mt.gov/external/library/mt_cons_convention/vol6.pdf) page images remains pending. Campbell's March 11 vote is distinct from Kelleher's March 9 13–79 vote.
  - **The bigotry charge:** Catholic delegate Schiltz called the clause "a badge of bigotry." United Methodist delegate Harper answered that separation of church and state is "an evidence of an evolution in history that has proved to be very wise." Delegates' self-identified religion did not predict their votes.
- **Causation assessment:** The 1889 predecessor belongs to a documented Blaine-era anti-Catholic/nativist political context. The **provisional 1972 reading is mixed**: reviewed passages discuss public-school protection, separationism, continuity, federal-funds access, and ratification risk. No delegate in the excerpts reviewed defended the clause on explicit anti-Catholic grounds; the absence of such language in these excerpts does not prove the motives of all delegates or committee members. The inherited "sectarian" wording was kept. *Espinoza* judges later interpreted this history differently. Full analysis: `../gap-closure/montana-1972-no-aid-convention.md`.
- **Confidence:** High for current law; **medium/provisional** for the 1972 reconstruction and lower for causal weighting until original page images and committee material have been inspected.
