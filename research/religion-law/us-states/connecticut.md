# Connecticut — Religion & Law Profile

**Research status:** Expanded anchor profile verified; contraception-law causation narrowed rather than overstated  
**Last verified:** 2026-09-27  
**Confidence:** High for current constitutional/statutory text and *Griswold*; medium for the 1879 law's motive mix

## Constitutional provisions

### Article First, §3 — Religious profession and worship

Connecticut protects the exercise and enjoyment of religious profession and worship without discrimination, subject to peace-and-safety limits.

- **Status:** In force.
- **Classification:** D — protects/limits religion.
- **Confidence:** High.

### Article Seventh — Of Religion

Connecticut's current constitution provides that people have a right to worship according to conscience, bars compelled support or association with religious bodies, bars legal preference for a religious society or denomination, and guarantees equal powers, rights, and privileges among religious societies.

- **Historical context:** The 1818 constitution used more explicitly theistic and Christian language and marked a major transition away from Connecticut's earlier Congregational establishment.
- **Status:** In force.
- **Classification:** D.
- **Confidence:** High.

## Religious accommodation and liberty

### Conn. Gen. Stat. §52-571b — state RFRA-like protection

Connecticut enacted a statutory strict-scrutiny protection in 1993.

The state or a political subdivision may not burden a person's exercise of religion under Article First, §3, even through a generally applicable rule, unless it demonstrates a compelling governmental interest and the least restrictive means. The statute authorizes the burdened person to assert a claim or defense and seek appropriate relief.

- **Status:** In force.
- **Classification:** D.
- **Confidence:** High.

This corrects the earlier research handoff's statement that Connecticut had no state RFRA-like protection.

## Historical or unenforceable rules

### 1879 anti-contraception law — *Griswold* predicate

Connecticut's 1879 law prohibited use of drugs, medicinal articles, or instruments for preventing conception and later supplied the statutory basis for the prosecution challenged in *Griswold v. Connecticut*, 381 U.S. 479 (1965).

- **Enacted:** March 28, 1879.
- **Historical sequence:** The federal Comstock Act was enacted in 1873; Connecticut then enacted a particularly restrictive state anti-obscenity/contraception law in 1879.
- **State legislative channel:** Connecticut historical material identifies Senator Carlos Smith as introducing the bill and P.T. Barnum, chair of the House Temperance Committee, as supporting it.
- **Religion connection:** The broader Comstock anti-vice movement included strong religious-moral advocacy, but the evidence located here also reflects Victorian anti-obscenity, gender-order, temperance, and public-morality concerns.
- **Causation assessment:** **Mixed religious and secular moral-reform influences.** The Comstock lineage and anti-vice channel are documented; a claim that the Connecticut enactment was exclusively or primarily caused by religion would overstate the state-specific evidence located in this pass.
- **Status:** Unenforceable within the constitutional rule announced in *Griswold* and superseded by later law.
- **Confidence:** High for enactment history and *Griswold*; medium for relative causal weight.

## Landmark cases

- *Griswold v. Connecticut*, 381 U.S. 479 (1965) — invalidated application of the contraception ban to married couples, recognizing constitutional protection for marital privacy.

## Historical-causation note

Connecticut is a case where subject matter alone is not enough. The law followed the Comstock anti-vice model, but the documentary record supports a **mixed** moral-reform explanation rather than a single-source “religion caused it” label.
