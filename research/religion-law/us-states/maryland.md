## Constitutional provisions

### Declaration of Rights, Article 36 — Religious liberty

Maryland's current Article 36 protects religious liberty, bars compelled support of worship or ministry, and still prints a proviso tying witness/juror competence to belief in God and moral accountability.

- **Current status:** In force as constitutional text.
- **Legal effect:** The free-exercise/noncompulsion protections remain operative. The printed theistic proviso must be read subject to controlling federal constitutional doctrine.
- **Historical evolution:** Maryland's 1776 declaration protected only persons “professing the Christian religion” and authorized a general tax for support of the Christian religion. The Christian-only limitation was removed in the 1851 Constitution; the old religious-tax language had already been eliminated.
- **Classification:** Current function = D (protects/limits religion); historical 1776 text also contained A/B features (explicit religious qualification and religious historical origin).
- **Confidence:** High for text and constitutional history.

### Declaration of Rights, Article 37 — Religious test for office

Article 37 still says that no religious test should be required for state office other than a declaration of belief in the existence of God.

- **Current enforceability:** The belief-in-God requirement is unenforceable under *Torcaso v. Watkins*, 367 U.S. 488 (1961).
- **Historical evolution:** Maryland's earlier constitutions used explicitly Christian religious qualifications before later broadening them to theism.
- **Classification:** Printed historical text = A; controlling constitutional rule = D.
- **Confidence:** High.

## Religious accommodation and liberty

Maryland does **not currently have an enacted general state RFRA** comparable to the statutes in Arkansas, Louisiana, Texas, or Montana.

- A 2026 proposal, H.B. 356, would have created a general strict-scrutiny statutory protection for burdens on religious exercise.
- As of this verification date, the official General Assembly page shows no enactment; the bill did not become part of the Maryland Code.
- Article 36 therefore remains the core state constitutional baseline, alongside federal Free Exercise doctrine.
- **Classification:** D.
- **Confidence:** High that H.B. 356 was not enacted; issue-specific accommodations remain open.

## Current religion-linked statutes

### 2026 protection for access to worship — enacted, not yet effective on verification date

H.B. 750 / Chapter 424 of the 2026 Acts prohibits using force, threat of force, or physical obstruction to interfere with or injure a person lawfully exercising religious beliefs at a place of worship.

- **Effective date:** October 1, 2026.
- **Status on 2026-09-27:** Enacted but not yet effective.
- **Classification:** D — protection of religious exercise.
- **Confidence:** High.

## Historical or unenforceable rules

### Former Sunday-closing law litigated in *McGowan*

Former Article 27, §521 generally restricted Sunday merchandise sales subject to exceptions.

The Supreme Court's treatment in *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420 (1961), is important because it separates historical origin from then-current legal purpose:

- the Court acknowledged the religious origin of Sunday-closing laws;
- it nevertheless accepted Maryland's modern day-of-rest and recreation rationale as secular.
- **Classification:** B for historical religious origin; current rule at the time of decision was upheld on a secular rationale.
- **Status:** Historical / superseded.
- **Confidence:** High.

## Landmark cases

- *Torcaso v. Watkins*, 367 U.S. 488 (1961) — Maryland could not require a declaration of belief in God as a condition of a notary commission.
- *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420 (1961) — upheld the Sunday-closing scheme at issue while distinguishing religious historical origin from modern secular purpose.

## Historical-causation notes

Maryland is a strong example of legal change across time:

- **1776:** explicit Christian privilege and potential Christian tax support.
- **19th century:** progressive removal of Christian-specific qualifications.
- **20th century:** federal constitutional doctrine rendered the remaining theist office qualification unenforceable.

The causal claim here is direct because the historical provisions themselves expressly regulated religious identity and support; no inference from modern demographics is needed.
