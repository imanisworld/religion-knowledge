# Formal Law vs. Institutional Practice

**Last verified:** 2026-09-27

## Purpose

This module tracks a different question from legal causation: what happens when a country has no formal state religion but Christian-origin traditions, symbols, schedules, or representation patterns remain visible in public institutions?

Do not treat these observations as proof that Christianity legally governs the United States. Keep four layers separate:

1. **Formal law** — what statutes, constitutions, and controlling doctrine require or prohibit.
2. **Explicit government practice** — official ceremonies, symbols, holidays, or language that reference religion.
3. **Institutional default / accommodation** — how calendars, procedures, and exceptions are structured in practice.
4. **Representation / culture** — demographic or cultural patterns that may matter politically or socially but are not themselves law.

Private cultural practices belong here only when they help explain a documented institutional pattern. They should not be coded as legal coercion.

## Verified examples

### Christmas Day

- **Formal status:** Christmas Day is a federal holiday under the federal holiday schedule administered by the U.S. Office of Personnel Management.
- **Institutional significance:** A Christian-origin observance is therefore built directly into the federal calendar.
- **Caution:** This does not establish an official state religion or prove that every modern observance of Christmas is religious.
- **Cultural note:** The canonical nativity accounts in Matthew 1–2 and Luke 1–2 do not provide a calendar date for Jesus's birth. December 25 functions as the traditional Christian observance date rather than a date stated in those texts.
- **Sources:** U.S. Office of Personnel Management, Federal Holidays; Matthew 1–2; Luke 1–2.

### Easter

- **Formal status:** Easter is not listed as a federal holiday on the OPM federal-holiday schedule.
- **Government practice:** The White House continues to host the White House Easter Egg Roll. The White House describes the event as an annual tradition dating officially to 1878; the 2026 event was held on April 6, 2026.
- **Interpretation:** Easter therefore provides a useful example of a Christian-origin observance that is culturally and institutionally visible in government practice without having federal-holiday status.
- **Caution:** The Easter Egg Roll is a civic/family event with Christian-origin timing; its existence does not by itself establish religious coercion.
- **Sources:** U.S. Office of Personnel Management, Federal Holidays; White House, White House Easter Egg Roll; White House, 2026 Easter Egg Roll materials.

### "Under God" and "In God We Trust"

- **Formal / official practice:** Congress added "under God" to the Pledge of Allegiance in 1954. Federal legislation in 1955 required "In God We Trust" on U.S. currency, and Congress declared "In God We Trust" a national motto in 1956.
- **Interpretation:** These are explicit religious references in official civic language and symbols, not merely private cultural habits.
- **Historical context:** Congressional debate in 1954 and 1955 tied these measures in part to Cold War-era contrasts between the United States and atheistic communism.
- **Caution:** Their legal treatment is distinct from establishing a church or requiring adherence to a religion.
- **Sources:** Congressional Record, June 7, 1954; U.S. House History, "The Legislation Placing 'In God We Trust' on National Currency"; U.S. Mint historical timeline.

### Religious composition of Congress

- **Observed pattern:** Pew Research Center reported that 87% of voting members of the 119th Congress identified as Christian, compared with 62% of U.S. adults. Pew also reported that less than 1% of Congress was religiously unaffiliated, compared with 28% of U.S. adults in the comparison data used for that report.
- **Interpretation:** This establishes a representation gap.
- **Caution:** The gap by itself does not establish why it exists, whether voters discriminate, or whether the legal system imposes a religious test.
- **Source:** Pew Research Center, "Faith on the Hill: The religious composition of the 119th Congress" (2025).

### Public-school religious accommodation

- **Formal rule:** Current U.S. Department of Education guidance states that public schools may not observe religious holidays as religious events or favor one religion over another.
- **Accommodation practice:** The same guidance recognizes excusals and accommodations for students' prayer, fasting, religious holidays, and other observances.
- **Interpretation:** This is an accommodation framework. Whether a particular district's default calendar makes one tradition easier to observe than another must be established with district-specific evidence rather than assumed nationally.
- **Source:** U.S. Department of Education, 2026 Guidance on Constitutionally Protected Prayer and Religious Expression in Public Elementary and Secondary Schools.

## Research rule

A "zero" on formal-theocracy measures answers only the formal-law question. It does not establish that public institutions are culturally religion-neutral in every respect. Conversely, a Christian-origin holiday, symbol, or demographic majority does not by itself prove legal coercion or that a policy was caused by religion.

When adding a record here, identify the layer explicitly:

- **Formal law**
- **Official government practice**
- **Institutional default / accommodation**
- **Representation / culture**

Then state what the evidence proves and what it does not prove.
