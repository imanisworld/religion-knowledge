# Maine Sunday Motor-Vehicle Sales Rule — Enactment History

**Status:** 🟢 Closed — Verdict: **Mixed; industry/competitive driver predominates, with explicit Lord's Day framing**
**Closed:** 2026-09-27  
**Target profile:** `../us-states/maine.md`  
**Current provision:** 17 M.R.S. §3203

---

## Existing verified baseline (do not reopen)

The Maine profile already verifies the current Sunday motor-vehicle sales restriction and the explicitly religious/holy-day architecture of Title 17, chapter 105. The open question was narrower: what actually caused the **motor-vehicle-specific** Sunday restriction?

The enactment-specific record is now sufficient to answer that question.

---

## Correction to the preliminary date inference

The rule was **not first enacted around 1955**.

The primary legislative record identifies the originating measure as **S.P. 381 / L.D. 1107, “An Act to Prohibit the Selling of Motor Vehicles on Sunday,” in the 99th Legislature (1959)**. A later federal decision reviewing the same legislative history likewise states that the prohibition was enacted in **1959**.

The current online §3203 history begins with a 1973 amendment, but the Maine Law Library explains that online section histories do not show derivations before the 1964 codification. The absence of a pre-1973 citation on the current statute page therefore cannot be used to date the original enactment.

## Original enactment — 1959

### Legislative vehicle

- **Bill:** S.P. 381 / L.D. 1107
- **Title:** “An Act to Prohibit the Selling of Motor Vehicles on Sunday”
- **Legislature:** 99th Maine Legislature (1959)
- **Committee:** Legal Affairs
- **Senate sponsor:** Sen. Hillman of Penobscot, sponsoring the measure for the Maine Automobile Dealers Association
- **Industry proponent:** Maine Automobile Dealers Association (MADA)

The Senate and House records establish that this was a **dealer-requested measure**, not a Legislature-originated extension of the older general Sunday-closing law.

### Dealer support

On May 5, 1959, the Senate record states that the Maine Automobile Dealers Association sponsored the bill and that roughly four-fifths of responding dealers supported Sunday closure. On May 6, the House record similarly identifies MADA representatives and records approximately 77% support among dealers responding to the association's questionnaire.

This directly establishes the organized transmission channel: **the automobile-dealer industry asked the Legislature for the restriction**.

---

## What motives are actually documented?

### 1. Competitive-coordination / industry protection — strongest documented mechanism

The most important practical problem was collective action among competing dealerships: if some dealers opened on Sunday, others would feel pressure to open as well rather than lose sales.

That reading is not merely inferred from a national pattern. In *Kittery Motorcycle, Inc. v. Rowe*, 201 F. Supp. 2d 189 (D. Me. 2002), the federal district court reviewed the 1959 legislative record and concluded that dealers were principally concerned with competitive effects: individual dealers would feel compelled to open if competitors could sell on Sunday.

**Causation weight:** High.

### 2. Business-operational concerns — directly documented

The 1959 House debate also records practical reasons supporting closure, including the difficulty of completing financing on Sunday and the view that many Sunday visitors were browsers rather than completed-sale customers.

The Senate sponsor described the problem in terms of customers taking test drives on Sunday when transactions could not be completed.

**Causation weight:** High.

### 3. Lord's Day / Sunday-observance framing — directly documented, but not a separate religious-lobby channel

The 1959 House record does contain explicit religious language: a committee member described the automobile industry as requesting permission to observe the **Lord's Day**.

That means the religious connection is **not merely architectural**. Religious/Sabbatarian language appears in the enactment debate itself.

What the record does **not** establish is a separate church, denomination, clergy organization, or other religious organization lobbying for the motor-vehicle restriction. The identifiable organized proponent was MADA.

**Causation weight:** Real but secondary/mixed. It supports religious cultural framing of Sunday closure, not a claim that a religious organization caused the provision.

### 4. Broader Maine Sunday-law inheritance — background, not sufficient by itself

Maine's general Lord's Day law dates to the nineteenth century and the motor-vehicle provision was later codified within the same Sunday/holy-day statutory architecture. The Maine Supreme Judicial Court recognized the motor-vehicle restriction as one of several Sunday-closing laws in *State v. Karmil Merchandising Corp.*, 186 A.2d 352 (Me. 1962).

That lineage matters as historical context, but the 1959 record provides the more specific causal explanation for §3203 itself.

## Verdict

**Mixed; industry/competitive driver predominates, with explicit Lord's Day framing.**

The earlier binary framing was too simple in both directions:

- It would be wrong to call §3203 simply a **religiously caused law** based on its placement in Maine's Lord's Day chapter.
- It would also be wrong to say the religious connection is only **codification architecture**, because the 1959 House debate itself uses Lord's Day observance language to describe the dealers' request.

The strongest documentary conclusion is that **Maine automobile dealers supplied the proximate organized pressure for the 1959 law, principally for competitive and business-operational reasons, within a legal and cultural setting where Sunday closure was still expressly framed in Lord's Day terms**.

That is a mixed causal history, not a purely religious or purely secular one.

---

## Current-status note

The present §3203 still prohibits specified motor-vehicle transactions on Sunday, subject to §3203-A. In 2025, LD 188 proposed permitting Sunday motor-vehicle sales, but the committee reported the bill **Ought Not to Pass** and it was not enacted.
