# Adversarial Pre-Review — Illness Explanations

**Date:** 20 October 2026
**Branch:** `research/illness-explanations-20261020` (PR #213, draft)
**What this is:** An **internal adversarial pass** by an AI assistant. It follows the format in [REVIEW.md](REVIEW.md) and tries to break each claim.
**What this is not:** **It is not independent review.** The same kind of checker that did the internal source check wrote it, and it does **not** satisfy open gate 1. Do not record any claim as independently reviewed on the strength of this file.

**Access limits in this pass:** The cited Bible sites, Perseus, MIT Classics, Oxford Academic and Cambridge Core could not be reached from the working environment. Evidence below comes from web-search results, which quote or summarize pages. Anything that would need the page itself is marked **unverified**. No Hebrew, Greek or Akkadian was checked against an edition.

**Nothing in the module or registry was changed by this pass.** Proposed corrections are listed for the owner and the independent reviewer to accept or reject.

**Most important findings:**

1. **John 9:3 depends on punctuation.** Greek manuscripts have no punctuation. John C. Poirier [CRITICAL] argues that the purpose clause belongs to v. 4, not v. 3. On that reading, Jesus does **not** explain the blindness as existing so that God's works could be revealed. The claim states the traditional punctuation as if it were the text.
2. **Sirach 38:15 differs between the Hebrew and the Greek.** Burkard Zapff [CRITICAL] (*Biblica* 92, 2011) finds the Hebrew (Ms B) more favourable to the physician and the Greek more negative, including at v. 15. The claim quotes one textual tradition without saying so.
3. **The Hippocratic "credit and blame" sentence was not found** in any translation this pass could retrieve. Related passages were found, but the claim's specific structure (credit if the patient recovers, the gods if the patient dies) needs the exact quoted sentence before it stays `verified`.

### `IL-DEUT-001` — Holds

- **Direct evidence:** ESV Deut 28:20–22 (search result): v. 20 curses, confusion and frustration; v. 21 pestilence; v. 22 wasting disease, fever, inflammation. The registry locator (28:21–22) matches.
- **Correction:** None.
- **Opposing interpretation:** None found that contests the narrative reading. The module already separates the narrative claim from a medical claim.
- **Unverified:** NABRE wording, the translation the registry cites.

### `IL-NUM-001` — Holds

- **Current wording:** "A plague is linked to divine wrath; Aaron offers incense and makes atonement; the plague ends."
- **Direct evidence:** ESV Num 16:46–49 (search result): wrath has gone out and the plague has begun (v. 46); Aaron makes atonement and stands between the dead and the living, and the plague is stopped (vv. 47–48); 14,700 die (v. 49).
- **Correction:** None.
- **Opposing interpretation:** None found.
- **Unverified:** NRSVue wording; versification in Hebrew Bible editions, where this passage may carry chapter 17 numbering. Confirm before citing Hebrew.

### `IL-MARK-001` — Holds with qualification

- **Current wording:** "A boy is described as convulsing, falling, becoming rigid, and foaming at the mouth; a spirit is expelled."
- **Direct evidence:** ESV Mark 9:18: the spirit "seizes him… throws him down, and he foams and grinds his teeth and becomes rigid." Luke 9:39 has convulsions and foaming.
- **Correction:** "Becoming rigid" is one translation. GOD'S WORD has "exhausted"; the KJV has "pineth away." Note that the rendering varies.
- **Opposing interpretation:** No source found contests the spirit-explanation reading. The module already declines a diagnosis.
- **Unverified:** The Greek verb behind "becomes rigid" and its lexical range.

### `IL-JOHN-001` — Holds with qualification; **recommend `verified` → `verified_with_qualification`**

- **Current wording:** "…Jesus rejects both attributions but explains the episode in terms of revealing God's works."
- **Direct evidence:** The rejection of both attributions (v. 3a) is secure. The second half rests on punctuating v. 3 as a complete sentence.
- **Opposing interpretation:** John C. Poirier [CRITICAL], "'Day and Night' and the Sabbath Controversy of John 9," *Filología Neotestamentaria* 19 (2006), pp. 113–119. Poirier moves the full stop so that "that the works of God might be revealed" goes with v. 4 ("we must work… while it is day"). A Working Preacher commentary offers the same alternative rendering. Poirier also summarizes J. Duncan M. Derrett (2003), who reads "the works of God" as the blindness understood as divine retribution, the opposite direction.
- **Proposed correction:** "Jesus rejects both attributions. In the usual punctuation he then presents the blindness as an occasion for God's works to be revealed; some scholars attach that clause to v. 4 instead, which removes the purpose explanation (Poirier 2006)."
- **Unverified:** Poirier's full argument, read here as a search summary of his article pages; Derrett 2003 not located.

### `IL-JOB-001` — Holds with qualification

- **Current wording:** "…at the end Yahweh rebukes the friends for speaking wrongly about him."
- **Direct evidence:** Job 42:7. The NET note glosses נְכוֹנָה as "the right thing / truth."
- **Opposing interpretations:**
  - **"About" is a translation choice.** John H. Walton [CONSERVATIVE-EVANGELICAL] (lecture materials, biblicalelearning.org) and the Enter the Bible commentary read the preposition as "to me": Job spoke rightly *to* God in his final words. On that reading, 42:7 does not endorse his speeches about God.
  - **Source-critical reading:** The prose frame may come from a different story than the poetic dialogue. If so, 42:7 may not refer to the dialogue at all.
- **Proposed correction:** "…rebukes the friends for not speaking rightly of (or to) him; the Hebrew preposition allows either."
- **Unverified:** Walton's argument as published; the source-critical literature was not retrieved.

### `IL-SIRACH-001` — Holds with qualification; **recommend `verified` → `verified_with_qualification`**

- **Current wording:** "…and says sinners will defy the physician (v. 15)."
- **Direct evidence:** Burkard Zapff [CRITICAL], "Sir 38,1-15 als Beispiel der Verknüpfung von Tradition und Innovation bei Jesus Sirach," *Biblica* 92 (2011), pp. 347–367. Per its abstract and page summaries, the Hebrew text is markedly more favourable to the physician, while the Greek is more traditional and more negative. Zapff ties the Greek's negative judgment in v. 15 to this pattern and judges Hebrew Ms B closer to the original.
- **Proposed correction:** Add: "Verse 15 differs between the Hebrew and Greek textual traditions; the wording here follows [name the translation's base text]."
- **Opposing interpretation:** Per Zapff's summary, Lührmann explains the difference by the physician's settled social place in the grandson's time, and Hogan (citing Segal) suggests the Hebrew verb in v. 1 may also mean "honour."
- **Unverified:** The exact wording of v. 15 in the Hebrew and in the Greek. Which tradition NABRE follows at v. 15. The c. 180 BCE date is conventional.

### `IL-HIPMED-001` — Holds with qualification

- **Current wording:** "The anonymous author challenges treating the 'sacred disease'… as uniquely divine, disputes purifications and incantations… and argues for bodily causes."
- **Direct evidence:** Translations quoted in search results: the disease has a natural cause; the brain is its cause; the practitioners used "the divinity as a pretext and screen of their own inability"; it can be treated "without purifying or doing magic." Wikipedia and other sources treat authorship as uncertain.
- **Correction:** None. The module's caveats (not modern neurology; does not reject all divine language) are consistent with the sources found.
- **Unverified:** The Greek text; the Perseus/Adams pages themselves; dating beyond the conventional c. 400 BCE.

### `IL-HIPBLAME-001` — **Unresolved**

- **Current wording:** "In §1, the author criticizes practitioners who take credit when a patient recovers but invoke the gods as an explanation if the patient dies."
- **Direct evidence found:** Related, but not the claim itself. Practitioners used divinity as "a pretext and screen" for their inability; they assigned symptoms to particular gods ("the Mother of the Gods is to blame"; "Ares has the blame"). A modern essay (Brewminate) describes the credit/blame pattern, but that is interpretation, not the text.
- **Problem:** No retrieved translation contained the specific credit-on-recovery / gods-on-death sentence. The claim may be correct; this pass could not confirm it.
- **Proposed action:** Quote the exact Adams §1 sentence, with its locator, in the registry notes before keeping `verified`. If it cannot be produced, reword the claim to what was confirmed ("uses the gods as a screen for their inability to help").
- **Unverified:** The sentence itself.

### `IL-MESMED-001` — Holds with qualification

- **Current wording:** "…Mesopotamian traditions included diagnostic descriptions, treatment knowledge, and specialists with different roles."
- **Direct evidence:** Several general sources (French Ministry of Culture glossary; University of Würzburg project pages; others) describe the *āšipu* (exorcist, ritual and diagnostic practitioner) and the *asû* (physician: medications, bone-setting).
- **Opposing interpretation:** The same sources report that the two roles blurred by the first millennium BCE, and that the traditional physician/exorcist split "has increasingly been challenged."
- **Proposed correction:** Cite a reachable source for the specialist roles, and add that the division between them is debated. Keep the Scurlock attribution marked unverified (see REVIEW.md, finding 3).
- **Unverified:** Scurlock 2018 content; Geller's work.

### `IL-MARI-001` — Holds with qualification

- **Current wording:** "Yitzhaq Feder (2019) discusses early eighteenth-century BCE letters from Mari describing separation of infected servants and restrictions on sharing vessels or furniture."
- **Direct evidence:** The TheTorah.com summary of Feder: Queen Šibtum reports an infected servant isolated and eating apart; Zimri-Lim replies that no one should drink from her cup, sit in her chair or sleep in her bed. Edward Neufeld, "The Earliest Document of a Case of Contagious Disease in Mesopotamia (Mari Tablet ARM X, 129)," *JANES* 18 (1986), is the standard secondary reference.
- **Proposed corrections:**
  - **Date:** Zimri-Lim's reign is conventionally c. 1775–1761 BCE (Wikipedia; Barreyra Fracaroli, *Aula Orientalis* 2011, notes that the reconstructions differ). "Early eighteenth-century" understates this. Use "first half of the eighteenth century BCE (reign of Zimri-Lim, c. 1775–1761 on the conventional chronology)."
  - **Source:** Add Neufeld 1986 alongside Feder.
- **Unverified:** The tablet edition (ARM X 129) and its transliteration; the servant's name; which letter says what.

### `IL-TZARAAT-001` — Holds

- **Current wording:** "The biblical category ṣāraʿat cannot be equated straightforwardly with Hansen's disease…; the ancient descriptions do not fit one modern diagnosis."
- **Direct evidence:** *Encyclopaedia Judaica* (via Jewish Virtual Library): the biblical descriptions lack the necrosis typical of Hansen's disease, and no biblical-period skeletons show it. Greek *lepra* covered many scaly conditions. J. M. Sasson (2003, Vanderbilt repository): the earliest skeletal evidence is from Ptolemaic Dakhleh Oasis, c. 200 BCE, and there is no pre-Christian skeletal evidence from Mesopotamia.
- **Opposing interpretation:** Greek *lepra* may have included Hansen's disease. Egyptologists debate one papyrus term. Neither undercuts the claim.
- **Unverified:** Current paleopathology review; the reference works cited are older.

### `IL-SNOW-001` — Holds

- **Current wording:** "Physician John Snow used deaths, interviews, and water-source patterns around London's Broad Street pump to argue for waterborne cholera transmission."
- **Direct evidence:** The MSU John Snow archive and Henry Whitehead history (via search): the handle was removed on 8 September 1854, and Snow himself said the outbreak was already declining before removal.
- **Proposed addition:** One clause guarding against the pump-handle myth: "the outbreak was already declining when the handle was removed (Snow's own account)." REVIEW.md already flags this myth.
- **Unverified:** Snow's 1849 first statement of the theory was not retrieved in this pass.

### `IL-KOCH-001` — Holds

- **Current wording:** "Koch reported the bacterial agent of tuberculosis, providing evidence for a specific infectious cause."
- **Direct evidence:** CDC MMWR 1982 and others: lecture to the Berlin Physiological Society on 24 March 1882; publication on 10 April 1882.
- **Note:** Sources disagree on the journal (*Berliner klinische Wochenschrift* vs *Berliner medicinische Wochenschrift*). The claim does not name the journal, so this does not affect it.
- **Unverified:** None material to the claim.
