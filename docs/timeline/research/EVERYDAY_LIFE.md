# Everyday Human Life — Childhood, Disability, Migration, Language, Food, Violence, Education & Social Experience

**Status:** Research synthesis only. Asserts no historical facts of its own — every claim carries a
confidence rating and citation; items the underlying research could not confirm are marked
**⟨UNVERIFIED⟩** rather than silently smoothed over. Nothing here is publishable `Claim` content
until it passes the research pipeline in `SPEC.md` §8.

**Governing question:** what was it actually like to be a person living in a given society, and how
did that experience differ by age, sex/gender, class, status, occupation, ethnicity, health,
location, and legal position? This extension covers the "ordinary life" layers this project's prior
tracks (human evolution, religion-as-mechanism, sex/gender/sexuality/family, oral traditions) left
underrepresented: childhood/age, disability/health, migration/identity/race, language,
food/ecology/economy, justice/violence, education, love/friendship/body/housing/entertainment,
information/memory, and disaster/cooperation. Compiled from seven parallel research passes, each
independently verified by live web search — not drawn from model memory. One research batch
partially failed on a first attempt due to an account-level rate limit and was fully relaunched and
completed; every finding below reflects the completed pass.

## A. Major Missing-Layer Assessment

Confirmed as genuinely underrepresented before this pass: childhood/life-course (age was nowhere
treated as its own variable), disability (absent entirely), migration/identity/race (present only
implicitly inside slavery and gender research), language (never separated from ethnicity), ordinary
food/economic life (only elite/state economic history existed), justice/violence beyond war,
education/socialization as a distinct institution, emotional/intimate life beyond sex, housing,
information networks, and historical memory as a mechanism (distinct from religious/political
legitimation, already covered). All ten are now populated below with global, non-default-Mesopotamia
research, matching this project's standing discipline.

## B. Childhood/Life-Course Research Map

**Age-grade systems.** Oromo Gadaa (Ethiopia/Kenya) — a cyclical ten-grade system, UNESCO-inscribed
2016; the *Luba* grade holds executive political power for a fixed 8-year term before ceding it in a
rotational ~40-year cycle across five parties — a genuine non-hereditary, non-electoral succession
mechanism. Specific grade-age bands circulating online are **⟨UNVERIFIED⟩** against Asmarom Legesse's
primary scholarship (*Gada: Three Approaches to the Study of African Society*, 1973; *Oromo
Democracy*, Red Sea Press, 2000) — the succession mechanism itself is solid, the age table is not.
Maasai age-sets (*olaji*) — women join their *husbands'* age-set rather than holding independent
membership, an important asymmetry. Nuer (Evans-Pritchard's trilogy, 1940/1951/1956) — scarification
initiation (*gar*) at 14–16, four-year gaps between age-sets. Zulu *amabutho* — pre-dates Shaka but he
converted it into standing age-regiments doubling as labor gangs and police, not purely military.
**Non-African comparator**: A'uwẽ/Xavante (Central Brazil), documented by David Maybury-Lewis —
two-moiety, four-group age-class system with a ceremonial log race; scholarship itself notes
non-African age-grade systems are comparatively under-studied, a real evidentiary asymmetry.

**Initiation ceremonies (non-African-only, per the governing global-coverage rule).** Apache Sunrise
Ceremony (Na'ii'ees) — female puberty rite, suppressed under U.S. assimilation policy, legally
re-enabled only after the 1978 American Indian Religious Freedom Act — its continuity was
state-interrupted, not simply traditional. Sambia (Papua New Guinea) male initiation — Gilbert
Herdt's "ritualized homosexuality" framing (*Guardians of the Flutes*, 1982) is now treated as a
**contested, over-generalizing term**, not settled anthropology. Aboriginal Australian Bora
initiation — subincision is described as unique to Aboriginal Australia among world initiation
practices; a 2024 study links higher-cost initiation rites to desert-habitat groups specifically
(ecological stress correlating with ritual severity). Samoan tatau/pe'a — Polynesian case, kept
distinct from the Melanesian Sambia case.

**Marriage age.** Rome — the "child-bride" picture is substantially an elite-literary artifact;
Saller and Shaw's epigraphic (tombstone) demographic analysis finds non-elite men married in their
late 20s, women late teens. The Hajnal Line (John Hajnal, 1965) — northwest Europe shows late
marriage (mid-20s both sexes), small spousal age gap, up to a third permanently unmarried, neolocal
household formation; the Netherlands averaged ~27 (women)/~30 (men) from the late 1400s to WWII.
China (Song–Qing) statutory age (16 men/14 women) is **⟨UNVERIFIED⟩** against the Lee-Campbell Group's
actual archival demographic findings — only the statutory figure was confirmed this pass.

**Child labor, five documented society-types.** Agricultural (medieval England — Barbara Hanawalt's
~3,000 coroners'-inquest dataset, *The Ties That Bound*, Oxford UP, 1986); pastoral (Maasai — skill
transfer largely through unsupervised "herding games," not direct instruction, from ~age 2); urban
craft (medieval guild apprenticeship, ages 10–15, 3–10 year indentures); industrial (Britain
1760s–1840s — the 1833 Factory Act is the first with real enforcement teeth: paid inspectors, age
certificates); and Caribbean plantation slavery — a genuinely age-graded coercive labor hierarchy
(Third/Second/First gang by age and strength), structurally distinct from the other four voluntary/
semi-voluntary cases.

**Old age.** Roman *senatus*/*senex* etymology reflects genuine structural ideology (age = accumulated
judgment), not just a retained title — though how long the literal age requirement held into the
late Republic is **⟨UNVERIFIED⟩**. Elder-abandonment counterexamples, actively sought per this
project's method: Netsilik Inuit assisted-death custom is real but **not universal across Inuit
groups** — at least one systematic ethnographic review found no evidence of abandonment in some
groups. Japanese *ubasute* is the clean negative case: folklorist Kunio Yanagita traced the
mountain-abandonment legend to Indian Buddhist mythology, and the *Kodansha Illustrated Encyclopedia
of Japan* states it "does not seem ever to have been a common custom" — a famous tradition that
current scholarship regards as legend, not documented practice.

**Education/socialization.** Mesopotamian *edubba* ("tablet house," traced to Ur-Namma, ~2112–2095
BCE) — fee-funded, restricted to upper-class families, ~12-year curriculum. Sankore/Timbuktu — a
decentralized confederation of mosque-based schools, not a centrally administered university; the
popular 25,000-student/700,000-manuscript figures are **actively contested by named scholarship**
(Charles Stewart) and should be reported as a range, not point figures. Buddhist monastic education
(Tibet, from Samye Monastery, mid-8th c.) — novices must already be literate before formal study
begins, an important sequencing detail. Aztec calmecac/telpochcalli — a directly documented case of
**class-stratified formal education by design**: noble boys to priesthood/government track, commoner
boys to warfare-only track, at age 14. Women's education: Rome (instrumentalized to marital utility,
not pursued for its own sake, though recent reassessment suggests broader female literacy than
earlier 20th-century scholarship assumed); the medieval/early-modern Islamic world (a genuine access
case, not just restriction — Fatima al-Fihri founded the University of al-Qarawiyyin, 9th c.; women
earned formal teaching licenses/*ijazahs*); Ming China (explicit restriction, documented via a
primary quote from scholar Lu Kun fearing educated daughters would become "over ambitious").
Elite-to-mass transition: Sweden (religious compulsion via the 1686 Church Law, not secular
schooling, produced >80% reading literacy by the early 18th century — parish examination registers
are a genuinely unusual pre-modern quantitative data source); Prussia (state absolutism +
Halle-Pietist reform, compulsory from 1717/1763, directly shaping Horace Mann's 1843-inspired U.S.
common-school movement). Explicit curricular exclusion: U.S. antebellum anti-literacy laws
(South Carolina 1740, tightened after Nat Turner's 1831 rebellion — penalties up to death for
teachers); Indian caste-based exclusion from Vedic learning (though scholarship is explicitly
divided on how absolute/universal this was across all periods and regions).

## C. Disability/Health Research Map

**Bioarchaeology of care — the methodological backbone.** Lorna Tilley's four-stage
"bioarchaeology of care" framework (*Theory and Practice in the Bioarchaeology of Care*, Springer,
2015) explicitly separates inferring *care-need* from skeletal pathology from inferring *social
attitude* — the latter requiring independent corroboration, exactly the caution this project's
governing brief demands. Three named cases: **Man Bac Burial 9** (Neolithic Vietnam, ~3,700–4,000
BP) — a young man survived roughly a decade of juvenile-onset quadriplegia fully dependent for all
activities of daily living; the skeleton proves sustained care was provided, not what social meaning
it carried. **Romito 2** (Upper Paleolithic Italy, ~11,000 BP) — earliest documented chondrodystrophic
dwarfism case, recently re-sexed via aDNA (2026) as female, a close relative of Romito 1; survival to
late adolescence shows accommodation, not the degree of integration. **Moche/Wari foot amputation**
(Peru, AD 100–1000) — healed, weight-bearing-renewed amputations with likely wooden prostheses;
Verano et al. explicitly list therapeutic, punitive, and ritual causation as competing explanations
the bone evidence alone cannot resolve — a textbook case of the caution this project insists on.

**Disability in recognized occupational roles.** Japan's blind-musician guild system is the strongest
documented case: *biwa hōshi* (itinerant Buddhist-robed lute performers, attested from the 10th
century, credited as original transmitters of the *Tale of the Heike*) and the *Tōdōza* guild
(founded 14th century, formally licensing blind men into musician/masseur/acupuncturist roles) versus
*goze* (blind women musicians) who occupied **markedly lower social standing** despite an equivalent
disability and occupation — a real, under-theorized finding that disability status did not override
gender hierarchy. **⟨UNVERIFIED⟩**: a blindness-specific link to West African griot status or Yoruba
babalawo roles was checked and not found — griot status is a hereditary caste role independent of
disability, and this should not be asserted.

**Exclusion/stigma/institutionalization, real legal evidence.** Medieval European leprosy law —
Lombard King Rothari's 643 CE edict declared lepers *tamquam mortuus* ("as if dead"), a formal
legal-death status, while later regimes (Louis VIII, 1226; Guy de Foulques, 1256–59) combined
segregation *with* self-governance (elected leper "masters") — a real complication of a simple
stigma narrative. U.S. "Ugly Laws" (1867–1974) — San Francisco's 1867 ordinance criminalized public
appearance by anyone "diseased, maimed, mutilated or deformed... unsightly or disgusting"; the last
documented arrest was Omaha, 1974 (Susan Schweik, *The Ugly Laws*, NYU Press, 2009).

**Deafness — four independently attested village sign-language cases.** Martha's Vineyard (17th–early
20th c., Nora Groce's definitive *Everyone Here Spoke Sign Language*, Harvard UP, 1985 — deaf
islanders fully integrated into governance/employment/church, hearing islanders bilingual); Al-Sayyid
Bedouin Sign Language (southern Israel, emerged since the 1930s–40s); Adamorobe Sign Language (Ghana,
~200 years old, hereditary deafness historically ~25% of the village); Yucatec Maya Sign Language
(Chican, Mexico — deafness functions as "a positive constitutive feature of community identity," per
the sourced ethnography). These four converge on a real, cross-cultural structural pattern: sustained
high hereditary-deafness incidence in a bounded, kin-dense community reliably produces bilingual
integration — but this is a structural finding about such communities specifically, not a claim about
how the surrounding mainstream societies treated deafness generally.

**Congenital conditions/infertility, legal treatment.** Rome — infertility was recognized grounds for
divorce with sharp gender asymmetry (surviving legal evidence almost entirely concerns wives divorced
for childlessness). Rabbinic law (*Mishnah Yevamot* 6:6) — ten years of childlessness triggers an
obligation to take an additional wife or divorce. Islamic fiqh — a formal two-tier legal category for
intersex/ambiguous-sex persons (*khuntha ghayr mushkil* / *khuntha mushkil*), with specific documented
inheritance-share rules for the ambiguous case — real, technical, textually preserved doctrine, not
inference.

**Health and medicine.** Epidemic disease outside Europe: China's Third Plague Pandemic origin
(Yunnan, 1855, driven by Han in-migration disrupting endemic rodent plague foci — >12 million deaths
in India and China combined); Japan's 735–737 CE smallpox epidemic (first epidemic recorded in a
Japanese state chronicle, killed ~one-third of the population including all four Fujiwara-clan
brothers simultaneously, directly triggering the Great Buddha commission at Tōdai-ji). Pre-Columbian
American disease: genuinely documented via recent ancient-genomic evidence — tuberculosis and
treponemal disease (yaws/bejel ancestral to syphilis) were present well before 1492 (a 5,500-year-old
*Treponema pallidum* genome from Colombia, *Science* 2026) — but this is **endemic disease presence**,
not documented large-scale **epidemic-style mortality events** comparable to post-contact epidemics;
report the distinction precisely, don't conflate the two. Life expectancy, three societies with
explicit ranges: Roman Egypt 22–25 years (Bagnall & Frier's papyrus census-return reconstruction,
Cambridge UP, 1994); Classic Maya 20–42.2 years (a genuinely wide, methodologically-contested range);
and a flagged **overreach case directly analogous to this project's own eleph/Kubaba pattern**: the
popular "35+ years, medieval Islamic Caliphate general population" claim traces to a thin estimate in
a non-demography journal — the well-attested figure (69–75 years) applies only to a highly privileged,
survivorship-biased subpopulation (Islamic scholars with biographical dictionaries), not the general
population. Do not treat the general-population figure as verified. Nutrition/dental bioarchaeology
(Nubia, Maya) connects directly to the already-established osteological paradox (Wood, Milner,
Harpending, Weiss 1992) — high stress-marker frequency does not unambiguously mean worse health,
because severely-stressed individuals may die before markers form. Empirical vs. ritual medicine:
Ayurveda/Sushruta Samhita's documented rhinoplasty technique (so effective 18th-century British
surgeons traveled to India to learn it from practicing potter-caste surgeons); TCM's artemisinin
(Tu Youyou's 2015 Nobel Prize, directly traced to a 4th-century materia medica reference); the Aztec
Badianus Manuscript (1552, 185 illustrated herbal entries organized empirically by ailment/body
location). Surgery: Peruvian trepanation survival rates rose from ~40% (400–200 BC) to ~91% (AD
1000–1400) across ~800 examined skulls (John Verano), a real quantified outcome trend. Sanitation:
Mohenjo-daro's individual house drains and Great Bath are well attested archaeologically, though the
popular "far exceeded Mesopotamia/Egypt" comparative ranking is **not traced to a rigorous comparative
study** in what was found — treat the infrastructure claim as solid, the ranking claim as unverified.

## D. Migration/Identity Research Map

**Migration and mobility.** Forced deportation as state policy, three regions: Neo-Assyrian mass
deportation (Bustenay Oded's ~4.4 million estimate is, by Oded's own admission, methodologically
fragile extrapolation — treat as order-of-magnitude, not a hard count); Soviet 1944 deportations
(Chechens/Ingush ~500,000 in one week; Crimean Tatars, NKVD figure 225,009, >40% mortality in
transit/exile — J. Otto Pohl documents the underlying "collective treason" charge was based on
fabricated NKVD reports); U.S. 1830s Indian Removal (Cherokee Trail of Tears, ~25% mortality on the
march; ~60,000 forcibly relocated across the Five Tribes). Legal mobility restriction: Russian
serfdom (1649 Sobornoye Ulozheniye legally bound peasants to land, but by the 18th century serfs were
routinely sold *without* land — closer to chattel slavery than the Western European model, per Peter
Kolchin's direct comparative study); Joseon Korea's *nobi* system (hereditary bound labor, ~30% of
the population at points — genuinely contested among Korean historians as "serfdom" vs. "slavery,"
not consensus). Labor migration: Indian indentured labor (1838–1917/1920, >500,000 to Caribbean
colonies, historian Hugh Tinker called it "a new form of slavery"); European guild journeyman tramping
(institutionalized, guild-mandated multi-year travel, minimum 3 years + 1 day in the German
tradition). Migration detected via science independent of text: Bell Beaker genomic study (Olalde,
Reich et al., *Nature* 2018) found ~90% replacement of the British gene pool within a few hundred
years, overturning prior "pots not people" assumptions specifically for Britain (while finding no
comparable migration signal between Iberian and Central European Beaker populations, where the
phenomenon spread culturally instead); the Amesbury Archer's strontium/oxygen isotopes show
individual migration from the Alpine foothills to Britain with zero textual record; a 2023 Xiongnu
Empire aDNA study found the empire was genetically heterogeneous, built through incorporation and
marriage rather than a single ethnic core.

**Identity, ethnicity, belonging — societies' own categories.** Greek *barbaros* (originally an
onomatopoeic, non-moralized label for unintelligible speech, hardened into a moralized Greek/Persian
binary only after the Persian Wars). Chinese Hua–Yi distinction — explicitly permeable, defined by
ritual/clothing/propriety rather than bloodline; outsiders could become "Hua" by adopting Chinese
norms; the Qing modified it into a tripartite system adding a "non-Yi" category for Manchus/Mongols.
Egyptian "Nine Bows" — a symbolic, not literal, totalizing category tied to Pharaonic maintenance of
Maat. Nahua "Chichimeca" — commonly glossed as "barbarian" but scholarship corrects this: it
connoted "hunter-forager" status and was applied inconsistently, including to sedentary farming
groups. Citizenship as a specific legal category: Athens' Periclean law (451/450 BCE) restricted
citizenship to those with *both* Athenian parents — genuine scholarly debate over whether this was an
anti-Cimon political weapon or immigration anxiety; Rome's tiered *civitas* system (full citizenship
vs. *civitas sine suffragio* vs. *peregrini* vs. *libertini*) graduated legal capacity rather than
functioning as a binary. Identity reconfigured by encounter — Hutu/Tutsi Rwanda (Mahmood Mamdani,
*When Victims Become Killers*, Princeton UP, 2001): Belgian colonial administration, formalized via
1926 identity cards sometimes assigned by cattle-ownership threshold, converted a fluid precolonial
status distinction into a fixed racialized political category.

**Race and racialization.** The ethnocentrism/racism distinction is itself a live, unresolved
scholarly debate, not settled: George Fredrickson (*Racism: A Short History*, Princeton UP, 2002)
defines racism strictly as requiring belief that group differences are hereditary/unalterable used to
justify permanent hierarchy — excluding most ancient ethnic prejudice; Benjamin Isaac (*The Invention
of Racism in Classical Antiquity*, Princeton UP, 2004) directly disputes this, arguing Greco-Roman
literature contains genuine proto-racism. **Present both as competing named positions, not resolve
in either direction.** Racial-ideology formation, a genuinely datable process: colonial Virginia
1662–1705 — the 1662 *partus sequitur ventrem* statute (child's bondage status follows the mother's,
reversing English common-law patrilineal norms specifically to lock in perpetual enslavement) is a
directly confirmed case matching this project's own methodological rule that ancient/medieval slavery
generally was not racialized in the modern sense; race-based hereditary slavery was constructed and
codified over decades (Winthrop Jordan; Ira Berlin), not automatically imported from preexisting
European prejudice. Caste vs. race, named scholarship: Oliver Cromwell Cox (1948) explicitly attacked
the mid-20th-century American "caste school" analogizing U.S. Black/white relations to Indian caste
as a category error; B.R. Ambedkar rejected biological-race theories of caste origin in favor of a
socio-religious/endogamy account while comparing caste's *social effects* to slavery; Isabel
Wilkerson's popular *Caste* (2020) equating race and caste directly is **contested by academic
specialists** (Daniel Immerwahr) and should be flagged as a popular, not consensus-academic, position.
Color terminology vs. racial categorization: medieval Arabic/Islamic usage of "white"/"black" was
inconsistent skin-color description (covering Arabs, Persians, Greeks, Turks, and Slavs collectively
as "white") not a fixed hereditary taxonomy — Ibn Khaldūn explicitly rejected the "Curse of Ham"
hereditary-curse explanation in favor of an environmental one. Frank Snowden's "no color prejudice in
antiquity" thesis (Harvard UP, 1970/1983) is dated and has been recontextualized/critiqued
(*Classical Receptions Journal*, 2021) as shaped by Cold War-era racial-liberal politics — cite with
that caveat, not as settled.

## E. Language Research Map

**Multilingualism/diglossia, three non-European cases.** Sanskrit/Prakrit in classical India —
Prakrit appears in epigraphy (Ashokan inscriptions, 3rd c. BCE) *before* Sanskrit; Jan Houben resolves
this "great linguistic paradox" via a diglossia model, not simple genetic succession. Wenyan/baihua
in China — Classical/Literary Chinese was the ~2,000-year prestige written medium while spoken
vernacular diverged as early as the 4th–5th centuries CE (the split is not a clean binary — baihua
retained wenyan elements). Sumerian/Akkadian scribal diglossia — Sumerian died as a spoken vernacular
but persisted as a formally-taught classical written/liturgical language for ~1,500 years, structurally
comparable to Latin's afterlife.

**Lingua francas, four cases.** Imperial Aramaic (Achaemenid Persia, adopted as administrative/
diplomatic lingua franca without displacing Old Persian, extending Mediterranean-to-Indus); Malay
(elevated from a regional vernacular via the Melaka Sultanate, 1400–1511); Sogdian (Silk Road, 5th–8th
c. CE, spread by commercial necessity, documented via the Sogdian Ancient Letters found near
Dunhuang, c. 313–314 CE — private business correspondence, not state documents); Swahili (East African
coast, 8th c. CE onward, trade-driven).

**Language suppression, with real legal evidence.** U.S. Indian boarding schools (Civilization Fund
Act, 1819; 400+/526+ federally-funded/church-run schools, 1819–1969, documented corporal punishment
for Native-language use). Francoist Spain — a specific dated legal instrument exists: the 28 July 1940
Civil Government of Barcelona circular mandating "exclusive use of the national language," with
dismissal of non-complying officials; Catalan/Basque personal names were forbidden for newborns from
1938. **Important negative finding**: the Welsh Not was researched and found to be **not** government
policy — an informal teacher-level practice, not mandated by London or Welsh school boards; do not
cite it alongside the two genuine state-policy cases.

**Creoles/pidgins**, formation process with three named cases: Haitian Creole (17th–18th c.
Saint-Domingue, formed from contact between French dialects and multiple West African languages among
enslaved laborers lacking a shared tongue — genuine scholarly debate exists over stage-theory vs.
Afro-Portuguese-pidgin-origin models); Hawaiian Pidgin/Creole English (1835–1930, sequential polyglot
plantation labor waves); Tok Pisin (late 19th c. Melanesian plantation labor, now an official PNG
language).

**Diglossia as a technical concept** — Charles Ferguson's foundational 1959 paper (*Word* 15(2))
confirmed, with his four original case studies verified: Arabic, Modern Greek, Swiss German, and
**Haitian Creole**.

**Information networks.** Speed estimates, with popular-exaggeration flags throughout: Persian
Angarium (Herodotus's account is disputed on exact road length, ~7–9 days for the full Susa–Sardis
route per modern reconstruction vs. ~3 months on foot); Inca chasqui relay — D'Altroy's peer-reviewed
figure is ~240 km/150 mi per day; the popular "1,250 miles in a week" figure is **unverified popular
amplification** beyond the peer-reviewed source and should not be used. Mongol Yam and Chinese yizhan
speed figures are similarly sourced only to non-peer-reviewed popular history in this pass — flag,
don't assert. Roman *cursus publicus* — state-monopolized, access restricted to imperial *diplomata*
permit-holders, unauthorized use punished — a deliberate documented information-flow control
mechanism.

**Historical memory — erasure vs. non-survival, kept analytically distinct.** Roman *damnatio
memoriae* (formal Senate procedure, contemporary term *abolitio nominis*) — three named cases:
Domitian (96 CE, sanctions so thorough almost no inscriptions survive), Commodus (192 CE, formally
**reversed** in 193 CE by Septimius Severus — direct evidence the mechanism was reversible and
politically contingent, not automatic), Geta (211 CE, forensically traceable at Leptis Magna, name
physically excised even from remote milestone markers). Non-Roman erasure cases, both flagged for
precision: Hatshepsut's monument destruction — **current scholarship (2025 study) substantially
revises the older "vengeful immediate erasure" narrative**; erasure began roughly two decades after
her death, and much is now interpreted as ritual "deactivation" (standard for any deceased ruler's
statues) rather than punitive rage — report the nuanced version, not the popular one. Aztec Itzcoatl's
1430 book-burning (per the Florentine Codex and Durán's *Historia*) — deliberate, attested rewriting
for legitimation purposes, though documented only through Spanish colonial-era sources, not
contemporary Aztec primary documents. Archives: the Library of Ashurbanipal (Nineveh, r. 668–627 BCE,
30,000+ tablets, systematically catalogued by subject); the Ebla royal archive (Syria, mid-3rd
millennium BCE, ~15,000 tablets, undisturbed since ~4,500 years ago); Domesday Book (1086, retained
continuing legal evidentiary authority throughout the Middle Ages).

## F. Food/Ecology Research Map

**Documented diet by class.** Classic Maya isotope meta-analysis (102 individuals, 8 lowland sites) —
a genuinely counterintuitive finding: **elite diet varied significantly over time while commoner diet
remained remarkably stable**, the reverse of the intuitive assumption. Deir el-Medina ration records
— a directly graded hierarchy (~300L wheat/~110L barley per worker monthly, foremen/scribes more);
meat was a regular ration only at the highest tier, workmen ate fish as their regular protein
(⟨UNVERIFIED⟩: standard citation should be Černý's ostraca corpus and Janssen 1975, not directly
pulled this pass). Cambridge "After the Plague" isotope project — a genuine self-audit finding: the
assumption that early medieval English nobles ate far more meat than commoners, sustained by the
*feorm* food-render system, **does not hold up** for the 5th–11th century (though it does for late
medieval Cambridge) — a documented case of received wisdom failing isotopic testing.

**Staple systems and labor.** Balinese *subak* wet-rice system — Stephen Lansing's work shows
water-temple-coordinated scheduling across whole watersheds prevents pest outbreaks; the 1970s Green
Revolution's uncoordinated "plant constantly" mandate caused exactly the outbreaks the traditional
system prevented, leading the Indonesian government to reinstate water-temple scheduling in the
1980s. Aztec chinampas — up to 5–7 harvests/year (exact figure **⟨UNVERIFIED⟩**, needs resolution
against a single peer-reviewed source), fed an estimated ~200,000 Tenochtitlan residents. West African
yam belt — weeding/tillage alone is ~40% of total production cost, a near-year-round labor constraint.

**Famine, with contested death tolls reported as ranges.** Bengal 1943 — figures range 800,000–3.8
million depending on source; Amartya Sen's central scholarly contribution is that this was **not**
primarily a food-availability-decline famine but an entitlement failure (wartime inflation denied
purchasing power to specific groups even as aggregate supply wasn't catastrophically short). Irish
Famine 1845–1852 — ~1 million dead, landless laborers/smallholders on potato monoculture were most
vulnerable; historiography stresses the entire land-tenure/political structure, not just blight,
created the exposure.

**Feasting as political mechanism**, three regions: Cahokia's borrow-pit feast deposits (genuine
scholarly debate between status-differentiation and community-cohesion readings, both current);
Pacific Northwest Coast potlatch (a full governmental/legislative/economic institution — resource
management, obligation-memorializing, risk insurance, investment capital, not merely ceremony);
Durrington Walls, Neolithic Britain (Richard Madgwick's isotope study, *Science Advances* 2019, found
pigs brought from across Britain for feasting events — direct isotopic evidence of long-distance
mobilization tied to Stonehenge-area monument-building labor).

**Environmental management — sustainable cases, actively counterbalancing destruction-only framing.**
Hawaiian *ahupua'a*/*loko i'a* fishponds — sluice-gate engineering plus *kapu* species-specific
spawning closures, a genuine adaptive-management regime built on accumulated ecological observation.
Indigenous Pacific salmon management — selective, stock-specific in-river technologies that colonial
commercial ocean-interception fishing disrupted (Atlas et al., *BioScience* 2021). Native American
cultural/prescribed burning — millennia of low-intensity fire regimes, actively suppressed by federal/
state policy from roughly the 1850s–1900s. **Degradation cases, with an important self-correction
example**: Maya deforestation-collapse is a genuinely live academic dispute, not settled — a longer
Copán sediment core found forest cover *increasing* 400–900 CE, directly undercutting the older
deforestation-caused-collapse narrative (Rue et al., *PNAS* 2010); more recent modeling argues drought
and deforestation *together*, neither alone, were sufficient — directly analogous to this project's
own standing caution against building causal stories from correlated facts.

**Ordinary economic life.** Household/unpaid labor: Nancy Folbre's "care economy" work quantifies the
scale via modern time-use data; historical cases include colonial Nigerian time-use records (Jack
Harris's 1939–40 fieldwork) and Barbara Hanawalt's/Jane Humphries's English peasant/industrial-era
household economy work (Humphries's *Childhood and Child Labour in the British Industrial Revolution*,
built from 617 working-class autobiographies, won the 2011 Gyorgi Ranki Prize). Non-European craft
guild comparison: Japanese *za* guilds (Muromachi–Tokugawa, over 80 around Nara alone) — a distinct
institutional logic from European guilds despite superficial similarity. Markets/money: West African
cowrie currency (documented by Ibn Battuta, 14th c.; colonial governments actively suppressed it due
to inflationary collapse from oversupply); wampum's dual nature (Haudenosaunee diplomatic-exchange
object *and*, separately, colonial legal tender in New England 1627–1661 — two distinct systems
layered onto the same object, per Marc Shell); the Tiv "spheres of exchange" (Paul Bohannan's classic
case — colonial money's cross-sphere convertibility collapsed a non-market moral hierarchy). Commons:
Elinor Ostrom's 2009 Nobel-winning *Governing the Commons* (800+ real-world cases refuting Garrett
Hardin's "tragedy of the commons" as universal) directly inspired by Robert Netting's Swiss alpine
Törbel study (350 years of communal-tenure records). Labor-burden estimation and the Sahlins debate:
Marshall Sahlins's "original affluent society" thesis rested on **thin empirical grounding** (two
populations, each under one month of field data); Ross Sackett's 1996 meta-analysis (309 pooled
studies) found foragers work ~6.5 hrs/day vs. ~8.8 hrs/day in agricultural/industrial societies — a
real but much smaller gap than popularized — **a genuine partial-collapse result structurally
identical to other overreach patterns this project has already flagged elsewhere.**

## G. Justice/Violence Research Map

**Status-dependent legal rights**, two systems: Code of Hammurabi's explicit three-tier compensation
scale (*awīlum*/*muškēnum*/*wardum*); Tokugawa Japan's status-determined court jurisdiction, plus the
~380,000-person "base people" (*senmin*) category split into *hinin* (with a path back to commoner
status) and hereditary *eta*.

**Compensation/restorative-justice, four regions.** Germanic *wergild* (Lex Salica — a free woman of
childbearing age valued at *triple* a free man's price, reflecting reproductive-loss logic). Nuer
cattle compensation, negotiated by the "leopard-skin chief" (a ritual mediator with no coercive
authority) — settlement likelihood dropped sharply with genealogical distance between parties. Māori
*utu*/*muru* — formally recognized by colonial law (1844, 1867) then abolished (1893). Haudenosaunee
condolence practice (wampum-based, ceremonial reparation and reintegration) — **⟨UNVERIFIED⟩**: no
fixed monetary compensation tariff comparable to wergild was found, only the ceremonial/mourning
framework.

**Customary law without formal courts**, four ethnographically documented systems: Somali Xeer (I.M.
Lewis's foundational *A Pastoral Democracy*, 1961 — unwritten inter-clan contract enforced via elder
mediation and collective clan liability); Kapauku Papuans (Leopold Pospisil's *Kapauku Papuans and
Their Law*, Yale, 1958, documenting 176 actual dispute decisions); Ifugao (R.F. Barton, 1919 —
"comparative peace and security" achieved through kin-brokered settlement despite a headhunting
culture); Nuer (as above).

**Punishment: actual practice vs. code text**, two systems. English "Bloody Code" — nominal capital
punishment for most property crimes, but juries routinely downgraded charges and pardons/commutation
to transportation were the actual dominant serious sentence. Qing China (Bodde & Morris's foundational
190-case study) — a graduated, reviewed system where sentence changes moved one category at a time
through a hierarchy reaching the emperor.

**Violence beyond war, kept distinct from warfare (already covered elsewhere).** Homicide rates
outside Western Europe are genuinely thin data — honestly report this rather than fill the gap;
Fariba Zarinebaf's Ottoman Istanbul court-register study and William Taylor's colonial Mexico trial
study are the right sources but specific per-capita rates were **⟨UNVERIFIED⟩** this pass. Domestic
violence with methodological caution: Margaret Judd's Kerma-period Nubia forearm-fracture study
explicitly critiques over-attributing skeletal trauma to interpersonal violence versus accidental
injury — the standard caution citation (Philip Walker, 2001) applies; only one population case was
verified, a second is a genuine gap. Feud as a distinct category, three systems: medieval Iceland
(Jesse Byock — *víg*, openly-acknowledged compensable killing, vs. *morð*, secret non-compensable
murder — feud as structured legal-social institution, not simple vendetta); Nuer feud; Albanian blood
feud under the Kanun of Lekë Dukagjini (a codified oral custom, 1,262 articles, that *regulated*
rather than simply licensed vengeance — protecting women/children/guests, establishing truces). State/
judicial violence: Qing *lingchi* ("death by a thousand cuts" — Brook, Bourgon & Blue's definitive
study traces incision counts rising over time and its 1905 abolition); Edo-period Japan's status-
graded execution system (Botsman) — the widely-repeated "200,000 executions at Kozukappara" figure is
**⟨UNVERIFIED⟩ and likely inflated**, sourced only to a secondary blog page. Ritual violence distinct
from Aztec flower-wars (already covered): Inca *capacocha* child sacrifice (isotope/hair-chemistry
evidence of escalating coca/alcohol administration, predominantly high-status children); late Shang
oracle-bone-documented human sacrifice (>13,000 estimated over ~200 years, disproportionately war
captives, especially the named Qiang group); Dahomey's Annual Customs (*Xwetanu*, typically 40–300
killed per year, an outlier report of 4,000 in 1727 — figures should be treated as period estimates,
not census data).

## H, J, K, L. Emotional Life, Beauty/Body, Housing, and Entertainment/Leisure

**History of emotions as an academic field.** Barbara Rosenwein's "emotional communities" (*Emotional
Communities in the Early Middle Ages*, Cornell UP, 2006) explicitly critiques Norbert Elias's
"civilizing process" thesis; William Reddy's "emotives" (*The Navigation of Feeling*, Cambridge UP,
2001) — self-referential emotional utterances that actively alter the speaker's state, not merely
describe it. Ritual friendship: Chinese sworn brotherhood (*jiébài xiōngdì*) could function as
economic/political alliance as much as friendship; Byzantine *adelphopoiesis* ("brother-making") is a
**live, unresolved historiographical dispute** — John Boswell read it as Church-sanctioned same-sex
union, Claudia Rapp reads it as monastic-derived social-networking kinship strategy; present both
named positions. Documented grief: Cicero's letters on his daughter Tullia's death defy the expected
Roman stoic-restraint norm for elite men; ancient Egyptian "Letters to the Dead" preserve genuine
expressed emotion including blaming the deceased for present misfortune. The Ariès debate: Philippe
Ariès's *Centuries of Childhood* (1960) claim that premodern parental affection is a historical novelty
is **substantially challenged** by Linda Pollock's ~400-diary analysis (*Forgotten Children*, Cambridge
UP, 1983), finding no significant change in parental care/affection 1500–1900.

**Body modification, three traditions plus a decline case.** Māori tā moko (rank/lineage/mana marker
— ⟨UNVERIFIED⟩ sourcing this pass is tourism-tier, cross-check against Te Awekotuku's *Mau Moko*
before use); Yoruba/West African facial scarification (*ilà*) — genuine academic sourcing (Megan
Vaughan 2007; Olatunji Ojo, *History in Africa* 2014), decline driven by colonial/missionary influence,
formally curtailed by Nigeria's 2003 Child Rights Act; Maya cranial modification — a two-tier system
(pointed for nobility, rounded for commoners) narrowing to an elite-only marker over the Classic
period. Chinese foot binding's suppression — the Foot Emancipation Society (tied to the 1898 Hundred
Days' Reform), Cixi's 1902 ban (rescinded, then reasserted via the Natural Feet Society, 1905),
concluding by the late 1920s–early 1930s. Sumptuary laws: England's 1363 Statute (repealed the *next*
Parliament for financial harm, likely never enforced); Tokugawa Japan's Keian Proclamation (1649,
widely flouted, mocked as "three-day laws," prompting documented workarounds).

**Housing.** Pompeii house-size-as-inequality-proxy (peer-reviewed 2023 *World Archaeology* study —
elite domus up to ~3,000 m², house-size variance interpreted as widening status competition; the
modern public/private binary is argued to poorly fit Pompeian houses, which functioned as "private
fora"). Deir el-Medina's standardized four-room workmen's houses, archaeologically identified sleeping
arrangements. Mohenjo-daro — near-universal private wells/indoor bathrooms/drainage across 108
surveyed houses.

**Time/calendars/entertainment.** Three calendar systems: Aztec dual tonalpōhualli (260-day
ritual)/xiuhpōhualli (365-day solar, governing 20-day market cycles); Islamic Hijri (purely lunar,
observational new-moon sighting); Chinese lunisolar (24 solar terms plus lunar months, all major
festivals fixed to it). Griots as musician-historians (West Africa, apprenticeship from age 7–8).
Native North American dice games — Stewart Culin's definitive 1907 catalogue of 293 historic dice
sets; a 2026 *American Antiquity* paper dates the earliest known dice to >12,000 years ago, predating
Old World Bronze Age dice. The Royal Game of Ur — Irving Finkel's reconstructed ruleset from a 2nd-c.
BCE cuneiform tablet, the clearest documented ancient board game case. Public spectacle: the Ottoman
1582 Surname-i Hümayun (52-day imperial circumcision festival, illustrated in a 250-miniature festival-
book, a primary source on Istanbul's economy/society well beyond its nominal religious occasion);
Venetian Carnevale (from 1094, functioning as a genuine political safety valve in a strict hierarchy,
becoming a major 18th-century commercial tourist draw). **Genuine coverage gaps, explicitly flagged**:
Southeast Asian wayang kulit, deeper South Asian pachisi/Mughal courtly context, Go/Weiqi, Aboriginal
corroboree, Pacific hula, South American/Andean music and dance, and Caribbean material were not
reached this pass due to search-budget exhaustion — real holes in the global-coverage ambition, not
resolved findings.

## N, O. Disaster/Resilience and Cooperation/Mutual-Aid

**Societal disaster response**, three cases. 1755 Lisbon earthquake — Pombal's response (corpse
disposal, price controls, a post-disaster parish questionnaire stripped of theological framing,
described by historians as the "birth of seismology") plus early international aid (Britain sent
£100,000). Qing China famine relief — the "ever-normal granary" (*changping cang*) system, documented
by Pierre-Étienne Will as centralized bureaucratic relief that prevented an 18th-century drought from
becoming full famine. The 1783–84 Laki eruption famine (Iceland) — ~20% population mortality, recently
reassessed (2024) as primarily hunger/disease rather than fluorosis poisoning as earlier assumed;
Danish crown relief failed in *execution* (a relief ship wrecked) rather than in intent — an
institutional-failure case, not a success case. **Genuine gap**: South Asian, Caribbean, Australian,
and Pacific-specific disaster cases were not reached — coverage is weighted toward Europe/East Asia/
North Atlantic, a real hole to fill in a follow-up pass.

**Institutional adaptation to repeated stress**: Andean *qocha* reservoir agriculture, intensifying
specifically during drought periods, managed through non-centralized "heterarchical" community
institutions rather than top-down state infrastructure. Qing granary bureaucracy itself is a case of
institutional adaptation, refined specifically in response to recurrent famine.

**Cooperation without centralized coercive authority**, three mechanism types across regions.
Rotating credit associations: West African *susu* (pre-colonial, traveled via the Middle Passage into
Caribbean forms — Jamaican "Partner," Trinidadian "Sou-Sou"); Chinese *hui*. Communal labor-sharing:
Indonesian *gotong royong* — a 2021 study directly documents its **restoration as post-disaster
solidarity** in Lombok after a real disaster, linking mutual-aid labor-sharing to documented recovery;
Andean *minga*/*ayni* reciprocal exchange solving free-rider problems in herding communities absent
centralized enforcement (Paerregaard 2017) — though a companion study notes informal kin-based
disaster aid is **not gender-neutral** in its distribution, worth carrying into the app's framing.
Guilds/mutual-aid beyond medieval Europe, three non-European comparisons: Chinese *huiguan*/*gongsuo*
(native-place and trade associations providing lodging, loans, funeral support, dispute mediation);
Ottoman *esnaf* guilds and the *vakıf* (waqf) charitable-endowment system (funding almost all public
welfare infrastructure — mosques, hospitals, almshouses, water systems); ancient Indian *shreni*
guilds (acting as banks and trustees from the Buddhist/Mauryan era, major architectural patrons —
documented through hundreds of donative inscriptions, e.g. an ivory-workers' guild funding a Sanchi
gateway, 1st c. CE).

## R. Recommended MVP Variables

Per record: `life_stage_or_age_marker` (using the society's own age/status categories, never assumed
chronological universals), `class_or_status_tier` (explicit, since nearly every finding above shows
class stratification, not universal experience), `sex_gender_marker` (cross-referencing the existing
`GenderSystem` entity), `evidence_type` (bioarchaeological/textual/legal/archaeological, each with its
own confidence ceiling — e.g., skeletal trauma alone never resolves cause without corroboration),
`region` and `period` (this project's existing lane/date-semantics discipline), `confidence`, and a
`counterexample_checked` field, since nearly every major claim in this document was actively tested
against and often complicated by a counterexample (Netsilik vs. ubasute on elder treatment; goze vs.
biwa hōshi on disability-and-gender; Sahlins vs. Sackett on foraging labor hours).

## S. Major Unknowns

1. Several headline popular figures across this document are traced to a single non-peer-reviewed or
   survivorship-biased source and should not be treated as settled: the medieval-Islamic-Caliphate
   "35+ years life expectancy" claim, the "200,000 executions at Kozukappara" figure, the Inca chasqui
   "1,250 miles/week" claim, and the Mongol Yam/Chinese yizhan specific speed figures.
2. Homicide rates outside Western Europe remain genuinely thin data across the historical record — this
   is a real, not merely under-researched, gap.
3. Whether Byzantine *adelphopoiesis* constituted Church-sanctioned same-sex union (Boswell) or a
   monastic-kinship networking strategy (Rapp) is unresolved in the field itself.
4. The comparative "Indus Valley sanitation exceeded Mesopotamia/Egypt" ranking claim lacks a
   traceable rigorous comparative study.
5. Whether ancient/medieval ethnic prejudice constitutes "proto-racism" (Isaac) or falls outside
   racism's proper technical definition (Fredrickson) remains a live, named scholarly disagreement.
