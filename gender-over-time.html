Gender Over Time

How societies have classified sex, gender, reproductive role, intersex variation, eunuchs, religious office, family status, and legal identity—and how those categories changed over time.

Do not collapse different questions into one

A culture can know how human reproduction works and still organize social gender in more than two categories. A person can have intersex traits without belonging to a third-gender category. A eunuch can occupy a distinct social role without being intersex. The module therefore tracks six layers separately.

Reproductive biologyGametes, fertility, reproductive anatomy and role.
Sex traitsChromosomes, gonads, hormones, anatomy, secondary traits.
Social genderThe society's own categories, expectations, dress and roles.
Religion / ritualPriesthood, sacred office, purity, ritual restriction or authority.
Law / statusMarriage, inheritance, citizenship, testimony, occupation, state markers.
Historical practiceWhat people actually did, not only what texts or institutions prescribed.
Religious evidence ruleKeep text/doctrine → institutional rule → historical practice → modern practice separate. A passage in a sacred text does not automatically establish how every community lived.

Starter cases

These are not being treated as interchangeable examples of a single phenomenon. Each case keeps its own terminology, period, evidence type, and qualification.

Bugis: sex, gender & meta-gender

Documented + counting qualification

South Sulawesi, Indonesia · modern ethnographic record

Sharyn Graham Davies distinguishes three sexes (female, male, hermaphrodite), four gender categories—makkunrai, oroané, calabai, and calalai—and bissu as a fifth meta-gender category with a priestly and ritual dimension.

“Five genders” is therefore shorthand, not the whole taxonomy: it compresses distinctions among sex classification, social gender, and ritual role.

Sources
  • Davies, Challenging Gender Norms (2007)

Fa'afafine

Documented + qualified

Samoa · modern research

Published research describes fa'afafine as a locally recognized gender-role category distinct from normative man and woman categories. Western terms such as “third gender” or “transgender” are analytical labels, not assumed to be exact translations.

Fa'afafine is not made to stand for all Samoan gender diversity; newer scholarship separately documents fa'atama.

Sources
  • Vasey & Bartlett (2007)
  • Vasey et al. (2011)

Fa'atama

Documented · thinner literature

Samoa · contemporary scholarship

Mandy Treagus and Dion Enari document fa'atama (formerly also fa'atane) as a comparatively under-studied Samoan category among people assigned female at birth who take masculine roles or identifications.

The evidence base is much thinner than for fa'afafine, so the site does not force both into a simple “four genders” formula or assume one Western identity label fits every person.

Sources
  • Treagus & Enari, “Fa'atama: Indigenous Tomboys of Sāmoa” (2024)

Khawaja Sira / hijra umbrella terms

Documented + qualified

Pakistan / South Asia · historical depth still expanding

Muhammad Azfar Nisar documents a heterogeneous gender-nonconforming community and significant internal distinctions among umbrella terms. Modern legal recognition is tracked separately from identity and historical practice.

Sources
  • Nisar, Governing Thirdness (2021/2022)

Indigenous traditions / Two-Spirit

Scope guardrail

Indigenous North America · nation-specific histories + modern umbrella

Many nation-specific categories are documented. “Two-Spirit” is treated as a modern pan-Indigenous umbrella adopted in 1990, not back-projected as the historical name of every Indigenous tradition. The older anthropological label berdache was rejected by many Indigenous participants and is not treated here as a neutral Indigenous self-category. Future records must identify the specific nation and local term wherever possible.

Sources
  • Smithers, Reclaiming Two-Spirits (2022)
  • Jacobs, Thomas & Lang, eds. (1997)

Byzantine eunuchs

Scholarly interpretation

Byzantine Empire · roughly 600–1100 CE

Kathryn M. Ringrose argues that Byzantine eunuchs occupied a distinct gender configuration beyond a simple male/female social binary, especially in courtly contexts. That interpretation is attributed to Ringrose rather than presented as if Byzantines used modern gender terminology.

Sources
  • Ringrose, The Perfect Servant

Intersex classification & medicalization

Documented

Biomedical classification · 20th century to present

The National Academies describes sex as multidimensional: multiple anatomical and physiological traits usually align but do not always do so. Intersex is therefore tracked as variation in sex traits—not automatically as a third gender. Separate historical work traces how medicine classified and surgically managed atypical sex characteristics.

Sources
  • National Academies (2022)
  • Griffiths (2018)
  • Muschialli et al. systematic review (2024)

Historical layer I: Ancient Near East to early Christianity

These cases are published only at the level the evidence supports. Ancient bodily categories, ritual offices, and gendered representation are not automatically translated into modern identities.

Mesopotamian assinnu

Documented + qualified

Mesopotamia · multiple cuneiform periods

Svärd and Nissinen find enough evidence for unconventional gender performance and gender-boundary transgression associated with the assinnu in Ištar's cult. They do not find decisive evidence that all assinnu were castrated or occupied a specific same-sex sexual role, and modern transgender terminology is not treated as an ancient self-description.

Sources
  • Svärd & Nissinen, “(Re)constructing the Image of the Assinnu” (2018)
  • Peled, “Visualizing Masculinities” (2016)

Hatshepsut and masculine kingship

Documented

Egypt · 18th Dynasty, c. 1473–1458 BCE

Hatshepsut was a woman who ruled as pharaoh. Some official statues depict her in the idealized masculine body and regalia of Egyptian kingship while inscriptions preserve feminine grammar and her female personal name. This is evidence about gendered royal representation, not a third-gender social category.

Sources
  • Met, Hatshepsut in a Devotional Attitude
  • Met, The Female Pharaoh Hatshepsut

Eunuchs in Deuteronomy and Isaiah

Direct textual contrast

Hebrew Bible

Deuteronomy 23:1 excludes a man whose genitalia have been crushed or cut from the assembly of YHWH. Isaiah 56:3–5 instead promises covenant-keeping eunuchs a place and enduring name in God's house. The contrast concerns religious inclusion and bodily status; it does not itself establish a third-gender identity.

Sources
  • Deuteronomy 23:1; Isaiah 56:3–5

Rabbinic tumtum and androgynos

Documented + qualified

Tannaitic and later rabbinic Judaism

Rabbinic legal texts explicitly discuss persons who are not definitely classifiable as male or female in particular legal contexts. The popular “six genders” slogan is too simple: these categories are heavily body- and legal-status-based, and scholarship shows that the male/female binary remains structurally important even while ambiguity is recognized.

Sources
  • Kanarek, “Rewriting ‘Arakhin” (2016)
  • Schleicher, “Constructions of Sex and Gender” (2011)

The galli of Cybele

Documented + interpretive

Hellenistic and Roman Mediterranean

The galli are documented as self-castrated priests associated with feminine-coded dress and appearance, and Roman authors often portrayed them as violating normative masculinity. Some modern scholars analyze them as nonbinary or outside an expected gender binary; that interpretation is not treated as an ancient self-label.

Sources
  • Mowat, “Don't be a Drag, Just be a Priest” (2021)
  • Rauhala, “Obscena Galli Praesentia” (2016)

Nicaea Canon 1: eunuch clergy

Direct institutional rule

Christian Roman Empire · 325 CE

Canon 1 distinguishes involuntary or medically necessary castration from voluntary self-castration. Eunuchs made by illness, violence, or masters could remain or be admitted to clergy if otherwise worthy; voluntary self-castration barred continued or future promotion. This regulates bodily status and clerical eligibility, not gender identity.

Sources
  • First Council of Nicaea, Canon 1

Historical layer II: Second Temple Judaism to precolonial South Asia

This layer makes the comparison sharper: eunuch status, ambiguous sex characteristics, sexual role, and named “third nature” categories did not produce one universal social classification.

Philo: eunuchs as gendered exegesis

Scholarly interpretation

Alexandria · c. 30 BCE–45 CE

Philo repeatedly uses eunuchism and castration in exegetical and philosophical treatments of male and female categories. Modern scholarship reads this material as important to Philo’s gender hierarchy and symbolic anthropology, not as evidence for a recognized Alexandrian Jewish third social gender.

Sources
  • Abusch, “Eunuchs and Gender Transformation” (2002)

The Ethiopian eunuch in Acts 8

Direct text + interpretation

Early Christianity · 1st-century narrative

Acts repeatedly calls the Ethiopian royal official a eunuch and depicts his baptism without narrating any change in that designation. Brittany Wilson argues that gender, ethnicity, and status make him a boundary-crossing figure rather than simply an elite convert.

Sources
  • Acts 8:26–40
  • Wilson, “‘Neither Male nor Female’” (2014)

Matthew 19:12 and Origen

Text + disputed reception

Early Christianity · 1st–3rd centuries CE

Matthew distinguishes eunuchs born so, made so by others, and those who make themselves eunuchs for the kingdom. Eusebius later reports that Origen took the passage literally enough to castrate himself; modern scholarship disputes how securely that episode can be treated as biography.

Sources
  • Matthew 19:12
  • Eusebius, Church History 6.8
  • Heine, “Origen and his Opponents on Matthew 19:12”

Rabbinic aylonit and saris

Documented + qualified

Tannaitic and later rabbinic Judaism

Rabbinic texts classify aylonit and forms of saris through bodily signs, fertility, and legal consequences. Sarra Lev argues that the constructed signs cross sex/gender markers even though rabbinic law generally does not treat the aylonit as a separate gender identity.

Sources
  • Lev, “How the ’Aylonit Got Her Sex” (2007)

Kāmasūtra: tṛtīyā prakṛti

Named textual category

South Asia · roughly 3rd–4th century CE

The Kāmasūtra explicitly describes a “third nature” and distinguishes two forms, including one marked by feminine dress, speech, manner, and a particular sexual/economic role. This is a real ancient textual category, but it is not automatically identical to a modern transgender or gay identity.

Sources
  • Doniger, “The Third Nature”

Mughal khwāja sarās

Documented corrective

Mughal India · 16th–18th centuries

Enslaved, castrated court officials could hold important roles in elite households and political space. Emma Kalb finds that naming, dress, office, and surviving textual evidence generally categorize Mughal khwāja sarās as men—though men whose masculinity could be viewed as different or inferior—not as a third gender.

The modern South Asian term khawaja sira shares a name, but the site does not assume direct institutional continuity across the colonial rupture.

Sources
  • Kalb, “A eunuch at the threshold” (2023)

Historical layer III: Colonial suppression and reclassification

Colonial power often changed gender systems through paperwork and institutions as much as through theology: legal definitions, registries, uniforms, school labor, mission discipline, and imported family rules could all force people into new categories.

British India: Criminal Tribes Act and Hijras

Law + elimination policy

North India · 1871–1952 national framework, with provincial variation

Part II of the 1871 Criminal Tribes Act created registers of people the colonial state called “eunuchs,” penalized registered people for appearing publicly in feminine dress or performing, and restricted guardianship and adoption. Jessica Hinchy’s archival work shows that officials sought to reduce Hijra public visibility and social reproduction over time.

The nationwide 1924 framework was repealed by the Criminal Tribes Laws (Repeal) Act 1952, effective 31 August 1952; some provinces repealed or narrowed the regime earlier. Hijra communities survived the suppression.

Sources
  • Criminal Tribes Act, 1871, Part II
  • Criminal Tribes Laws (Repeal) Act, 1952
  • Hinchy, Governing Gender and Sexuality in Colonial India (2019)

“Eunuch” as a colonial male category

Documented reclassification

British North India · mid–late 19th century

Colonial administration grouped distinct South Asian populations under the English category “eunuch” and defined that legal category in male terms, including persons “of the male sex” judged impotent. The administrative label therefore did not simply translate one indigenous identity; it flattened differences among Hijras, khwāja sarās, zenanas, and others.

Sources
  • Hinchy, Power, perversion and panic (2013)
  • Hinchy book excerpt

Alta California: *joyas* and mission suppression

Primary testimony + interpretation

Spanish California · late 18th–early 19th centuries

Missionary and military sources describe Indigenous people who dressed and worked in feminine-coded roles and frequently use the colonial label joya. Later scholarship shows that this label collapsed distinct nation-specific roles and terms. Missionaries condemned the practices and explicitly expected missionization to eliminate them.

Deborah Miranda calls this process “gendercide”; the page keeps that as her interpretive term rather than applying it automatically to every California community.

Sources
  • Miranda, “Extermination of the Joyas” (2010)
  • Pérez Gerardo (2024)
  • Magaña Mancillas & Balbuena Bello (2010)

Federal Indian boarding schools

Institutional assimilation

United States · late 19th–20th centuries

Federal boarding schools imposed sex-segregated dress, labor, domestic training, and Victorian gender expectations as part of forced assimilation. Two-Spirit scholarship identifies boarding schools and compulsory Christianity as major forces disrupting Indigenous gender frameworks, while survivor histories also document resistance rather than total cultural erasure.

Sources
  • Lomawaima, “Domesticity in the federal Indian schools” (1993)
  • Adams, Education for Extinction
  • Evans-Campbell et al. (2012)

Hawai‘i: missionary gender and family order

Multicausal structural change

Hawai‘i · c. 1820s–1850s

Protestant missionaries promoted permanent sexually exclusive heterosexual marriage and a more patriarchal domestic model. Sally Engle Merry emphasizes that the resulting shift was negotiated and multicausal—missionaries, Hawaiian chiefs, commerce, demographic catastrophe, political change, and existing institutions all mattered.

This is not yet a māhū-specific suppression claim; that requires its own culture- and source-specific pass.

Sources
  • Merry, “Early Pacific Encounters and Masculinity” (2021)

Historical layer IV: Modern medicine and legal sex classification

This layer follows a different kind of gender history: how clinicians and governments turned sex classification into medical protocols, civil records, and passport fields. The categories overlap, but they are not the same system.

Intersex surgery before John Money

Historical correction

Britain · 1930–1955

British clinicians were already performing surgical interventions on people with atypical sex characteristics before the Johns Hopkins protocols became internationally influential. David Griffiths shows that surgery, hormones, gonads, social sex, psychology, and welfare-state concerns were already being combined in changing ways.

The history therefore cannot be reduced to “John Money invented intersex surgery.”

Sources
  • Griffiths, “Diagnosing sex” (2018)

Johns Hopkins and sex assignment

Primary publication + history

United States · 1950s

John Money, Joan Hampson, and John Hampson published formal recommendations in 1955 on sex assignment, sex change, and psychological management. Later historical scholarship describes an influential model emphasizing early assignment, consistent rearing, and bodily conformity to the assigned sex.

That model also had a broader clinical genealogy: it built on existing U.S. and European endocrine and surgical practices rather than appearing from nowhere.

Sources
  • Money, Hampson & Hampson (1955)
  • Eder, “Gender and Cortisone” (2018)

Intersex activism and the 2006 consensus

Institutional shift

North America / international medicine · 1993–2006

The Intersex Society of North America was founded in 1993 to challenge secrecy, stigma, and unwanted genital surgery. By 2006 an international medical consensus explicitly acknowledged patient-advocacy and ethical concerns and proposed “disorders of sex development” as replacement clinical terminology.

The new terminology did not resolve disputes over surgery, consent, or medicalization; it marks an institutional shift, not the end of the debate.

Sources
  • ISNA movement history
  • 2006 international consensus

Birth certificates are administrative technology

Legal history

New York City · 1965–2006

Paisley Currah and Lisa Jean Moore trace major changes in New York City rules for changing the sex designation on birth certificates. Administrative reasoning moved from concerns about preventing “fraud” toward demands that a change demonstrate permanence.

The case shows that legal sex criteria can change even when the document itself still offers the familiar male/female field.

Sources
  • Currah & Moore (2009)

Germany: blank → “divers” → self-determination

Court + legislation

Germany · 2013–2024

Germany first allowed a blank sex entry for some people who could not be assigned male or female. In 2017 the Federal Constitutional Court held that, if the state requires sex registration, people who permanently identify as neither male nor female cannot be limited to only male/female positive categories. The legislature added “divers” in 2018.

The 2024 Self-Determination Act later created a broader procedure allowing people whose gender identity differs from their registered marker to declare another available marker or remove it.

Sources
  • Federal Constitutional Court, 1 BvR 2019/16
  • 2018 Bundestag reform
  • 2024 Self-Determination Act

India: NALSA and legal third-gender recognition

Supreme Court ruling

India · 2014

In National Legal Services Authority v. Union of India, the Supreme Court recognized transgender persons as entitled to constitutional protection and directed legal recognition of self-identified gender as male, female, or third gender.

This is a modern legal classification and rights ruling, not proof that one timeless precolonial “third gender” category existed unchanged across South Asia.

Sources
  • Supreme Court judgment (2014)
  • Official NALSA summary

Iran: transition inside a binary framework

Legal-medical framework

Iran · mid-1980s–present

Afsaneh Najmabadi documents a system in which sex-reassignment and transition have been legally permitted and partly subsidized while same-sex conduct and identities have been treated very differently by law, medicine, and religious authorities.

The case matters because legal recognition of transition can operate largely inside a male/female classification system rather than by creating a third legal gender category. It is not simplified here into a claim that every trans person is coerced into surgery.

Sources
  • Najmabadi, Professing Selves (2014)

Nepal: “other” and X

Administrative recognition

Nepal · 2007–present

After a 2007 Supreme Court decision, Nepal began recognizing a third-gender / “other” category in official documents. Current passport instructions say the sex field should follow the citizenship certificate: M for male, F for female, and X for other.

“Other” and X are legal-administrative categories; they do not imply that everyone using them shares one social identity.

Sources
  • Government of Nepal report to the UN
  • Nepal Department of Passports

Passport markers are not globally uniform

2026 policy snapshot

United States · Australia · New Zealand · as of 28 September 2026

The U.S. State Department currently issues only M or F passport markers matching sex at birth and does not issue X markers. Australia and New Zealand currently retain X options in their passport systems.

This is deliberately date-stamped: passport policies are administrative rules that can change through legislation, executive policy, litigation, or agency practice.

Sources
  • U.S. Department of State
  • Australian DFAT passport policy
  • New Zealand Passports

Historical layer V: Africa and deeper Pacific / Southeast Asia

This layer separates two things that are often incorrectly counted together: named gendered social categories, and kinship/status roles that can cross ordinary sex-linked expectations.

Nnobi: “male daughters” and “female husbands”

Influential interpretation + debate

Igbo society, southeastern Nigeria

Ifi Amadiume documents institutions in which women could occupy socially masculine kinship and political positions described analytically as “male daughters” or “female husbands.” Her work demonstrates that sex did not mechanically determine every social role in Nnobi society.

The broader thesis is debated, so these roles are not presented as proof that all Igbo—or all African societies—had the same gender system.

Sources
  • Amadiume, Male Daughters, Female Husbands (1987)
  • Semley retrospective (2017)

Nandi female husbands

Kinship / status role

Kenya

Regina Smith Oboler documents women who married wives, paid bridewealth, managed property, and became the social and legal fathers of their wives’ children in order to secure heirs. Nandi informants described the female husband as becoming socially male in important property and family contexts.

This is primarily a kinship, inheritance, and fatherhood institution—not automatically a lesbian identity or a separate gender identity.

Sources
  • Oboler, “Is the Female Husband a Man?” (1980)

Hausa yan daudu

Documented local category

Northern Nigeria

Rudolf Gaudio documents yan daudu as feminine men in Hausa Muslim society, with distinctive social networks, speech practices, occupations, and relationships to women’s spaces and bori traditions.

The category intersects gender expression, sexuality, class, religion, and urban social life. It is not reduced here to “gay man,” “trans woman,” or “third gender.”

Sources
  • Gaudio, Allah Made Us (2009)

Tahitian māhū

Historical reconstruction + modern differentiation

Tahiti / Society Islands

Douglas Oliver’s reconstruction of late Indigenous Tahitian society reports a high degree of institutionalized tolerance for anatomically male people who adopted female behavioral roles, identified as māhū.

Modern scholarship distinguishes māhū from raerae and shows that family life, tourism, colonialism, and globalization have reshaped the categories. Modern categories are not assumed to be unchanged survivals from the eighteenth century.

Sources
  • Oliver, Ancient Tahitian Society (1974)
  • Besnier, “Polynesian Gender Liminality”
  • Kuwahara, “Living as and Living with Māhū and Raerae”

Tongan fakaleitī

Documented local category

Tonga

Ethnographic scholarship documents fakaleitī as a recognized Tongan category involving feminine presentation and social roles among people assigned male. Their lives and identities are heterogeneous and are shaped by Tongan kinship, sexuality, religion, migration, and global media.

The term is therefore not treated as a one-to-one translation of “trans woman.”

Sources
  • Good, “The Fokisi and the Fakaleitī” (2014)
  • Besnier (2002)

Indonesian waria

Documented + qualified

Indonesia

Tom Boellstorff documents waria as a widely recognized Indonesian feminine-male / transfeminine social category embedded in national culture.

His analysis explicitly cautions against turning waria into a generic global “third gender”; he describes the category through Indonesian ideas of femininity, masculinity, authenticity, and nation.

Sources
  • Boellstorff, “Playing Back the Nation” (2004)

Reproduction is not the same question as social classification

Biology established · social inference under test

Two gamete types do not by themselves dictate the number of social genders.

Human sexual reproduction uses two gamete types—sperm and ova. In evolutionary biology, anisogamy names this large-gamete/small-gamete distinction.

At the organism level, the National Academies treats sex as multidimensional across chromosomes, gonads, hormones, anatomy, and secondary traits, which do not always all correspond to one binary category.

The additional claim that two reproductive roles require exactly two exhaustive social gender categories does not follow from gamete biology alone. That claim needs its own historical evidence about identity, labor, kinship, law, ritual, inheritance, or status.

Sources
  • National Academies (2022)
  • Henshaw, Bittlingmaier & Schärer, “Hermaphroditic origins of anisogamy” (2023)

What about cultures with seven or more genders?

Audit result: unsupported so far

No defensible seven-or-more case cleared the source bar in this audit.

The September 2026 red-team audit did not identify a university-press monograph, peer-reviewed article, or primary source establishing a historical culture with seven or more formally enumerated social gender categories.

Rabbinic lists can reach six only by converting body/anatomy and halakhic-status categories into “genders,” which this project does not do. Even the Bugis “five genders” shorthand compresses three sexes, four gender categories, and the bissu meta-gender/ritual role.

This is an evidence status, not proof that no such culture ever existed. A stronger culture- and period-specific source would change the verdict.
