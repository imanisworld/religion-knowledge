How to test a prophecy claim

Not every passage reused by a later author was originally written as a future prediction. Compare the earlier context, claimed fulfillment, independent evidence, and real disagreement before judging a claim.

  1. Check the earlier Hebrew, Aramaic, or Greek and relevant textual variants.
  2. Identify the original audience, date, situation, literary genre, and whether it was a forecast, warning, or retrospective story.
  3. Find the later quotation and compare what changed in wording or referent.
  4. Check independent contemporary evidence rather than treating a later fulfillment narrative as independent proof.
  5. Consider documented Jewish, Christian, and historical-critical interpretations.
  6. Record separately what the original text means, what later authors claimed, and what history supports.

A prophecy of judgment may be conditional: Deuteronomy 18:22, Jeremiah 18:7–10 and Jonah 3 must be read together.

Primary evidence, one passage at a time

Isaiah 7:10–17 — The young woman and Immanuel

Later use/test: Matthew 1:22–23 · Contemporary sign later reapplied

Original expression
הָעַלְמָה (ha-‘almah; the young woman)
Earlier situation
Isaiah addresses King Ahaz during a military crisis in Judah; the child functions as a sign within a near-term political timetable (see 7:15–16).
Later use
Matthew quotes the Greek-scripture form using parthenos and applies it to Mary's conception of Jesus.
Relationship
Retrospective messianic rereading, with a Hebrew-to-Greek lexical change.
Historical evidence & limits
The connection is explicit in Matthew. The Hebrew noun alone does not require a miraculous conception; the supernatural claim is part of Matthew's narrative and not independently established.
Current assessment
Original-context reading strong; historical miracle unverified

Hosea 11:1–4 — Out of Egypt I called my son

Later use/test: Matthew 2:14–15 · Past narrative later reapplied

Original expression
כִּי נַעַר יִשְׂרָאֵל (when Israel was a boy)
Earlier situation
Hosea recalls the historical Exodus story and addresses Israel as God's wayward child; its verbs point back to a past relationship.
Later use
Matthew cites Hosea 11:1 immediately after narrating the family's flight into Egypt (Matthew 2:14–15), although their return from Egypt is narrated later (2:19–21).
Relationship
Matthew identifies Jesus with Israel by typology. Seong-Kwang Kevin Kim (2019) notes that the quotation about coming out is placed before Matthew narrates the return and offers a larger Israel/Egypt pattern to explain the application.
Historical evidence & limits
Hosea describes Israel's past Exodus. Matthew's quotation and retelling are explicit but do not independently confirm the family’s travel. The timing of the quotation within Matthew is a real interpretive issue.
Current assessment
Earlier reference to Israel explicit; later typological use explicit
Primary text and scholarship (5)

  • Matthew 2:15
  • TheTorah: Hosea 11 and Israel as a child
  • Yale Bible Study: Matthew and new Exodus

Jeremiah 31:15–17 — Rachel weeping for her children

Later use/test: Matthew 2:16–18 · Exile lament later reapplied

Original expression
רָחֵל מְבַכָּה (Rachel is weeping)
Earlier situation
Rachel personifies grief for Israelite exile; the very next lines promise that the children will return from enemy territory.
Later use
Matthew quotes the lament when narrating Herod's killing of Bethlehem's boys.
Relationship
A poetic lament in an exile-restoration context is given a new narrative referent.
Historical evidence & limits
The reuse is explicit and the original exile context can be verified. Matthew's particular massacre narrative is not independently established by this scriptural quotation.
Current assessment
Different historical referents documented; event evidence limited

Micah 5:2–5 (English numbering) — A ruler from Bethlehem

Later use/test: Matthew 2:5–6; Luke 2:4–7 · Future royal oracle

Original expression
בֵּית־לֶחֶם אֶפְרָתָה (Bethlehem Ephrathah)
Earlier situation
Micah looks toward a ruler emerging from David's ancestral setting amid political insecurity, with hopes of peace and protection for Israel.
Later use
Matthew explicitly quotes Micah as identifying the Messiah's Bethlehem connection; Luke also places the birth in Bethlehem but supplies a different journey narrative and does not use the same fulfillment formula.
Relationship
The earlier passage is a genuine future-oriented royal oracle, later understood christologically.
Historical evidence & limits
Micah contains a forward-looking Davidic royal oracle. The Bethlehem association may refer to Davidic origins as well as a locality; the two later nativity accounts do not supply independent contemporary corroboration of Jesus' precise birthplace.
Current assessment
Earlier royal oracle clear; Bethlehem birth details unverified independently
Primary text and scholarship (4)
  • Hebrew Micah 5:2
  • Matthew 2:5–6
  • TheTorah: why Bethlehem
  • Yale Matthew study guide

Zechariah 9:9–10 — The king riding a donkey

Later use/test: Matthew 21:4–7; Mark 11:1–10; John 12:14–15 · Future royal oracle

Original expression
עַל־חֲמוֹר וְעַל־עַיִר (on a donkey, and on a colt)
Earlier situation
Zion is told to welcome a humble, victorious king, contrasted with military power and associated with the end of war in the next verse.
Later use
Mark and John describe one colt/donkey; Matthew names both a mother donkey and colt while citing Zechariah. What the pronoun 'them' refers to in Matthew 21:7 is also debated.
Relationship
There are competing explanations: Matthew may have read Zechariah's poetic parallelism as two animals (Yale's account); Tucker Ferda (2020) argues Matthew combined Zechariah 9:9 with Genesis 49:10–11; Adam Kubiś (2018) defends a historically two-animal scene. Zechariah's Hebrew parallel phrases need not refer to two mounts.
Historical evidence & limits
Matthew's two-animal version is a textual fact. Its relationship to the one-animal accounts and the event itself is debated, so the Gospel quotation alone does not independently establish the exact incident.
Current assessment
Textual reuse explicit; exact event details not independently established
Primary text and scholarship (5)
  • Hebrew Zechariah 9:9
  • Matthew 21:4–7
  • Yale on Matthew 21
  • Tucker S. Ferda (2020), Journal of Theological Studies
  • Adam Kubiś (2018), Biblical Annals

Isaiah 52:13–53:12 — The suffering servant

Later use/test: Matthew 8:17; Acts 8:32–35; 1 Peter 2:22–25 · Servant poem later identified with Jesus

Original expression
עַבְדִּי (my servant; Isaiah 52:13)
Earlier situation
The servant poem belongs to Isaiah 40–55's exile-and-restoration setting. Isaiah 49:3 explicitly calls the servant Israel, but Isaiah 49:5 also describes a servant commissioned to restore Israel. This complicates a simple identification; views include collective Israel, an individual representative, or an idealized servant.
Later use
New Testament texts apply servant phrases to Jesus' healings, suffering, death, and perceived redemptive role.
Relationship
Later interpretation identifies the servant with Jesus; interpretations of the original figure include collective Israel and individual or idealized servants.
Historical evidence & limits
New Testament identification of the servant with Jesus is explicit. Even if Jesus' execution is accepted historically, it does not independently prove that the poem originally referred to him or that its theological portrayal of suffering was an advance prediction.
Current assessment
Servant identity debated; New Testament application documented

Psalm 22 — My God, why have you forsaken me?

Later use/test: Mark 15:34; Matthew 27:46; John 19:24 · Lament psalm later reused

Original expression
כָּאֲרִי (Masoretic 'like a lion') / כארו (Naḥal Ḥever variant): Hebrew Psalm 22:17, often 22:16 in English Bibles
Earlier situation
Psalm 22 is an anguished prayer of a suffering speaker who ultimately voices praise, not a prose forecast naming a future crucifixion.
Later use
Jesus' cry and the distribution of garments are narrated with language that evokes Psalm 22; Christians also connect the debated hands-and-feet reading to crucifixion.
Relationship
The evangelists echo an earlier lament, including its opening cry and imagery. The Masoretic text has 'like a lion'; a Naḥal Ḥever fragment supports a spelling with final waw; the Greek ὤρυξαν is 'they dug'. Translating the verbal tradition as 'pierced' is interpretive and disputed.
Historical evidence & limits
Hopkin (2005) argues that the ancient fragment supports the verbal reading; Strawn (2000) stresses the lion imagery and textual difficulties; NET translation notes challenge 'pierce' as a gloss. Competing textual readings do not by themselves show that Psalm 22 originally predicted Jesus' crucifixion.
Current assessment
Ancient reading variation documented; prophetic status is interpretive
Primary text and scholarship (6)
  • Masoretic Hebrew Psalm 22:17
  • Shon D. Hopkin (2005), BYU Studies
  • Brent A. Strawn (2000), Journal of Biblical Literature
  • NET Bible notes, Psalm 22
  • Matthew 27:46
  • John 19:24

Isaiah 40:3–5 — The voice in the wilderness

Later use/test: Mark 1:2–4; Matthew 3:3; Luke 3:4–6 · Restoration oracle and punctuation

Original expression
קֹול קֹורֵא בַּמִּדְבָּר (a voice calls: in the wilderness...)
Earlier situation
Second Isaiah (Isaiah 40–55) is commonly situated in the sixth-century BCE exilic context. Hebrew poetry pairs 'in the wilderness' with 'in the desert'; the voice calls for a processional road prepared for YHWH's return to the people.
Later use
Gospel authors use the Greek textual tradition so that the voice belongs to John the Baptist in the wilderness.
Relationship
The Septuagint does not reproduce the Hebrew 'in the desert' parallel phrase in the second line, enabling 'in the wilderness' to describe the crying voice. The Gospels apply that Greek reading to John the Baptist. The shift involves the Greek version's wording as well as punctuation.
Historical evidence & limits
Hebrew parallelism and Greek translation structure support distinct construals; the New Testament application is explicit. The earlier text does not name John the Baptist.
Current assessment
Literary and translation shift documented
Primary text and scholarship (3)
  • Hebrew Isaiah 40:3
  • Greek Mark 1:3
  • David M. Freidenreich, TheTorah (2026): Hebrew and Greek compared

Jeremiah 46:13–26 — Babylon's predicted conquest of Egypt

Later use/test: Historical campaigns of Nebuchadnezzar II · Historical prediction checked against external records

Original expression
לָבוֹא נְבוּכַדְרֶאצַּר ... לְהַכּוֹת אֶת־אֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם (Nebuchadnezzar coming to strike Egypt)
Earlier situation
The oracle imagines a destructive Babylonian invasion and devastation of Egypt after Babylon's military rise.
Later use
Later interpreters may regard partial raids as fulfillment or understand the oracle as revised in light of events.
Relationship
A concrete military forecast, testable with Egyptian and Babylonian evidence and the book's editorial history.
Historical evidence & limits
Babylon fought Egypt and conducted raids; Egyptian independence continued until Persian conquest. Dan'el Kahn argues parts of the present prophetic text were revised with later Egyptian civil-war knowledge.
Current assessment
Predicted full conquest not documented; partial attacks documented; textual layers debated
Primary text and scholarship (2)
  • Hebrew Jeremiah 46:13
  • Dan'el Kahn (2022), TheTorah

Ezekiel 29:8–16; 29:17–21 — Egypt uninhabited for forty years

Later use/test: Egyptian historical record of the sixth century BCE · Time-specified judgment oracle

Original expression
אַרְבָּעִים שָׁנָה (forty years)
Earlier situation
The prophecy predicts devastation, dispersion, and lack of inhabitants for forty years, associated with Egyptian and Babylonian power.
Later use
The forty-year desolation can be read as a literal timetable or as a rounded/rhetorical period. Ezekiel 29:17–21 offers Egypt as compensation for Nebuchadnezzar's inadequate reward from his campaign against Tyre; historical conquests require outside evidence.
Relationship
Specific forecast compared to independent historical records, with attention to whether numbers are literal or rhetorical.
Historical evidence & limits
No independently verified Egyptian history supports forty years of the entire country being uninhabited. Babylonian attacks occurred, but the available record does not establish complete depopulation on the prophesied scale. Interpretive debate remains over the imagery and intended literalness.
Current assessment
Literal fulfillment unsupported by known independent record
Primary text and scholarship (3)
  • Hebrew Ezekiel 29:11
  • Ezekiel 29:17–21
  • Dan'el Kahn (2022), Egyptian and Babylonian evidence

Jonah 3:4–10; Jeremiah 18:7–10 — Forty days until Nineveh's overthrow

Later use/test: Nineveh's repentance within Jonah's story · Conditional warning, not fixed timetable

Original expression
עֹוד אַרְבָּעִים יֹום (yet forty days)
Earlier situation
The story has Jonah announce impending destruction; the city's response leads the narrator to say God does not bring the announced disaster.
Later use
Sometimes claimed as failed prediction or defended as a conditional warning.
Relationship
A teaching story about judgment and mercy, paired with Jeremiah's explicit principle that warnings may be conditional.
Historical evidence & limits
The narrative itself says the announced disaster did not occur. It gives a reason within the story; this does not independently establish that Jonah preached to historical Nineveh or that its repentance happened.
Current assessment
Non-occurrence and conditional logic explicit within the narrative
Primary text and scholarship (3)
  • Hebrew Jonah 3:4
  • Jonah 3:10
  • Jeremiah 18:7–10

No surviving identifiable scriptural quotation — He will be called a Nazarene

Later use/test: Matthew 2:23 · Fulfillment formula with no identifiable antecedent · New Batch 2 research

Original expression
Ναζωραῖος κληθήσεται (Greek Matthew 2:23, 'he will be called a Nazarene'); no corresponding Hebrew prophecy text is known
Earlier situation
Matthew refers to what 'the prophets' said in the plural, but no extant verse in the Hebrew Bible makes this exact statement. Proposed connections include the Hebrew 'branch' (netser) in Isaiah 11:1, the Nazirite tradition, or a broader motif of social contempt; each has lexical or contextual obstacles.
Later use
Matthew says Joseph's family settled in Nazareth so that the words spoken through the prophets might be fulfilled.
Relationship
A fulfillment claim without a securely identifiable prior passage. An association with an older scriptural theme is plausible, but reconstructing an exact lost quotation or the author's motive exceeds the evidence.
Historical evidence & limits
Matthew 2:23 is verifiable. The lack of an extant matching oracle prevents word-for-word comparison or independent confirmation that 'Nazarene' was predicted; proposed Hebrew wordplays remain hypotheses.
Current assessment
Fulfillment claim explicit; original quoted source unidentified
Primary text and scholarship (3)
  • Matthew 2:23 with NET translation notes
  • Matthew 2:23 Greek text
  • Isaiah 11:1 (netser) comparison

Isaiah 8:23–9:1 (Hebrew numbering; English Isaiah 9:1–2) — Great light in Galilee

Later use/test: Matthew 4:14–16 · Restoration oracle applied to Jesus' Galilean ministry · New Batch 2 research

Original expression
הָעָם הַהֹלְכִים בַּחֹשֶׁךְ רָאוּ אוֹר גָּדוֹל ('people walking in darkness saw a great light')
Earlier situation
The Isaiah passage names the territories of Zebulun and Naphtali in northern Israel after devastation associated with imperial warfare in the eighth century BCE; its original horizon is hope after crisis. Its precise literary layering and royal expectation are debated.
Later use
Matthew quotes the passage after Jesus relocates to Capernaum and begins his ministry in Galilee, identifying his activity with the light reaching the north.
Relationship
The application is explicit; Matthew adapts the geographical and metaphorical imagery to Jesus. The earlier oracle does not name Jesus or specify a first-century Capernaum ministry.
Historical evidence & limits
The Hebrew text, its historical north-Israel setting, and the textual correspondence with Matthew are checkable. Matthew's report of the ministry cannot be treated as independent proof of supernatural fulfillment simply because Matthew supplies the citation.
Current assessment
Earlier northern-restoration imagery and later application documented; supernatural forecast unverified
Primary text and scholarship (3)
  • Isaiah 9:1–2 and Matthew 4:14–16
  • Hebrew Isaiah 9:2
  • Richard Beaton, Isaiah's Christ in Matthew's Gospel (2002), research program

Isaiah 53:4 — He took our infirmities

Later use/test: Matthew 8:16–17 · Healing formula quotation; lexical difference across Hebrew and Greek · New Batch 2 research

Original expression
חֳלָיֵנוּ הוּא נָשָׂא וּמַכְאֹבֵינוּ סְבָלָם ('our sicknesses he bore, our pains he carried')
Earlier situation
Isaiah's fourth servant poem depicts a sufferer bearing illness and pain, interpreted within the larger exile-restoration discourse. The ancient Greek version renders a related clause using 'sins', making the textual tradition crucial.
Later use
Matthew quotes Isaiah 53:4 after reporting Jesus' healings and exorcisms, drawing attention to physical sickness in the wording; this is a narrower use than later applications to Jesus' death.
Relationship
A particular use of Isaiah 53 distinct from the broader servant-identity case already in this audit (isaiah-53). Matthew's phrasing is closer to the Hebrew's physical-illness sense than the old Greek version's 'sins'. Rikk Watts (2015) connects it to Matthew's wider theme of ending Israel's afflictions.
Historical evidence & limits
The verbal source and Hebrew–Septuagint difference are independently inspectable. Matthew's literary explanation for healings does not independently establish that the reported miracles occurred, nor prove an original forecast of Jesus.
Current assessment
Explicit citation and identifiable translation choice; specific theological significance debated
Primary text and scholarship (4)
  • Isaiah 53:4 / Matthew 8:17 parallel
  • Hebrew Isaiah 53:4
  • Greek Septuagint comparison
  • Rikk Watts (2015), Journal for the Study of the New Testament

Isaiah 42:1–4 — The quiet servant bringing justice

Later use/test: Matthew 12:15–21 · Servant-justice oracle applied to Jesus · New Batch 2 research

Original expression
הֵן עַבְדִּי ... מִשְׁפָּט לַגּוֹיִם יוֹצִיא ('Here is my servant ... he will bring justice to the nations')
Earlier situation
Isaiah's servant is characterized as chosen and Spirit-supported, bringing justice without destructive force; the servant's identity across Isaiah 40–55 is debated, and the text belongs to an exile/restoration horizon.
Later use
Matthew quotes a lengthy adapted version to interpret Jesus' healing and instruction, including his restraint and the prospect of justice or hope among the nations.
Relationship
Explicit later identification with Jesus. The Greek wording used in Matthew differs in places from both the standard Hebrew and the old Greek Isaiah text; it is an interpretive quotation rather than an exact unedited reproduction.
Historical evidence & limits
The passage's servant/justice language exists earlier, and Matthew's theological application is documented. Richard Beaton (2000) argues that the servant's justice is integral to Matthew's compassionate portrayal of Jesus; the original identity and fulfillment standard are debatable.
Current assessment
Text-to-text use direct; earlier servant identity and fulfillment interpretation disputed
Primary text and scholarship (4)
  • Hebrew Isaiah 42:1–4
  • Matthew 12:17–21 and Isaiah 42:1–4
  • Richard Beaton (2000), Matthew's use of Isaiah 42
  • Richard Beaton, Isaiah's Christ in Matthew's Gospel (2002)

Psalm 78:1–8 (especially 78:2) — I will open my mouth in parables

Later use/test: Matthew 13:34–35 · Historical teaching psalm described as fulfilled · New Batch 2 research

Original expression
אֶפְתְּחָה בְמָשָׁל פִּי ('I will open my mouth with a parable')
Earlier situation
Psalm 78, associated by its superscription with Asaph, introduces a poetic instruction that retells Israel's past and teaches coming generations. In its own context it describes the psalmist's mode of teaching rather than a named future person's preaching.
Later use
Matthew cites the line to explain Jesus' choice to teach in parables. Matthew's longer wording mentions what was hidden since the foundation of the world, whereas Hebrew Psalm 78:2 speaks about things 'from of old'.
Relationship
A later literary application of ancient wisdom and teaching language, not an unambiguous predictive oracle. Some Greek manuscripts name Isaiah as the 'prophet' in Matthew 13:35; most do not. The source remains Psalm 78:2.
Historical evidence & limits
The older psalm and Matthew's citation are directly comparable; the choice of literary quotation does not independently prove historicity of every speech of Jesus. NET and NABRE textual notes document the rare Matthew 'Isaiah' variant.
Current assessment
Earlier retrospective instruction and later reuse established; minor NT textual variant noted
Primary text and scholarship (4)
  • Psalm 78:2 / Matthew 13:35 parallel
  • Hebrew Psalm 78:2
  • NET textual note Matthew 13:35
  • Yale Bible Study on Matthew's parables

Zechariah 11:12–13 (and debated allusions to Jeremiah 18–19; 32) — Thirty silver pieces attributed to Jeremiah

Later use/test: Matthew 26:14–16; 27:3–10 · Composite attribution and redaction of a shepherd oracle · New Batch 2 research

Original expression
שְׁלֹשִׁים כָּסֶף ('thirty [pieces of] silver', Zechariah 11:12); Matthew says 'through Jeremiah'
Earlier situation
Zechariah 11 presents a symbolic shepherd drama about the rejection of a shepherd and the contemptuous thirty-silver payment. It mentions disposing of the money in the house of YHWH, but not the purchase of a potter's field.
Later use
Matthew's Judas narrative specifies thirty silver pieces, money thrown into the temple, and the purchase of a potter's field; Matthew 27:9 calls the quotation Jeremiah although its closest wording is Zechariah 11:12–13.
Relationship
The discrepancy is explicit. Some scholars posit composite imagery from Jeremiah 19 or 32; Maarten J. J. Menken (2002) argues Zechariah 11:13 is the main source and Matthew's redaction explains the altered wording and selected narrative details.
Historical evidence & limits
The source attribution in Matthew is demonstrably different from the best matching earlier text. A correction based solely on Matthew's own claim that Judas acted this way would be circular; the earlier source independently exists, but the thirty-coin event is not independently verified by that correspondence.
Current assessment
Zechariah source and Jeremiah attribution verifiable; precise cause of misattribution debated
Primary text and scholarship (4)
  • NRSVUE Zechariah 11:12–13 vs Matthew 27:9–10
  • Zechariah 11:12 Hebrew
  • Maarten J.J. Menken (2002), Biblica 83
  • Jeremiah 32:6–9 for comparison

Isaiah 61:1–2; 58:6 (Greek textual combination) — Good news, liberation, and the omitted day of vengeance

Later use/test: Luke 4:16–21 · Fulfillment declaration combining Hebrew Bible passages through Greek wording · New Batch 3 research; independent specialist review pending

Original expression
רוּחַ אֲדֹנָי יְהוִה עָלָי (Spirit of the Lord YHWH is upon me); Luke draws on Greek Isaiah 61 and 58
Earlier situation
Isaiah 61 addresses the restoration community with news of liberation, comfort, and reversal; 61:2 includes both a favorable year and a day of divine vengeance. Isaiah 58:6 speaks of release from oppression in a critique of unjust fasting.
Later use
Luke depicts Jesus reading an Isaianic scroll in Nazareth and declaring 'Today this scripture has been fulfilled'. Luke's Greek passage features sight for the blind from the Old Greek, adds a release line from Isaiah 58:6, and stops before the day-of-vengeance clause.
Relationship
Explicit fulfillment claim with a composite quotation, selection and omission. Timothy Michael Law (2013) identifies the Septuagint sources; scholars debate whether omitting vengeance signals Luke's editorial theological emphasis.
Historical evidence & limits
The differences between Isaiah's Hebrew, Old Greek, and Luke's quotation are directly visible. The narrated synagogue encounter establishes Luke's literary depiction, not independently verified firsthand testimony about a historical speech.
Current assessment
Quote and editorial changes directly checkable; historical speech uncorroborated
Primary text and scholarship (4)
  • Isaiah 61:1–2 in NRSVUE
  • Luke 4:18–21 in NRSVUE
  • Greek and Hebrew comparison (with Timothy Michael Law 2013)
  • Peer-reviewed analysis, HTS Teologiese Studies (2018)

Joel 2:28–32 (Hebrew 3:1–5) — Afterward becomes in the last days

Later use/test: Acts 2:16–21 · Future Spirit oracle reread as Pentecost fulfillment · New Batch 3 research; independent specialist review pending

Original expression
וְהָיָה אַחֲרֵי־כֵן ('and it shall be afterward')
Earlier situation
Joel anticipates restoration and God's Spirit poured upon people of differing ages and genders after the communal crisis described in the book; the vision also includes cosmic signs and judgment.
Later use
Acts' Peter says the Pentecost events are what Joel spoke about. Acts 2:17 introduces Joel's prophecy as occurring 'in the last days' rather than the Hebrew passage's simpler 'afterward'.
Relationship
Explicit prophetic identification and interpretive temporal framing. The addition 'in the last days' matters for how the narrator places Pentecost inside eschatological history.
Historical evidence & limits
The textual wording and narrative use are independently checkable. Acts is a later report of the event being interpreted; it does not alone provide an independent verification of miraculous Spirit outpouring or the completion of cosmic signs.
Current assessment
Original/Acts time-language difference confirmed; supernatural fulfillment a faith claim
Primary text and scholarship (3)

  • Hebrew Joel 2:28
  • Acts 2:16–21 context

Amos 9:11–12 — Edom possessed or humankind seeking God?

Later use/test: Acts 15:15–19 · Greek translation changes the actors in a restoration oracle · New Batch 3 research; independent specialist review pending

Original expression
לְמַעַן יִירְשׁוּ אֶת־שְׁאֵרִית אֱדוֹם (so they may possess the remnant of Edom)
Earlier situation
The closing oracle of Amos envisions restoration of David's fallen structure and renewed relationships with Edom and the nations. Its exact dating, possible editorial growth, and whether it envisages conquest or broader inclusion are discussed in scholarship.
Later use
Acts presents James citing a Greek form in which 'the rest of humankind' seek the Lord, supporting the inclusion of Gentiles without requiring them to become Jews.
Relationship
The Septuagint has 'seek' and 'humankind' in place of Hebrew 'possess' and 'Edom'. These are major grammatical and semantic changes, often explained through similar Hebrew letters and alternate vocalization; however, the phrase 'all the nations who are called by my name' is shared, so inclusion does not depend solely on the new Greek subjects.
Historical evidence & limits
Hebrew Amos, Old Greek, and Acts 15 can be compared. James D. Nogalski (2015) traces three stages of the text's interpretation. The Acts account itself is not independent proof the earlier author foresaw a specific first-century council.
Current assessment
Substantial source-language difference documented; exact translation-history mechanism debated
Primary text and scholarship (4)
  • Amos 9:11–12 Hebrew/English
  • Acts 15:16–18 text
  • James D. Nogalski (2015), Three Faces of Hope
  • Septuagint/Hebrew details as secondary comparative source

Zechariah 12:10–14 — Look on the one they pierced

Later use/test: John 19:34–37; Revelation 1:7 · Piercing oracle and divergent Greek/Hebrew wording · New Batch 3 research; independent specialist review pending

Original expression
וְהִבִּיטוּ אֵלַי אֵת אֲשֶׁר־דָּקָרוּ ('they will look toward me whom they pierced' in the Masoretic Text)
Earlier situation
In Zechariah 12, the house of David and inhabitants of Jerusalem are part of a future conflict, divine deliverance and communal mourning. The identity of the pierced figure and the relationship between first- and third-person phrasing are debated.
Later use
John quotes the oracle immediately after describing a soldier piercing Jesus' side; Revelation 1:7 echoes it when describing Jesus' appearance.
Relationship
The verb in John's quote is closer to Hebrew 'pierced' than the ancient Greek Zechariah's markedly different expression. John's Greek also frames the object in third person while the Masoretic text includes 'to me'. Whether John translated Hebrew directly or used an available Hebraizing Greek form is unproven.
Historical evidence & limits
The verbal parallels and variants are checkable. Christian and Jewish readings identify the subject differently. An independently accepted execution by crucifixion does not establish that the original Zechariah oracle specifically foretold John's narrative detail.
Current assessment
Original and later wording documented; intended historical identity and textual route disputed
Primary text and scholarship (4)
  • Hebrew Zechariah 12:10
  • John 19:37
  • Adam Kubiś on Zechariah in John (2012)
  • Jewish interpretive perspective

Jeremiah 31:31–34 — A new covenant for Israel and Judah

Later use/test: Hebrews 8:8–13 · New covenant promise applied to later Christian religious community · New Batch 3 research; independent specialist review pending

Original expression
וְכָרַתִּי ... בְּרִית חֲדָשָׁה ('I will make ... a new covenant')
Earlier situation
Jeremiah promises the houses of Israel and Judah a renewed covenant, in which divine teaching or Torah is internalized and sins are forgiven, as part of a restoration sequence after exile.
Later use
Hebrews quotes Jeremiah at length to argue that a new priestly or covenantal order has become available through Jesus; Hebrews 8:13 says the earlier order is becoming old and approaching disappearance.
Relationship
Explicit reuse and reinterpretation. Scholars disagree about how far Hebrews' language extends to replacing Judaism or Israel's covenant; Nicholas J. Moore (2024) argues the immediate argument centers on cultic aspects and warns against translating its Greek as a blanket denunciation of Judaism.
Historical evidence & limits
The shared wording is observable. The earlier text specifically names Israel and Judah; it does not name Jesus. Religious conclusions about superseding Judaism depend on later interpretation, and there is no independent historical observation that can confirm a divine covenant transfer.
Current assessment
Quotation documented; theological and supersessionist readings disputed
Primary text and scholarship (4)
  • Jeremiah 31:31–34 NRSVUE
  • Hebrews 8:8–13 NRSVUE
  • Nicholas J. Moore (2024), Supersessionism and the Cult Attitude of Stephen and Hebrews
  • Ted Goshorn (2016), Emory thesis on non-supersessionist covenant readings

Isaiah 6:9–10 — Hearing but not understanding

Later use/test: Matthew 13:13–15; Mark 4:12; Luke 8:10; John 12:39–40; Acts 28:26–27 · Judgment-commission text reused to explain response to Jesus · New Batch 4 research; independent review pending

Original expression
הַשְׁמֵן לֵב־הָעָם הַזֶּה (Hebrew Isaiah 6:10: make the heart of this people dull/fat); Greek: ἐπαχύνθη γὰρ ἡ καρδία (their heart has grown dull)
Earlier situation
Isaiah's call narrative commissions him to address a people who will fail to understand, immediately before an oracle about destruction and a remnant. The Hebrew imperative can depict divine causation in hardening; the ancient Greek wording instead describes their hearts as having become dull and their own closing of eyes.
Later use
Matthew quotes the Greek form to interpret why some people do not understand Jesus' parables; Acts 28:26–27 also follows the Greek. John 12:40 instead uses active hardening language closer to the Hebrew agency. Mark 4:12 and Luke 8:10 employ shorter versions.
Relationship
A pre-existing commissioning/judgment passage is applied to later audiences. The original verses are not a named future prediction of first-century parable teaching; distinct biblical authors use different textual forms with meaningful changes in emphasis.
Historical evidence & limits
The Hebrew and Greek versions and New Testament reuse can be compared directly. The International Critical Commentary notes Matthew's reliance on the Greek quotation and its relationship to Mark 4:12. Variants in divine/reader agency do not, by themselves, settle doctrines of predestination.
Current assessment
Direct biblical quotations verifiable; original referent distinct from later application
Primary text and scholarship (4)

  • NET Bible notes on Matthew 13:14–15
  • International Critical Commentary on Matthew 13
  • John 12:40 Greek text

Zechariah 13:7–9 — Strike the shepherd, and the sheep will scatter

Later use/test: Mark 14:27; Matthew 26:31 and 26:56; John 16:32 (allusion) · Shepherd oracle applied to Jesus' arrest and disciples' flight · New Batch 4 research; independent review pending

Original expression
הַךְ אֶת־הָרֹעֶה וּתְפוּצֶיןָ הַצֹּאן (Hebrew imperative: strike the shepherd and let the sheep be scattered)
Earlier situation
Zechariah 13 describes purification and judgment for the people. God's sword is called against a shepherd called God's associate; the people are then divided and tested. The shepherd's original identity and whether the figure is positive or negative are disputed.
Later use
In Mark 14:27 and Matthew 26:31 Jesus quotes the line to describe the disciples' desertion. Matthew changes the Greek wording to 'I will strike the shepherd' and adds 'of the flock.' Matthew 26:56 then narrates that the disciples fled.
Relationship
A late prophetic shepherd oracle is explicitly applied to Jesus and the disciples, with an important shift from the Hebrew imperative to the first-person future 'I will strike'. Matthew's form is related to Mark's. Menken (2011) analyzes the earliest Greek and Hebrew forms and the sequence of Christian reception.
Historical evidence & limits
The textual forms, including imperatives and future speech, are directly available. Matthew's narration of flight is not independent verification that the ancient oracle was a prediction about Jesus or that events occurred exactly as portrayed.
Current assessment
Quotational reuse explicit; original shepherd identity and historical fulfillment disputed
Primary text and scholarship (4)
  • Hebrew Zechariah 13:7
  • Matthew 26:31 and 26:56
  • Maarten J. J. Menken (2011), Biblica 92, pp. 39–59
  • The Use and Reception of the Prophets in the New Testament (2022)

No single earlier scripture identified in Matthew 26:54, 56 — All this to fulfill the scriptures of the prophets

Later use/test: Matthew 26:54, 56 · Generic multiple-scripture fulfillment assertion · New Batch 4 research; independent review pending

Original expression
πληρωθῶσιν αἱ γραφαί (Matthew 26:54: that the Scriptures might be fulfilled); αἱ γραφαὶ τῶν προφητῶν (26:56: writings of the prophets)
Earlier situation
Unlike a direct quotation, these sentences do not provide a new verse-by-verse prophetic original. Wider passion themes are often connected to Isaiah 53, Psalm 22, and Zechariah 13:7; Matthew 26:31 has already made the latter connection explicit.
Later use
Matthew has Jesus present his arrest as necessary for fulfillment in 26:54; a narrative remark in 26:56 also attributes events to the prophets' writings. The scene then says the disciples fled.
Relationship
General fulfillment rationale attached to a constructed literary sequence, with a specific nearby source in Zechariah 13:7 but without a separately identifiable quotation in 26:54/56. Do not invent a single Hebrew original for the plural phrase.
Historical evidence & limits
The Greek reference to scriptures is explicit. Proposed antecedents are interpretive, and the narrator's fulfillment label does not independently confirm arrest details. Link to the separate Zechariah 13:7 record for the specific shepherd citation.
Current assessment
Generic scriptural claim documented; exact set of earlier passages unspecified
Primary text and scholarship (3)
  • Matthew 26:54–56 in parallel translations
  • Matthew 26:31 and Zechariah 13:7 parallel
  • ESV cross references for unspecified fulfillment claims
