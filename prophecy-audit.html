How to test a prophecy claim

Not every passage reused by a later author was originally written as a future prediction. Compare the earlier context, claimed fulfillment, independent evidence, and real disagreement before judging a claim.

  1. Check the earlier Hebrew, Aramaic, or Greek and relevant textual variants.
  2. Identify the original audience, date, situation, literary genre, and whether it was a forecast, warning, or retrospective story.
  3. Find the later quotation and compare what changed in wording or referent.
  4. Check independent contemporary evidence rather than treating a later fulfillment narrative as independent proof.
  5. Consider documented Jewish, Christian, and historical-critical interpretations.
  6. Record separately what the original text means, what later authors claimed, and what history supports.

A prophecy of judgment may be conditional: Deuteronomy 18:22, Jeremiah 18:7–10 and Jonah 3 must be read together.

Isaiah 7:10–17 — The young woman and Immanuel

Later use/test: Matthew 1:22–23 · Contemporary sign later reapplied

Original expression
הָעַלְמָה (ha-‘almah; the young woman)
Earlier situation
Isaiah addresses King Ahaz during a military crisis in Judah; the child functions as a sign within a near-term political timetable (see 7:15–16).
Later use
Matthew quotes the Greek-scripture form using parthenos and applies it to Mary's conception of Jesus.
Relationship
Retrospective messianic rereading, with a Hebrew-to-Greek lexical change.
Historical evidence & limits
The connection is explicit in Matthew. The Hebrew noun alone does not require a miraculous conception; the supernatural claim is part of Matthew's narrative and not independently established.
Current assessment
Original-context reading strong; historical miracle unverified

Hosea 11:1–4 — Out of Egypt I called my son

Later use/test: Matthew 2:14–15 · Past narrative later reapplied

Original expression
כִּי נַעַר יִשְׂרָאֵל (when Israel was a boy)
Earlier situation
Hosea recalls the historical Exodus story and addresses Israel as God's wayward child; its verbs point back to a past relationship.
Later use
Matthew cites Hosea 11:1 immediately after narrating the family's flight into Egypt (Matthew 2:14–15), although their return from Egypt is narrated later (2:19–21).
Relationship
Matthew identifies Jesus with Israel by typology. Seong-Kwang Kevin Kim (2019) notes that the quotation about coming out is placed before Matthew narrates the return and offers a larger Israel/Egypt pattern to explain the application.
Historical evidence & limits
Hosea describes Israel's past Exodus. Matthew's quotation and retelling are explicit but do not independently confirm the family’s travel. The timing of the quotation within Matthew is a real interpretive issue.
Current assessment
Earlier reference to Israel explicit; later typological use explicit

Jeremiah 31:15–17 — Rachel weeping for her children

Later use/test: Matthew 2:16–18 · Exile lament later reapplied

Original expression
רָחֵל מְבַכָּה (Rachel is weeping)
Earlier situation
Rachel personifies grief for Israelite exile; the very next lines promise that the children will return from enemy territory.
Later use
Matthew quotes the lament when narrating Herod's killing of Bethlehem's boys.
Relationship
A poetic lament in an exile-restoration context is given a new narrative referent.
Historical evidence & limits
The reuse is explicit and the original exile context can be verified. Matthew's particular massacre narrative is not independently established by this scriptural quotation.
Current assessment
Different historical referents documented; event evidence limited

Micah 5:2–5 (English numbering) — A ruler from Bethlehem

Later use/test: Matthew 2:5–6; Luke 2:4–7 · Future royal oracle

Original expression
בֵּית־לֶחֶם אֶפְרָתָה (Bethlehem Ephrathah)
Earlier situation
Micah looks toward a ruler emerging from David's ancestral setting amid political insecurity, with hopes of peace and protection for Israel.
Later use
Matthew explicitly quotes Micah as identifying the Messiah's Bethlehem connection; Luke also places the birth in Bethlehem but supplies a different journey narrative and does not use the same fulfillment formula.
Relationship
The earlier passage is a genuine future-oriented royal oracle, later understood christologically.
Historical evidence & limits
Micah contains a forward-looking Davidic royal oracle. The Bethlehem association may refer to Davidic origins as well as a locality; the two later nativity accounts do not supply independent contemporary corroboration of Jesus' precise birthplace.
Current assessment
Earlier royal oracle clear; Bethlehem birth details unverified independently

Zechariah 9:9–10 — The king riding a donkey

Later use/test: Matthew 21:4–7; Mark 11:1–10; John 12:14–15 · Future royal oracle

Original expression
עַל־חֲמוֹר וְעַל־עַיִר (on a donkey, and on a colt)
Earlier situation
Zion is told to welcome a humble, victorious king, contrasted with military power and associated with the end of war in the next verse.
Later use
Mark and John describe one colt/donkey; Matthew names both a mother donkey and colt while citing Zechariah. What the pronoun 'them' refers to in Matthew 21:7 is also debated.
Relationship
There are competing explanations: Matthew may have read Zechariah's poetic parallelism as two animals (Yale's account); Tucker Ferda (2020) argues Matthew combined Zechariah 9:9 with Genesis 49:10–11; Adam Kubiś (2018) defends a historically two-animal scene. Zechariah's Hebrew parallel phrases need not refer to two mounts.
Historical evidence & limits
Matthew's two-animal version is a textual fact. Its relationship to the one-animal accounts and the event itself is debated, so the Gospel quotation alone does not independently establish the exact incident.
Current assessment
Textual reuse explicit; exact event details not independently established

Isaiah 52:13–53:12 — The suffering servant

Later use/test: Matthew 8:17; Acts 8:32–35; 1 Peter 2:22–25 · Servant poem later identified with Jesus

Original expression
עַבְדִּי (my servant; Isaiah 52:13)
Earlier situation
The servant poem belongs to Isaiah 40–55's exile-and-restoration setting. Isaiah 49:3 explicitly calls the servant Israel, but Isaiah 49:5 also describes a servant commissioned to restore Israel. This complicates a simple identification; views include collective Israel, an individual representative, or an idealized servant.
Later use
New Testament texts apply servant phrases to Jesus' healings, suffering, death, and perceived redemptive role.
Relationship
Later interpretation identifies the servant with Jesus; interpretations of the original figure include collective Israel and individual or idealized servants.
Historical evidence & limits
New Testament identification of the servant with Jesus is explicit. Even if Jesus' execution is accepted historically, it does not independently prove that the poem originally referred to him or that its theological portrayal of suffering was an advance prediction.
Current assessment
Servant identity debated; New Testament application documented

Psalm 22 — My God, why have you forsaken me?

Later use/test: Mark 15:34; Matthew 27:46; John 19:24 · Lament psalm later reused

Original expression
כָּאֲרִי (Masoretic 'like a lion') / כארו (Naḥal Ḥever variant): Hebrew Psalm 22:17, often 22:16 in English Bibles
Earlier situation
Psalm 22 is an anguished prayer of a suffering speaker who ultimately voices praise, not a prose forecast naming a future crucifixion.
Later use
Jesus' cry and the distribution of garments are narrated with language that evokes Psalm 22; Christians also connect the debated hands-and-feet reading to crucifixion.
Relationship
The evangelists echo an earlier lament, including its opening cry and imagery. The Masoretic text has 'like a lion'; a Naḥal Ḥever fragment supports a spelling with final waw; the Greek ὤρυξαν is 'they dug'. Translating the verbal tradition as 'pierced' is interpretive and disputed.
Historical evidence & limits
Hopkin (2005) argues that the ancient fragment supports the verbal reading; Strawn (2000) stresses the lion imagery and textual difficulties; NET translation notes challenge 'pierce' as a gloss. Competing textual readings do not by themselves show that Psalm 22 originally predicted Jesus' crucifixion.
Current assessment
Ancient reading variation documented; prophetic status is interpretive

Isaiah 40:3–5 — The voice in the wilderness

Later use/test: Mark 1:2–4; Matthew 3:3; Luke 3:4–6 · Restoration oracle and punctuation

Original expression
קֹול קֹורֵא בַּמִּדְבָּר (a voice calls: in the wilderness...)
Earlier situation
Second Isaiah (Isaiah 40–55) is commonly situated in the sixth-century BCE exilic context. Hebrew poetry pairs 'in the wilderness' with 'in the desert'; the voice calls for a processional road prepared for YHWH's return to the people.
Later use
Gospel authors use the Greek textual tradition so that the voice belongs to John the Baptist in the wilderness.
Relationship
The Septuagint does not reproduce the Hebrew 'in the desert' parallel phrase in the second line, enabling 'in the wilderness' to describe the crying voice. The Gospels apply that Greek reading to John the Baptist. The shift involves the Greek version's wording as well as punctuation.
Historical evidence & limits
Hebrew parallelism and Greek translation structure support distinct construals; the New Testament application is explicit. The earlier text does not name John the Baptist.
Current assessment
Literary and translation shift documented

Jeremiah 46:13–26 — Babylon's predicted conquest of Egypt

Later use/test: Historical campaigns of Nebuchadnezzar II · Historical prediction checked against external records

Original expression
לָבוֹא נְבוּכַדְרֶאצַּר ... לְהַכּוֹת אֶת־אֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם (Nebuchadnezzar coming to strike Egypt)
Earlier situation
The oracle imagines a destructive Babylonian invasion and devastation of Egypt after Babylon's military rise.
Later use
Later interpreters may regard partial raids as fulfillment or understand the oracle as revised in light of events.
Relationship
A concrete military forecast, testable with Egyptian and Babylonian evidence and the book's editorial history.
Historical evidence & limits
Babylon fought Egypt and conducted raids; Egyptian independence continued until Persian conquest. Dan'el Kahn argues parts of the present prophetic text were revised with later Egyptian civil-war knowledge.
Current assessment
Predicted full conquest not documented; partial attacks documented; textual layers debated

Ezekiel 29:8–16; 29:17–21 — Egypt uninhabited for forty years

Later use/test: Egyptian historical record of the sixth century BCE · Time-specified judgment oracle

Original expression
אַרְבָּעִים שָׁנָה (forty years)
Earlier situation
The prophecy predicts devastation, dispersion, and lack of inhabitants for forty years, associated with Egyptian and Babylonian power.
Later use
The forty-year desolation can be read as a literal timetable or as a rounded/rhetorical period. Ezekiel 29:17–21 offers Egypt as compensation for Nebuchadnezzar's inadequate reward from his campaign against Tyre; historical conquests require outside evidence.
Relationship
Specific forecast compared to independent historical records, with attention to whether numbers are literal or rhetorical.
Historical evidence & limits
No independently verified Egyptian history supports forty years of the entire country being uninhabited. Babylonian attacks occurred, but the available record does not establish complete depopulation on the prophesied scale. Interpretive debate remains over the imagery and intended literalness.
Current assessment
Literal fulfillment unsupported by known independent record

Jonah 3:4–10; Jeremiah 18:7–10 — Forty days until Nineveh's overthrow

Later use/test: Nineveh's repentance within Jonah's story · Conditional warning, not fixed timetable

Original expression
עֹוד אַרְבָּעִים יֹום (yet forty days)
Earlier situation
The story has Jonah announce impending destruction; the city's response leads the narrator to say God does not bring the announced disaster.
Later use
Sometimes claimed as failed prediction or defended as a conditional warning.
Relationship
A teaching story about judgment and mercy, paired with Jeremiah's explicit principle that warnings may be conditional.
Historical evidence & limits
The narrative itself says the announced disaster did not occur. It gives a reason within the story; this does not independently establish that Jonah preached to historical Nineveh or that its repentance happened.
Current assessment
Non-occurrence and conditional logic explicit within the narrative

No surviving identifiable scriptural quotation — He will be called a Nazarene

Later use/test: Matthew 2:23 · Fulfillment formula with no identifiable antecedent

Original expression
Ναζωραῖος κληθήσεται (Greek Matthew 2:23, 'he will be called a Nazarene'); no corresponding Hebrew prophecy text is known
Earlier situation
Matthew refers to what 'the prophets' said in the plural, but no extant verse in the Hebrew Bible makes this exact statement. Proposed connections include the Hebrew 'branch' (netser) in Isaiah 11:1, the Nazirite tradition, or a broader motif of social contempt; each has lexical or contextual obstacles.
Later use
Matthew says Joseph's family settled in Nazareth so that the words spoken through the prophets might be fulfilled.
Relationship
A fulfillment claim without a securely identifiable prior passage. An association with an older scriptural theme is plausible, but reconstructing an exact lost quotation or the author's motive exceeds the evidence.
Historical evidence & limits
Matthew 2:23 is verifiable. The lack of an extant matching oracle prevents word-for-word comparison or independent confirmation that 'Nazarene' was predicted; proposed Hebrew wordplays remain hypotheses.
Current assessment
Fulfillment claim explicit; original quoted source unidentified

Isaiah 8:23–9:1 (Hebrew numbering; English Isaiah 9:1–2) — Great light in Galilee

Later use/test: Matthew 4:14–16 · Restoration oracle applied to Jesus' Galilean ministry

Original expression
הָעָם הַהֹלְכִים בַּחֹשֶׁךְ רָאוּ אוֹר גָּדוֹל ('people walking in darkness saw a great light')
Earlier situation
The Isaiah passage names the territories of Zebulun and Naphtali in northern Israel after devastation associated with imperial warfare in the eighth century BCE; its original horizon is hope after crisis. Its precise literary layering and royal expectation are debated.
Later use
Matthew quotes the passage after Jesus relocates to Capernaum and begins his ministry in Galilee, identifying his activity with the light reaching the north.
Relationship

Earlier situation
Isaiah 40–55 addresses an audience for whom exile and Babylonian domination are live concerns and Cyrus is an agent of deliverance. The British Museum and Getty independently date Cyrus's capture of Babylon to 539 BCE. Original expression
וְלֹא תִבָּנֶה עוֹד ('you shall not be rebuilt again', Ezekiel 26:14)
Earlier situation
An oracle announces waves of nations against Tyre, a major Phoenician port with mainland and island settlements. It names Nebuchadnezzar in 26:7–11, predicts desolation, and states Tyre will not be rebuilt. Ezekiel 29:18 later acknowledges Nebuchadnezzar's labor against Tyre without receiving an adequate reward.
Later use
Nebuchadnezzar did campaign against Tyre; the precise outcome and duration are debated. Alexander captured the island city in 332 BCE. Some apologetic readings distribute 26:3–6 to many nations and 26:7–11 to Babylon, then appeal to Alexander for a later phase.
Relationship
Test who is the predicted agent in each verse; separate mainland from island and the history of an ancient urban site from the continuous modern city. A later conqueror does not retroactively satisfy every explicit Nebuchadnezzar clause. Ezekiel's own later statement in 29:18 is part of the primary textual evidence.
Historical evidence & limits
A 2024 Protestant Theological University (PThU) Bible-blog discussion, whose author is not named in the retrieved record, argues that Ezekiel 26's envisioned outcome was not fully realized under Nebuchadnezzar and that appealing to Alexander changes the claim. Kris J. Udd (Tyndale Bulletin 56.1, 2005) likewise argues the oracle was not fulfilled as predicted, noting that Ezekiel 29:17–20 itself concedes Nebuchadnezzar's failure to take Tyre. Conservative archaeological defense disputes this and distinguishes land from island. The extant city and later occupations are incompatible with a literal claim that the entire location was never again inhabited; the exact sense of 'never rebuilt' and the original oracle's redaction are debated.
Current assessment
Attack and later conquest historically attested; complete literal permanent destruction not established

The Fall of Nineveh Chronicle (BM 21901, ABC 3) describes the Babylonians and Medes besieging and capturing Nineveh in 612 BCE. This is external evidence for the outcome, rather than for the date of Nahum's oracle.

Original expression

Earlier situation
Isaiah 17:1–3 couples the end of Damascus's kingship with weakness in Ephraim/Israel, fitting the Syro-Ephraimite alliance and war of 735–732 BCE. Joseph Blenkinsopp's critical commentary in First Isaiah and the Cambridge exposition link these clauses to that crisis; literary units elsewhere in chapter 17 may have different horizons.
Later use
Tiglath-pileser III conquered Damascus in 732 BCE and ended the independent Aramean kingdom. Yet Damascus persisted as an inhabited city over subsequent periods, with extensive Hellenistic, Roman, Byzantine and Islamic layers documented by UNESCO.
Relationship
The end of Aram-Damascus's sovereign status is historically grounded and is closely connected with the oracle. The image of Damascus no longer being a city or reduced to rubble exceeds the lasting result: it should be described as destruction rhetoric, temporary damage, an unfulfilled literal detail, or a future expectation only with stated interpretive grounds.
Historical evidence & limits
The Assyrian victory and loss of kingship can be independently confirmed from history. The composition date relative to 732 BCE is inferred from literary and historical context, not secured by an extant dated pre-conquest manuscript.

Assyrian defeat and ended kingdom supported; permanent urban disappearance contradicted by history; original scope debated

Earlier situation
Obadiah denounces Edom's pride and alleged exploitation of Jerusalem's disaster, then imagines reversal, territorial recovery and divine rule. Scholarly dates range widely; Elie Assis (2014) argues for three composed stages: before Jerusalem's fall, in response to 586 BCE and later Edomite movement into southern Judah. The text shares striking sections with Jeremiah 49:7–22.
Later use
The Edomite polity disappeared over time, but Edomite/Idumaean identity continued in southern Judea through the Hellenistic and Roman periods; Idumaeans figure in the Hasmonean era and Herod's ancestry. Later Jewish and Christian interpreters may read the fall of Edom as fulfilled, symbolic, or typological judgment.
Relationship
Loss of an independent kingdom must be separated from the survival of Edomite people, their relocation and identity, and literal eradication of every descendant. The phrase 'no survivor' can function as national judgment rhetoric, but readers who take it literally face contrary evidence for Idumaean continuation. The prophecy's date and its shared literary traditions affect how far it could precede the events described.

Oxford's entry on Idumaea and research on Edomite-related settlement document survival of the identity for centuries, limiting literal extermination claims. Juan Manuel Tebes (2011) questions the traditional portrayal of Edomite betrayal in 586 BCE, warning against treating partisan narrative blame as an independently verified war report.

Earlier situation
Haggai dates this encouragement to the second year of Darius (normally identified as Darius I, 520 BCE), when Judah's community was rebuilding the Jerusalem temple. The modest appearance of the project relative to Solomon's temple is explicit in 2:3, followed by promises of shaking nations, wealth, glory and peace. Later use
The Hebrew phrase often printed as 'desire of all nations' is commonly rendered 'treasures/precious things of the nations' because the plural verb indicates a collective sense and the next verse speaks of silver and gold. Some Christians have interpreted a singular 'Desired One' as Jesus; Hebrews 12:26 quotes the cosmic-shaking line in an eschatological argument.
Relationship
The temple-focused immediate setting, the treasure-vs-person linguistic question and the later use of the shaking image are distinct. A direct named prediction of Jesus from 2:7 is grammatically weak; faith-based typology is a different interpretive claim. 'Greater glory' can refer to cultic honor, prosperity or divine presence, making purely architectural comparisons insufficient.

The Persian-period temple restoration is broadly documented by ancient literary sources, but exact glory, world-shaking and peace promises lack an independently measurable complete historical fulfillment. Earlier situation
Zechariah 14 pictures hostile nations attacking Jerusalem, YHWH's personal intervention on the Mount of Olives, extraordinary light, living waters, and surviving nations worshipping at Sukkot. Zechariah 9–14 is widely treated as having a literary history distinct from dated 1–8; Paul Redditt (2021) situates major additions in the fifth century BCE while other scholars argue differing Persian/Hellenistic dates.
Later use
Jewish liturgy reads this chapter at Sukkot, treating the festival pilgrimage and universal kingship as future hope. Christian traditions connect the Mount of Olives and divine arrival to Jesus' second coming, sometimes with literal geological and geopolitical expectations. Those readings must be individually attributed rather than merged into one consensus.
Relationship
This is a visionary description of universal divine rule with cosmic and geographic imagery; whether meant literally, symbolically or as still-future eschatology is contested. It is not evidence that an independently dated historic Mount-of-Olives rupture occurred, nor does a later Jerusalem conflict reproduce every described feature.
Historical evidence & limits
No independent historical documentation establishes the combined series of mountain splitting, perpetual miraculous light, world worship and uninterrupted seasonal water flows as an already completed event. Conversely, absence of such confirmation does not logically disprove an unbounded future expectation. A research verdict must specify past-fulfillment versus still-future claim.

Daniel 2:31–45 (Aramaic) — Statue of four kingdoms and a kingdom that will never end

Later use/test: Successive Babylonian, Median, Persian, Greek or Roman empires; divine kingdom · Four-empire apocalyptic scheme and enduring-kingdom prediction

Original expression
Daniel 2:44, Aramaic: יְקִים אֱלָהּ שְׁמַיָּא מַלְכוּ ('the God of heaven will establish a kingdom'); the king is explicitly called the head of gold in 2:38.
Earlier situation
Daniel's story is set at Nebuchadnezzar's Babylonian court. From 2:4b the chapter is in Aramaic, and it supplies no individual names for its last three kingdoms. Critical scholarship reads the sequence in the context of Greek-era Jewish apocalyptic expectation; the finished visions were edited in the second-century BCE Antiochene crisis, although individual court tales may be older.
Later use
Yale's John J. Collins reconstructs Babylon, Media, Persia and the Greek Seleucid kingdom from the book's own succession, with failed Seleucid/Ptolemaic marriage alliances in the iron/clay imagery. A long-lived Christian interpretation instead combines Media and Persia and ends with Rome; traditions vary over whether the stone kingdom began with Jesus' ministry or remains future.
Relationship
The relative dating of the composed chapter and the selected fourth kingdom determine whether an interpretation predicts long-distant Rome or criticizes Greek imperial power in its own day. The eternal and universal divine kingdom has no agreed independently measured historical completion.

Earlier situation
Mark describes Jesus and his followers leaving Herod's Temple complex; the disciples admire its great stones and buildings, and Jesus announces their dismantling. Relationship

The destruction of the Temple in 70 CE is strongly evidenced, while exact independent confirmation of the speech or an earlier dated manuscript is unavailable. Earlier situation
A vision following the beast's judgment depicts Satan bound for one thousand years, martyrs reigning, a later release and final battle, judgment of the dead, and a new heaven and new earth descending with a vast cubic Jerusalem. The author reworks Isaiah 25, 65–66, Ezekiel 37–48 and Danielic resurrection/court imagery. The 'new city' has no separate temple because God and the Lamb embody its sanctuary.
Later use
Premillennialists read a future distinct thousand-year reign followed by final judgment; amillennialists understand the thousand years symbolically as Christ's present reign with a final consummation; postmillennial readings view an extended gospel transformation before final return. Jewish and Christian readings of Isaiah/Ezekiel antecedents differ. Catholic USCCB notes explicitly interpret the millennium as a symbolic, rather than literal, long period.
Relationship
The gigantic cube, innumerable architectural jewels and replacement of sun/temple are structured symbols; a blanket assertion that the material describes exactly 1,000 elapsed modern years is a tradition-specific premise. The promise of no more death is a more direct observable end-state condition, and no historical period has fulfilled it as a universal physical fact.
Historical evidence & limits
Earlier intertexts and competing reception histories can be verified. No independently observable global resurrection, permanent ending of death, destruction of the cosmos or descent of a jewel-built heavenly metropolis has been recorded. A future expectation is logically different from an already verified historical event.
Current assessment
Apocalyptic intertexts and competing millennium interpretations attested; final world transformation unverified

Exodus 23:20; Malachi 3:1; Isaiah 40:3 — Isaiah is named over a three-passage composite quotation

Later use/test: Mark 1:2–3, applied to John the Baptist's ministry · Composite literary quotation with 'Isaiah' vs 'prophets' textual variant

Original expression
Mark 1:2 ἐν τῷ Ἠσαΐᾳ τῷ προφήτῃ ('in Isaiah the prophet') in the early critical witnesses; later variant ἐν τοῖς προφήταις ('in the prophets'). Mark's first quoted messenger wording echoes Exodus 23:20 and Malachi 3:1; 1:3 cites Isaiah 40:3.
Earlier situation
Exodus's messenger originally guides Israel toward the land; Malachi warns of coming purification and a messenger before YHWH's arrival; Isaiah 40 announces comfort and a wilderness highway for YHWH during exile/restoration. None originally names John the Baptist.
Later use
Mark makes John the Baptist the preparing wilderness messenger for Jesus and captions both source fragments with Isaiah. Matthew and Luke rework these traditions in their own order. Many manuscripts read 'in the prophets,' a possible smoothing of the difficult naming issue; the older/widely attested critical reading favors Isaiah.
Relationship
Mark's introductory quotation is conflated from at least two different prophet texts and language echoed from Exodus. It is not a single uncontested exact forward-looking prediction. Attribution to the leading prophet can be an ancient composition convention; a mistaken attribution or later editorial correction are alternative scholarly explanations, but each must explain the manuscript evidence.
Historical evidence & limits
Direct primary comparison and well-documented textual variants establish the wording change. NET critical notes specify witnesses and explain why the harder Isaiah reading has early support; this determines which 'attribution problem' an analyst is actually judging. The Gospel's literary reuse does not independently corroborate John the Baptist as a uniquely predicted individual.
Current assessment
Three-source composite and early Isaiah attribution supported; original referents distinct from later Christian reading

Psalm 22:18 (Hebrew; 22:19 in some numbering) — Psalm 22's garment-lot lament becomes two fulfillment actions

Later use/test: John 19:23–24 (compare Mark 15:24; Matthew 27:35; Luke 23:34) · Poetic lament reused as a passion scene with narrative elaboration

Original expression
Psalm 22:18 יְחַלְּקוּ בְגָדַי לָהֶם וְעַל־לְבוּשִׁי יַפִּילוּ גוֹרָל ('they divide my clothes among themselves, and cast lots for my clothing').
Earlier situation
Psalm 22 is a first-person lament by a speaker who believes he has been abandoned and exposed to humiliating violence, before a turn toward divine deliverance and praise. Its two garment expressions use synonymous poetic parallelism to depict enemies treating the sufferer as doomed; no first-century Roman soldiers are named.
Later use
All four canonical passion accounts mention garments and casting lots. John alone quotes Psalm 22 directly at this exact point and distinguishes the soldiers' dividing several garments from separately casting lots for a seamless tunic; the USCCB note describes the scene as a literal division of poetic parallelism into two narrated actions.
Relationship
The earlier lyric describes humiliation and dispossession within a prayer, while the Gospel treats it as Jesus' fulfilled scripture. Multiple Gospels may preserve shared tradition and cannot automatically be counted as four independent eyewitness confirmations; deliberate narrative shaping around scripture is a live historical-critical possibility.
Historical evidence & limits
Psalm wording and John's allocation of the two actions can be compared. There is no external material record of the soldiers' precise lottery, so the evangelist's assertion of fulfillment cannot independently establish that every physical detail occurred as narrated.
Current assessment
Scriptural quotation and parallelism clear; specific allocation scene lacks independent attestation

Isaiah 53:7–8 (Hebrew and ancient Greek) — The Ethiopian official asks who Isaiah's suffering servant is

Later use/test: Acts 8:26–40, especially 8:32–35 · Ancient Greek servant quotation used as a Christian teaching interpretation

Original expression
Acts 8:33 quotes Greek ἐν τῇ ταπεινώσει ἡ κρίσις αὐτοῦ ἤρθη ('in his humiliation his justice/judgment was taken away'); Hebrew Isaiah 53:8 reads מֵעֹצֶר וּמִמִּשְׁפָּט לֻקָּח ('by/from oppression and judgment he was taken').
Earlier situation
Isaiah's servant poem appears in Isaiah 52:13–53:12 and has disputed identity, including collective Israel, representative individual and other proposals; Isaiah 49:3 can call the servant Israel even as 49:5 depicts a mission to restore Israel. The Hebrew and ancient Greek forms of 53:8 differ significantly in their portrayal of oppression and judgment.
Later use
Acts narrates a royal official of the Candace, queen of the Ethiopians (the kingdom of Kush/Meroë in Nubia), returning from worship in Jerusalem while reading Isaiah's Greek form. He asks Philip whom the prophet means, and Philip teaches that Jesus is the referent, after which the official is baptized. The narrative's question records that the passage's identity was not treated as self-evident.
Relationship
Acts quotes the old Greek more closely than the Hebrew on the 'humiliation/justice' clause and applies an earlier servant poem to Jesus. The later interpretation is clearly documented as a claim by Acts' author and characters; it does not independently establish the earlier writer's intended identity or the historicity of the encounter.
Historical evidence & limits
USCCB's critical Acts 8 note identifies the quoted verses and evangelization role, and a direct comparison shows Hebrew/Greek wording differences. The reported angelic instructions, sudden disappearance and precise dialogue are only attested within Acts, not confirmed by independent contemporary records.
Current assessment
Greek quotation and Jesus application explicit; Hebrew/Greek differences and earlier servant identity remain contested
