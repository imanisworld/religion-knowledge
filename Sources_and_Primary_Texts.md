> **Who is saying what.** Three markers run through all seven documents:
>
> - **⟨DOCUMENTED⟩** — a named scholar in a named publication. Checkable. If no name is attached, it is not this.
> - **⟨INFERENCE⟩** — reasoning built on documented facts but not itself found in any source. Claude's analysis. Defensible, but nobody has published it, and it should never be cited as though someone had.
> - **⟨YOURS⟩** — your own observation, from reading or live conversation. Recorded because it held up, not because it was agreeable.
>
> **The rule:** anything marked ⟨INFERENCE⟩ is Claude reasoning. Treat it exactly as sceptically as you treat everything else here.


> **How to use this.** Every entry gives you three things: what the source *is*, what it *actually says*, and what it *does and does not establish*. Links go to free, reputable full texts wherever they exist.
>
> **The rule that matters:** never cite a source you have not opened. If you name Josephus in a conversation and cannot say which passage, you will be asked, and the answer decides whether the rest of your case is taken seriously.

## 1. Ancient Near Eastern Inscriptions

These are the hard anchors. They are physical objects with dates, and they are not Jewish or Christian in origin, which is exactly what makes them useful.

### 1.1 The Merneptah Stele — c. 1208 BCE

**What it is.** An Egyptian victory monument of Pharaoh Merneptah, granite, discovered by Flinders Petrie at Thebes in 1896, now in the Egyptian Museum in Cairo.

**What it says.** Mostly a victory account over the Libyans. <cite index="54-1">The last three of its 28 lines deal with a separate campaign in Canaan, and a majority of scholars translate a set of hieroglyphs in line 27 as "Israel."</cite> The line claims Israel is laid waste and his seed is not.

**What it establishes.** <cite index="54-1">It is the earliest textual reference to Israel and the only one from ancient Egypt.</cite> Crucially, the hieroglyphic determiner marks Israel as a *people*, not a city-state or territory — a group without a fixed place, on the ground in Canaan by 1208 BCE.

**What it does not establish.** Nothing about an exodus, a conquest, or a covenant. An Egyptian king says he defeated a people called Israel. That is the whole content.

→ [Text and translation (Bible Dudes)](https://bibledudes.com/biblical-studies/finds/merneptah-translation.php) · [Background (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merneptah_Stele)

### 1.2 The Mesha Stele / Moabite Stone — c. 840 BCE

**What it is.** <cite index="55-1">Basalt, about a meter tall, 34 surviving lines in the Moabite language, discovered 1868–70 in Jordan, now in the Louvre.</cite>

**What it says.** <cite index="55-1">King Mesha of Moab records that Chemosh, the god of Moab, had been angry with his people and allowed them to be subjugated by the kingdom of Israel — and that Chemosh then returned, helped Mesha throw off the Israelite yoke, and restored Moab's lands.</cite>

**Why this one is worth reading in full.** ⟨INFERENCE — the inscription's content is documented; this structural comparison is Claude's⟩ The theology is *identical in structure* to the Deuteronomistic history: national god is angry, permits defeat by enemies, relents, delivers his people. Mesha is running the exact interpretive framework the biblical authors run. That is the argument. It is not a uniquely Israelite theology; it is standard Iron Age Levantine political theology, and a neighbouring king with a different god states it in the same terms.

**What else it establishes.** <cite index="55-1">It is the most extensive inscription ever recovered referring to the kingdom of Israel — the "House of Omri" — and it bears the earliest certain extrabiblical reference to YHWH.</cite>

**The believer's use of it, stated fairly.** Livius notes it directly: <cite index="60-1">in the nineteenth century many scholars had begun doubting the Bible's reliability as a historical source, and the Mesha stela was seen as an important argument against those skeptics.</cite> It does corroborate the general situation of 2 Kings 3. Concede that; the theology point is the stronger one anyway.

→ [Full translation and commentary (Livius.org)](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/anet/320-the-stela-of-mesha/)

### 1.3 The Tel Dan Stele — c. 840 BCE

**What it is.** An Aramaic victory inscription, discovered 1993–94, now in the Israel Museum.

**What it says.** An Aramaean king (probably Hazael of Damascus) claims victory over the king of Israel and the king of the "House of David."

**What it establishes.** That within roughly 150 years of David's supposed reign, a neighbouring hostile kingdom identified the southern dynasty by his name. This ended the serious minimalist argument that David was wholly legendary.

**What it does not establish.** Anything about the *scale* of his kingdom — which is where the actual scholarly fight is. See Historical Framework §2.3.

**Handle with care.** If you argue David never existed, this is the object that will be produced, and you will lose. Argue scale, not existence.

### 1.4 The Sennacherib Prism — 691 BCE

**What it is.** <cite index="57-1">Three near-identical complete hexagonal clay prisms — the Taylor and Jerusalem Prisms (691 BC) and the ISAC prism (689 BC) — plus at least eight fragmentary prisms in the British Museum.</cite> The Chicago copy is in the Institute for the Study of Ancient Cultures.

**What it says.** The Assyrian king's own annals of his 701 BCE campaign, describing the capture of Levantine cities and the siege of Jerusalem — with the famous line that he shut Hezekiah up like a bird in a cage.

**What makes it exceptional.** <cite index="57-1">It gives a different perspective on these events from the Books of Kings, and some passages in 2 Kings 18–19 agree with at least a few of the claims on the prism.</cite> The prism records tribute and the shutting-up, and does **not** claim Jerusalem was taken. 2 Kings says the siege lifted. Two hostile sources, one event, compatible facts, incompatible explanations.

**This is the best-corroborated event in the Hebrew Bible.** ⟨INFERENCE — a judgement about relative strength, not a quoted finding⟩ Concede it immediately and without hedging. Doing so establishes that you follow evidence, which is what makes your other concessions demands credible.

→ [Translation (Livius.org)](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/anet/287-the-sennacherib-prism/) · [Background (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sennacherib's_Annals)

## 2. Jewish Texts

### 2.1 Sefaria — the whole Jewish library, free

**What it is.** <cite index="38-1">An open-source, free digital library of Jewish scriptural, theological, philosophical, legal, mystical, historical, and ethical texts, in English, Hebrew, and Yiddish.</cite> <cite index="40-1">The largest free library of Jewish texts online — Torah, Tanakh, Talmud, Mishnah, Midrash, and commentaries.</cite>

**Why it is the single most useful link in this document.** Every Hebrew text is displayed alongside the English, and every verse links to its commentary tradition — Rashi, Ramban, Ibn Ezra, and the Talmudic passages that cite it. When you want to know what a Hebrew word actually says, or what the rabbis did with a verse, this is where you go.

**Specific things to look up here:**
- *Makkot* 23b — R. Simlai's 613 count, the whole basis for that number
- *Makkot* 24a — the gematria derivation (Torah = 611, plus two heard at Sinai)
- Leviticus 18 and 19 in Hebrew, to see for yourself that no verse marks the moral/ceremonial boundary
- Maimonides, *Sefer HaMitzvot* — the enumeration and the fourteen governing principles

→ [Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/texts) · [Josephus on Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/The_Antiquities_of_the_Jews)

### 2.2 The Dead Sea Scrolls — discovered 1947

**What they are.** Roughly a thousand manuscripts from Qumran, dating from about the third century BCE to the first century CE — a millennium older than the previously oldest Hebrew biblical manuscripts.

**What they establish, and it cuts both ways.**

*For textual stability:* the Great Isaiah Scroll is substantially the same text as the medieval Masoretic Isaiah, across roughly a thousand years of copying. That is a genuinely striking result and apologists cite it correctly.

*For textual fluidity:* the scrolls also contain variant text-types, including readings agreeing with the Septuagint against the Masoretic text, and multiple divergent versions of some books (Jeremiah appears in a significantly shorter form). The picture is not one stable text but several competing text-traditions circulating simultaneously in the Second Temple period.

**The honest summary** ⟨INFERENCE⟩**:** the consonantal text was transmitted carefully once it stabilized — and it stabilized *late*, after a period of genuine plurality. Both halves are true and most people only ever hear one.

**They also establish** how varied Second Temple Judaism was: sectarian rules, apocalyptic literature, and community organization that appear nowhere in the Hebrew Bible. See Historical Framework §2.4.

→ [Leon Levy Dead Sea Scrolls Digital Library](https://www.deadseascrolls.org.il/) · [Israel Museum digitized scrolls](http://dss.collections.imj.org.il/)

### 2.3 Josephus — c. 37–100 CE

**What he is.** A Jewish aristocrat and priest who commanded rebel forces in Galilee during the First Revolt, surrendered to the Romans, and then wrote history under Flavian patronage. That patronage matters: he is writing to explain the Jews to a Roman audience and to explain his own defection.

**The two works that matter.** *The Jewish War* (c. 75 CE) and *Antiquities of the Jews* (c. 93 CE). <cite index="35-1">Josephus published the Antiquities eighteen years after the War, in the thirteenth year of Domitian.</cite>

**The two Jesus passages.**

*Antiquities* 20.200 — the James reference. Mentions James, "the brother of Jesus who was called Christ." <cite index="28-1">Widely accepted as authentic</cite> and the stronger of the two, precisely because it is incidental — Josephus is telling a story about a high priest overstepping his authority, and Jesus appears only to identify which James is meant.

*Antiquities* 18.63–64 — the *Testimonium Flavianum*. <cite index="28-1">Contains language, including explicit affirmation of the resurrection and messianic titles, that many scholars regard as later Christian interpolation, while several recent scholars argue a substantial core describing a wise man executed by Pilate is original.</cite>

**Do not call it a forgery as though that were settled.** <cite index="29-1">Ken Olson and Paul Hopper have argued for wholesale later insertion — solid scholarship by qualified people with no obvious agenda — but they remain a minority, while online mythicism treats "forgery" as hard fact rather than minority interpretation.</cite> The point against you is easy to look up.

**One argument from silence worth knowing, from the other direction:** <cite index="30-1">no form of the Testimonium is cited by Justin Martyr, Theophilus, Melito, Minucius Felix, Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria, Julius Africanus, Tertullian, Hippolytus, Origen, Methodius, or Lactantius — all of whom show familiarity with Josephus.</cite> The counter-explanation is also worth knowing: <cite index="30-1">if the original text did nothing more than establish that Jesus existed, and nobody in the first centuries questioned that, there would be no occasion to quote it.</cite>

→ [Antiquities, full text (Perseus)](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0146) · [The Jewish War (Perseus)](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0148) · [The Testimonium debate, documented](http://www.earlychristianwritings.com/testimonium.html)

## 3. Roman Sources

### 3.1 Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44 — c. 115 CE

**What it says.** Reporting Nero's scapegoating of Christians for the fire of Rome, Tacitus explains who they were: Christus, from whom the name derives, suffered the extreme penalty under Pontius Pilate during Tiberius's reign, and the movement spread from Judea to Rome.

**Why it carries weight.** <cite index="28-1">Modern scholarship treats the reference as independent and hostile to Christianity, which strengthens its evidentiary value.</cite> Tacitus regards the movement as a destructive superstition. Hostile witnesses do not fabricate origin stories for groups they despise.

**What it establishes.** A man executed under Pilate, a movement named for him, spread to Rome by the 60s. Solid.

**What it does not.** Tacitus is writing ~85 years later and may be reporting what Christians in Rome said about themselves. It confirms what was believed by the early second century, not what happened in 30 CE. And note the honest limitation raised from the skeptic side: he is not an infallible source and the passage was transmitted by Christian scribes.

### 3.2 Pliny the Younger, *Letters* 10.96 — c. 112 CE

**What it says.** A provincial governor writes to the emperor Trajan asking how to handle Christians: whether to punish the name itself, what to do with those who recant. He describes them meeting before dawn and singing to Christ as to a god.

**Why it matters.** It is administrative correspondence, not polemic and not apologetics. It establishes early second-century Christian worship practice from a completely disinterested source.

## 4. Christian Texts and Manuscripts

### 4.1 Codex Sinaiticus — 4th century CE

**What it is.** <cite index="49-1">Handwritten over 1,600 years ago, containing the Christian Bible in Greek, including the oldest complete copy of the New Testament.</cite> <cite index="50-1">Its fragments, scattered across four institutions, have been reunited digitally and made freely accessible.</cite>

**Why you should actually open it.** You can see the manuscript evidence yourself rather than taking anyone's word. Two things to look at:

- **Mark ends at 16:8** — with the women fleeing and saying nothing. The longer ending (16:9–20), including the snake-handling and poison-drinking, is not there.
- **John 7:53–8:11** — the woman caught in adultery — is absent.

Both passages are in most printed Bibles. Being able to say *I have looked at the fourth-century manuscript and it is not there* is worth more than any secondary citation.

→ [Codex Sinaiticus (browse the manuscript)](https://www.codexsinaiticus.org/en/manuscript.aspx)

### 4.2 Early Christian Writings — the extracanonical corpus

**What it is.** A free archive of the non-canonical and sub-apostolic literature: the Didache, 1 Clement, the Epistle of Barnabas, the Shepherd of Hermas, the Gospel of Thomas, the Gospel of Peter, and the rest, with dating discussions and scholarly bibliography.

**Why it matters for the canon argument.** Several of these were treated as scripture in some regions and read in churches. 1 Clement and the Shepherd of Hermas appear in some early manuscript collections. The canon did not divide cleanly into "obviously scripture" and "obviously not" — that clarity is retrospective. See Historical Framework §5.

→ [Early Christian Writings](http://www.earlychristianwritings.com/)

### 4.3 What to read in the New Testament itself, in this order

For understanding how the theology was built rather than how the story is told:

1. **1 Thessalonians** (c. 50 CE) — the earliest Christian document that survives. Note what is *absent*: no virgin birth, no empty tomb narrative, no biography.
2. **1 Corinthians 15:3–7** — the pre-Pauline creed. Concede its early date. Note it lists appearances and does not mention an empty tomb.
3. **Galatians 1–2** — Paul's own account of the Jerusalem conflict, then compare with Acts 15. See Master Notes §9.3.
4. **Mark 16:1–8** — the original ending.
5. **John 1:1–18** — the prologue, where the high Christology is explicit.

## 5. Islamic Sources

**Quran.** [quran.com](https://quran.com) gives parallel Arabic and multiple English translations, with word-by-word breakdown. Compare at least two translations of any verse you intend to use; the divergences between Sahih International, Yusuf Ali, and Pickthall are frequently the whole argument.

**Hadith.** [sunnah.com](https://sunnah.com) hosts the major collections — Bukhari, Muslim, Abu Dawud, Tirmidhi — with grading notation. The grading system itself is worth understanding: the tradition developed an explicit apparatus for rating transmission chains as sound, good, weak, or fabricated. That is a source-critical method internal to the tradition, and it is more developed than anything the Christian tradition applied to its own texts.

**The comparison worth holding.** The Quranic text was standardized under Uthman within about twenty years of Muhammad's death — much tighter than the New Testament's transmission, and Muslim apologists press this correctly. But the *sira* and hadith, which supply almost all biographical detail, were compiled one to two centuries later, a gap comparable to or worse than the Gospels'. Ibn Ishaq's biography survives only through Ibn Hisham's later edition.

## 6. Checking Yourself

### 6.1 History for Atheists

**What it is.** Tim O'Neill, an atheist, systematically debunking bad atheist history — the Nicaea myths, mythicism, the Dark Ages thesis, the Horus parallels, the Hypatia legend.

**Why it is in this document.** Cited approvingly at Historical Framework §9. If you are about to use a historical claim in an argument, check it here first. His work on the Testimonium is a model of how to state a position: name the minority scholars, credit their work, and then say plainly that they are a minority.

→ [History for Atheists](https://historyforatheists.com/)

### 6.2 The standing rule

Three questions before any source enters an argument:

1. **Have I opened it?** Not read about it — opened it.
2. **How long between the event and the writing?**
3. **Is this independent, or does it trace back to something I have already counted?**

A source you cannot locate is not evidence. It is a rumour with a footnote.
