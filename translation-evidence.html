How to establish what changed

First identify the exact passage and editions. Then distinguish a different source manuscript from a translation choice, a punctuation decision, or a modern publisher's revision. If a translator's motive is unrecorded, mark it unknown.

  1. Identify the verse and edition dates.
  2. Read original-language words and surrounding context.
  3. Classify the difference: word, grammar, speaker, punctuation, manuscripts, or revision.
  4. Check documented translators’ notes or publisher change logs.
  5. Show the reader effect and alternative interpretations.
  6. Separate evidence from claims of deliberate concealment.

Evidence by passage

2 Corinthians 12:16

Speaker framing

ὑπάρχων πανοῦργος δόλῳ ὑμᾶς ἔλαβον

What changed
KJV presents a first-person statement; ESV adds “you say”; God's Word frames it as a question.
How / why
The surrounding verses (12:17–18) challenge an allegation that Paul exploited the Corinthian church. The UBS Handbook explains the implied accusation.
What it means
The English framing can make Paul sound like he confessed deceit or like he rejected an accusation. The Greek has neither an explicit “you say” nor a question in verse 16.
Evidence status
Strong contextual reading; intent not proven

Romans 3:7

Hypothetical speaker

ἐν τῷ ἐμῷ ψεύσματι … τί ἔτι κἀγὼ … κρίνομαι

What changed
Literal versions retain “my lie”; NIV introduces the argument with “Someone might argue.”
How / why
The passage develops objections in Romans 3:5–8. Greek εἰ means “if,” and τί means “why”: a conditional plus a question already exist.
What it means
This is no demonstrated confession that Paul lied. The translation makes an inferred argumentative speaker explicit.
Evidence status
Greek grammar verified; attribution contextual

Genesis 3:16

Committee revision

אֶל־אִישֵׁךְ

What changed
ESV 2016 used “contrary to your husband”; the 2025 revision restored “for your husband.”
How / why
Crossway says the restoration better preserves interpretive options for Hebrew ’el; it also replaced the contrastive “but” with “and.”
What it means
A reading about antagonism between woman and man became less compulsory in English. The committee's documented reasoning can be examined; a hidden motive cannot be assumed.
Evidence status
Documented by publisher

Isaiah 7:14

Lexical choice and reception

הָעַלְמָה / παρθένος

What changed
Hebrew ‘almah is rendered “young woman” in some translations, “virgin” in others. The ancient Greek Septuagint uses parthenos, which Matthew 1:23 quotes.
How / why
The Hebrew noun specifies a young woman rather than explicitly asserting virginity; the Greek and later Christian reading connect the verse to Jesus' birth.
What it means
Read the Hebrew prophecy in its eighth-century BCE context and separately examine its use in Matthew. A religious interpretation should not be mistaken for an unambiguous Hebrew lexical requirement.
Evidence status
Original terms documented; later interpretation debated

Genesis 1:1–3

Syntax / sentence division

בְּרֵאשִׁית

What changed
Traditional “In the beginning God created…” contrasts with “When God began to create…”
How / why
The debate concerns how to construe bĕrēʾšît, the opening clause, and its relationship to verses 2–3. Rashi and modern scholars have argued for the dependent-clause translation.
What it means
Neither translation alone proves previous human populations or earlier creations. A 2025 peer-reviewed study argues that the missing article does not rule out “in the beginning.”
Evidence status
Genuine grammatical debate

1 John 5:7–8

Manuscript variation

ὁ πατήρ, ὁ λόγος, καὶ τὸ ἅγιον πνεῦμα (later expanded reading)

What changed
The KJV contains the explicit heavenly-witnesses clause naming Father, Word, and Holy Spirit; modern critical editions exclude it from their base text.
How / why
This is a text-critical issue: the clause is absent from early Greek witnesses and is principally documented in later Latin and some very late Greek witnesses.
What it means
Calling this merely a modern “mistranslation” conceals the earlier manuscript history. The variant affects whether this passage explicitly states a Trinitarian formula; it does not alone determine the theology of the Trinity.
Evidence status
Strong manuscript evidence; development history studied

Genesis 37:35 / Psalm 9:17 / Numbers 16:33

Vocabulary and semantic range

שְׁאוֹל

What changed
The KJV variously translates sheʾol as “grave,” “hell,” and “pit.” The previous Translations document wrongly claimed it used “hell” throughout.
How / why
Translators chose English equivalents for an ancient term associated with the realm of the dead; modern readings of “hell” can import later associations not contained in every Hebrew use.
What it means
Compare the term across several contexts before concluding any one verse describes eternal fiery punishment.
Evidence status
Verified factual correction

Young woman, virgin, and young man across biblical contexts

The terms “young woman,” “maiden,” and “virgin” can describe overlapping social states. Their meanings differ by passage and historical setting. They do not assign a fixed numeric age or demonstrate sexual history by themselves. Compare the male descriptions in the same literary environments.

What the male comparison shows

Directly paired: Isaiah 62:5 calls a young groom bachur and a maiden betulah. In Genesis 24:16, 43, the narrator calls Rebekah a na‘arah, a betulah, and an almah, but adds “no man had known her” to specify sexual inexperience. Different labels carry different information.

Legal contrast: Deuteronomy 22:13–21 regulates accusations about the bride's virginity without a parallel groom-virginity test in that law. Matthew 19:20 simply calls its male interlocutor a neaniskos (“young man”). These do not prove the same contrast exists in every passage.

Revelation 14:4: Parthenoi (“virgins”) is applied to a male-coded group in apocalyptic imagery about the 144,000. The scene also describes relations with women as defiling. Scholars disagree whether it depicts literal celibacy, a symbolically faithful community, or military/angelic purity. This unusual masculine usage does not establish equal virginity expectations for men and women. Zimmermann (2003) · Breu (2022).

Earlier male abstinence: 1 Samuel 21:4–5 describes temporary abstinence for holy bread; Matthew 19:12 concerns forgoing marriage for the kingdom; 1 Corinthians 7:7–9 commends singleness. None imposes the equivalent of Deuteronomy 22:13–21’s bride-virginity accusation and punishment on men.

Then and now: what changes

Ancient Israelite contexts: family, betrothal, kinship, and marital obligations help explain descriptions of young women. Isaiah 7:14 refers to a sign in a story set amid an eighth-century BCE crisis; the word almah alone neither proves virginity nor fixes a modern age.

Ancient Greek and first-century texts: parthenos could signify a maiden or virgin. Classical Athenian marriage norms provide context for Greek vocabulary but cannot simply be projected onto biblical Judah. Luke 1 specifies Mary's betrothal and explicitly frames her lack of sexual experience in the larger narrative.

Modern English: “Young woman,” “unmarried,” and “virgin” usually communicate three different claims. A translator choosing one term can make a social expectation sound like a biological statement.

Research: Susan Ackerman (2025), women's life-cycle rituals in ancient Israel; Susan Ackerman (2025), a young woman's wedding; Avi Shveka & Avraham Faust, premarital sex in biblical law; Giulia Sissa (2017), parthenos and social meanings of virginity; Liddell–Scott–Jones Greek lexicon, parthenos (including males in Revelation 14:4); Liddell–Scott–Jones Greek lexicon, neaniskos; Metropolitan Museum of Art, women in Classical Athens (a comparison, not ancient Israel).

These are nine checked examples, not a frequency survey. Genre, region, date of composition, and a text's depicted historical setting must be kept distinct.

What these comparisons establish

Documented: English wording differs; some Greek and Hebrew expressions admit more than one construction; the ESV publisher has disclosed a specific revision; early Greek copies lack the longer 1 John 5:7–8 clause.

Interpretation: Paul’s opponents likely supplied the accusation in 2 Corinthians 12:16, and a translator may make that implied speaker explicit. Such cases must show contextual evidence.

Not established: A single planned effort to hide every uncomfortable verse, a numerical rate of dishonesty across translations, or ancient populations existing before Genesis merely because the opening grammar is debated.
