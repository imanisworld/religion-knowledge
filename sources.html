What Each Source Actually Says — With Links

Who is saying what. Three markers run through all seven documents:

  • DOCUMENTED — a named scholar in a named publication. Checkable. If no name is attached, it is not this.
  • INFERENCE — reasoning built on documented facts but not itself found in any source. Claude’s analysis. Defensible, but nobody has published it, and it should never be cited as though someone had.
  • YOURS — your own observation, from reading or live conversation. Recorded because it held up, not because it was agreeable.

The rule: anything marked INFERENCE is Claude reasoning. Treat it exactly as sceptically as you treat everything else here.

How to use this. Every entry gives you three things: what the source is, what it actually says, and what it does and does not establish. Links go to free, reputable full texts wherever they exist.

The rule that matters: never cite a source you have not opened. If you name Josephus in a conversation and cannot say which passage, you will be asked, and the answer decides whether the rest of your case is taken seriously.

§1Ancient Near Eastern Inscriptions

These are the hard anchors. They are physical objects with dates, and they are not Jewish or Christian in origin, which is exactly what makes them useful.

§1.1The Merneptah Stele — c. 1208 BCE

What it is. An Egyptian victory monument of Pharaoh Merneptah, granite, discovered by Flinders Petrie at Thebes in 1896, now in the Egyptian Museum in Cairo.

What it says. Mostly a victory account over the Libyans. The last three of its 28 lines deal with a separate campaign in Canaan, and a majority of scholars translate a set of hieroglyphs in line 27 as “Israel.” The line claims Israel is laid waste and his seed is not.

What it establishes. It is the earliest textual reference to Israel and the only one from ancient Egypt. Crucially, the hieroglyphic determiner marks Israel as a people, not a city-state or territory — a group without a fixed place, on the ground in Canaan by 1208 BCE.

What it does not establish. Nothing about an exodus, a conquest, or a covenant. An Egyptian king says he defeated a people called Israel. That is the whole content.

§1.2The Mesha Stele / Moabite Stone — c. 840 BCE

What it is. Basalt, about a meter tall, 34 surviving lines in the Moabite language, discovered 1868–70 in Jordan, now in the Louvre.

What it says. King Mesha of Moab records that Chemosh, the god of Moab, had been angry with his people and allowed them to be subjugated by the kingdom of Israel — and that Chemosh then returned, helped Mesha throw off the Israelite yoke, and restored Moab’s lands.

Why this one is worth reading in full. INFERENCE — the inscription’s content is documented; this structural comparison is Claude’s The theology is identical in structure to the Deuteronomistic historyDeuteronomistic historyJoshua through 2 Kings, read as one work shaped by the theology of Deuteronomy.: national god is angry, permits defeat by enemies, relents, delivers his people. Mesha is running the exact interpretive framework the biblical authors run. That is the argument. It is not a uniquely Israelite theology; it is standard Iron Age Levantine political theology, and a neighbouring king with a different god states it in the same terms.

What else it establishes. It is the most extensive inscription ever recovered referring to the kingdom of Israel — the “House of Omri” — and it bears the earliest certain extrabiblical reference to YHWH.

The believer’s use of it, stated fairly. Livius notes it directly: in the nineteenth century many scholars had begun doubting the Bible’s reliability as a historical source, and the Mesha stela was seen as an important argument against those skeptics. It does corroborate the general situation of 2 Kings 3. Concede that; the theology point is the stronger one anyway.

→ Full translation and commentary (Livius.org)

§1.3The Tel Dan Stele — c. 840 BCE

What it is. An Aramaic victory inscription, discovered 1993–94, now in the Israel Museum.

What it says. An Aramaean king (probably Hazael of Damascus) claims victory over the king of Israel and the king of the “House of David.”

What it establishes. That within roughly 150 years of David’s supposed reign, a neighbouring hostile kingdom identified the southern dynasty by his name. This ended the serious minimalistminimalistA scholar who thinks very little of the Hebrew Bible's history is historically reliable. argument that David was wholly legendary.

What it does not establish. Anything about the scale of his kingdom — which is where the actual scholarly fight is. See Historical Framework §2.3.

Handle with care. If you argue David never existed, this is the object that will be produced, and you will lose. Argue scale, not existence.

§1.4The Sennacherib Prism — 691 BCE

What it is. Three near-identical complete hexagonal clay prisms — the Taylor and Jerusalem Prisms (691 BC) and the ISAC prism (689 BC) — plus at least eight fragmentary prisms in the British Museum. The Chicago copy is in the Institute for the Study of Ancient Cultures.

What it says. The Assyrian king’s own annals of his 701 BCE campaign, describing the capture of Levantine cities and the siege of Jerusalem — with the famous line that he shut Hezekiah up like a bird in a cage.

What makes it exceptional. It gives a different perspective on these events from the Books of Kings, and some passages in 2 Kings 18–19 agree with at least a few of the claims on the prism. The prism records tribute and the shutting-up, and does not claim Jerusalem was taken. 2 Kings says the siege lifted. Two hostile sources, one event, compatible facts, incompatible explanations.

This is the best-corroborated event in the Hebrew Bible. INFERENCE — a judgement about relative strength, not a quoted finding Concede it immediately and without hedging. Doing so establishes that you follow evidence, which is what makes your other concessions demands credible.

§2.2The Dead Sea Scrolls — discovered 1947

What they are. Roughly a thousand manuscripts from Qumran, dating from about the third century BCE to the first century CE — a millennium older than the previously oldest Hebrew biblical manuscripts.

What they establish, and it cuts both ways.

For textual stability: the Great Isaiah Scroll is substantially the same text as the medieval Masoretic Isaiah, across roughly a thousand years of copying. That is a genuinely striking result and apologists cite it correctly.

For textual fluidity: the scrolls also contain variant text-types, including readings agreeing with the SeptuagintSeptuagintThe Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures, made around 250 BCE. What most New Testament authors actually quote. against the Masoretic text, and multiple divergent versions of some books (Jeremiah appears in a significantly shorter form). The picture is not one stable text but several competing text-traditions circulating simultaneously in the Second Temple periodSecond Temple periodc. 516 BCE to 70 CE — between the rebuilding and the destruction of the Temple. When most distinctive first-century Jewish beliefs formed..

The honest summary INFERENCE: the consonantal text was transmitted carefully once it stabilized — and it stabilized late, after a period of genuine plurality. Both halves are true and most people only ever hear one.

They also establish how varied Second Temple Judaism was: sectarian rules, apocalypticapocalypticA genre and worldview: the present age is corrupt, God will intervene catastrophically and soon. Not the same as 'apocalypse means disaster'. literature, and community organization that appear nowhere in the Hebrew Bible. See Historical Framework §2.4.

§2.3Josephus — c. 37–100 CE

What he is. A Jewish aristocrat and priest who commanded rebel forces in Galilee during the First Revolt, surrendered to the Romans, and then wrote history under Flavian patronage. That patronage matters: he is writing to explain the Jews to a Roman audience and to explain his own defection.

The two works that matter. The Jewish War (c. 75 CE) and Antiquities of the Jews (c. 93 CE). Josephus published the Antiquities eighteen years after the War, in the thirteenth year of Domitian.

The two Jesus passages.

Antiquities 20.200 — the James reference. Mentions James, “the brother of Jesus who was called Christ.” Widely accepted as authentic and the stronger of the two, precisely because it is incidental — Josephus is telling a story about a high priest overstepping his authority, and Jesus appears only to identify which James is meant.

Antiquities 18.63–64 — the Testimonium FlavianumTestimonium FlavianumThe passage in Josephus describing Jesus. Partly altered by Christian copyists; how much is disputed.. Contains language, including explicit affirmation of the resurrection and messianic titles, that many scholars regard as later Christian interpolationinterpolationWords inserted into a text by a later hand, presented as if original., while several recent scholars argue a substantial core describing a wise man executed by Pilate is original.

Do not call it a forgery as though that were settled. Ken Olson and Paul Hopper have argued for wholesale later insertion — solid scholarship by qualified people with no obvious agenda — but they remain a minority, while online mythicismmythicismThe claim that Jesus never existed as a person. A fringe position among historians, including atheist ones. treats “forgery” as hard fact rather than minority interpretation. The point against you is easy to look up.

One argument from silence worth knowing, from the other direction: no form of the Testimonium is cited by Justin Martyr, Theophilus, Melito, Minucius Felix, Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria, Julius Africanus, Tertullian, Hippolytus, Origen, Methodius, or Lactantius — all of whom show familiarity with Josephus. The counter-explanation is also worth knowing: if the original text did nothing more than establish that Jesus existed, and nobody in the first centuries questioned that, there would be no occasion to quote it.

§3Roman Sources

§3.1Tacitus, Annals 15.44 — c. 115 CE

What it says. Reporting Nero’s scapegoating of Christians for the fire of Rome, Tacitus explains who they were: Christus, from whom the name derives, suffered the extreme penalty under Pontius Pilate during Tiberius’s reign, and the movement spread from Judea to Rome.

Why it carries weight. Modern scholarship treats the reference as independent and hostile to Christianity, which strengthens its evidentiary value. Tacitus regards the movement as a destructive superstition. Hostile witnesses do not fabricate origin stories for groups they despise.

What it establishes. A man executed under Pilate, a movement named for him, spread to Rome by the 60s. Solid.

What it does not. Tacitus is writing ~85 years later and may be reporting what Christians in Rome said about themselves. It confirms what was believed by the early second century, not what happened in 30 CE. And note the honest limitation raised from the skeptic side: he is not an infallible source and the passage was transmitted by Christian scribes.

§3.2Pliny the Younger, Letters 10.96 — c. 112 CE

What it says. A provincial governor writes to the emperor Trajan asking how to handle Christians: whether to punish the name itself, what to do with those who recant. He describes them meeting before dawn and singing to Christ as to a god.

Why it matters. It is administrative correspondence, not polemic and not apologetics. It establishes early second-century Christian worship practice from a completely disinterested source.

§4Christian Texts and Manuscripts

§4.1Codex Sinaiticus — 4th century CE

What it is. Handwritten over 1,600 years ago, containing the Christian Bible in Greek, including the oldest complete copy of the New Testament. Its fragments, scattered across four institutions, have been reunited digitally and made freely accessible.

Why you should actually open it. You can see the manuscript evidence yourself rather than taking anyone’s word. Two things to look at:

  • Mark ends at 16:8 — with the women fleeing and saying nothing. The longer ending (16:9–20), including the snake-handling and poison-drinking, is not there.
  • John 7:53–8:11 — the woman caught in adultery — is absent.

Both passages are in most printed Bibles. Being able to say I have looked at the fourth-century manuscript and it is not there is worth more than any secondary citation.

§4.3What to read in the New Testament itself, in this order

For understanding how the theology was built rather than how the story is told:

  1. 1 Thessalonians (c. 50 CE) — the earliest Christian document that survives. Note what is absent: no virgin birth, no empty tomb narrative, no biography.
  2. 1 Corinthians 15:3–7 — the pre-Pauline creed. Concede its early date. Note it lists appearances and does not mention an empty tomb.
  3. Galatians 1–2 — Paul’s own account of the Jerusalem conflict, then compare with Acts 15. See Master Notes §9.3.
  4. Mark 16:1–8 — the original ending.
  5. John 1:1–18 — the prologue, where the high ChristologyChristologyThe theology of who Jesus is. 'High' Christology treats him as divine; 'low' as human or exalted-but-created. is explicit.

§5Islamic Sources

Quran. quran.com gives parallel Arabic and multiple English translations, with word-by-word breakdown. Compare at least two translations of any verse you intend to use; the divergences between Sahih International, Yusuf Ali, and Pickthall are frequently the whole argument.

HadithhadithReports of Muhammad's sayings and actions, collected and graded for reliability.. sunnah.com hosts the major collections — Bukhari, Muslim, Abu Dawud, Tirmidhi — with grading notation. The grading system itself is worth understanding: the tradition developed an explicit apparatus for rating transmission chains as sound, good, weak, or fabricated. That is a source-critical method internal to the tradition, and it is more developed than anything the Christian tradition applied to its own texts.

The comparison worth holding. The Quranic text was standardized under Uthman within about twenty years of Muhammad’s death — much tighter than the New Testament’s transmission, and Muslim apologists press this correctly. But the sirasiraThe traditional biographies of Muhammad, written 100–200 years after his death. and hadith, which supply almost all biographical detail, were compiled one to two centuries later, a gap comparable to or worse than the Gospels’. Ibn Ishaq’s biography survives only through Ibn Hisham’s later edition.

§6.2The standing rule

Three questions before any source enters an argument:

  1. Have I opened it? Not read about it — opened it.
  2. How long between the event and the writing?
  3. Is this independent, or does it trace back to something I have already counted?

A source you cannot locate is not evidence. It is a rumour with a footnote.
