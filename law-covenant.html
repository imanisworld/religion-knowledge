Torah, the Biblical Covenants, Second Temple Judaism, and Paul — What the Texts Say and Where the Readings Divide

Who is saying what. The same three markers run through the whole corpus:

  • DOCUMENTED: a named scholar in a named publication, or a primary text quoted directly. You can check it.
  • INFERENCE: reasoning built on documented facts that is not itself found in any source. It's the assistant's analysis, and it should never be cited as if someone had published it.
  • YOURS: your own observation, recorded because it held up.

The short version. "Torah" means instruction more than "law," and the Hebrew Bible frames it as a gift inside a covenant, not an entrance exam. Since E.P. Sanders (1977), most scholars agree that Second Temple Judaism was not a religion of earning salvation by merit. They still disagree about how much room it left for final judgment according to works. Paul says the law is "holy and just and good" and also that "the letter kills." Scholars split into four main camps over how those statements fit together. Christian traditions then built frameworks the texts don't contain themselves: moral/ceremonial/judicial law, covenant of works and covenant of grace, dispensations. Since 1965, official Catholic teaching has moved away from the idea that the church simply replaces Israel.

§1The Words: Torah, Berit, Nomos

DOCUMENTED Torah (תּוֹרָה) comes from the root y-r-h, which in the Hiphil means "to direct, teach, instruct." Brown–Driver–Briggs lists "instruction, direction" as the first sense of torah, ahead of "law" (BDB entry). The word covers a parent's teaching (Prov 1:8), priestly rulings, prophetic instruction, and eventually the five books of Moses as a whole.

DOCUMENTED Nomos is the Septuagint's usual rendering of torah, and it is the word Paul uses. Greek nomos carries the sense of civic or statutory law. That is one reason English readers of Paul hear "law" in a legal-courtroom sense the Hebrew word doesn't require.

DOCUMENTED Berit (בְּרִית), "covenant," names a binding relationship with obligations. It can be a treaty between kings, a marriage, or a royal grant. §2 covers why the type of covenant matters.

DOCUMENTED The "613 commandments" comes from one Talmudic homily, not from the Torah itself. Rabbi Simlai (b. Makkot 23b–24a) says 613 commandments were given to Moses: 365 prohibitions matching the days of the solar year and 248 positive commands matching a person's limbs. Rabbinic literature as a whole barely supports an exact count, and outside that passage the manuscripts usually read "all the commandments." The number looks symbolic rather than arithmetical (TheTorah.com; b. Makkot 23b). Medieval codifiers such as Maimonides produced the familiar lists later.

DOCUMENTED The Noahide commandments. Rabbinic tradition did not require Gentiles to keep the Torah. The Tosefta (Avodah Zarah 8:4, c. 70–300 CE) lists seven commandments binding on all "children of Noah": establishing courts of justice, and prohibitions of idolatry, blasphemy, sexual immorality, bloodshed, robbery, and eating a limb torn from a living animal (Posen Library). This matters for §6. The question of what Gentiles owe God, and how that differs from what Israel owes, was already a Jewish question before it became a Christian one.

DOCUMENTED Treaty and grant. George Mendenhall's comparison of Sinai and Deuteronomy with Hittite and Neo-Assyrian vassal treaties is covered in Study Notes §9.6.3 and not repeated here. Moshe Weinfeld added the contrast: alongside the obligatory treaty form, the ancient Near East had a royal grant, where a king rewards a loyal servant with land or dynasty. He read the Abrahamic and Davidic covenants on that model ("The Covenant of Grant in the Old Testament and in the Ancient Near East," JAOS 90 (1970): 184–203; bibliographic record).

INFERENCE The tidy split between "conditional" and "unconditional" covenants is partly a later system laid over the text. The Davidic promise is unconditional in 2 Sam 7:15–16. Ps 132:12 and 1 Kgs 2:4 attach conditions to the same dynasty. The Abrahamic promise of Gen 15 picks up the circumcision obligation in Gen 17. The texts sit in tension, and the frameworks in §6 differ largely over which texts they treat as governing.

DOCUMENTED The new covenant is promised to Israel and Judah, not to a different people. Jeremiah follows the promise straight away with a statement about Israel's permanence: "If this fixed order were ever to cease from my presence, says the Lord, then also the offspring of Israel would cease to be a nation before me forever" (Jer 31:36). The Christian reception of this passage in Hebrews 8 is the subject of the last audit in §7.

§3Torah in Second Temple Judaism

§3.1Diversity, not a single "Judaism"

DOCUMENTED In Paul's lifetime, Jews disagreed sharply about how to keep the Torah. Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes, the Qumran community, and Diaspora interpreters such as Philo disagreed about calendar, purity, oral tradition, and resurrection. The Qumran letter 4QMMT (Miqṣat Maʿaśê ha-Torah, "Some of the Works/Precepts of the Torah"), composed around 150 BCE, sets out purity and calendar rulings on which its authors split from the Jerusalem establishment. It closes by saying that doing them "will be accounted to you as righteousness" (Abegg's translation, quoted in the 4QMMT overview).

DOCUMENTED Why 4QMMT matters for Paul. Its title phrase is the closest Hebrew parallel to Paul's erga nomou, "works of the law" (Gal 2:16; 3:2, 5, 10; Rom 3:20, 28). Martin Abegg argued that Paul was rebutting this kind of theology ("Paul, 'Works of the Law' and MMT," BAR 20:6 (1994); BAR issue). James D.G. Dunn argued that MMT's "works" are exactly the sectarian, boundary-drawing rulings his reading of Galatians predicts ("4QMMT and Galatians," NTS 43 (1997): 147–153; Cambridge).

INFERENCE 4QMMT cuts both ways. It supports Dunn: the works in question are specific rulings that mark one group off from another. It also supports the traditional reading: those works are said to be "accounted… as righteousness." Neither camp gets it free.

§3.2Sanders and "covenantal nomism"

DOCUMENTED E.P. Sanders, Paul and Palestinian Judaism (1977), surveyed Palestinian Jewish literature from about 200 BCE to 200 CE: Tannaitic rabbinic texts, the major Dead Sea Scrolls, and selected Apocrypha and Pseudepigrapha from Ben Sira to 4 Ezra (review summary). His definition:

"covenantal nomism is the view that one's place in God's plan is established on the basis of the covenant and that the covenant requires as the proper response of man his obedience to its commandments, while providing means of atonement for transgression" (p. 75; quoted at Stark)

In short, the covenant comes before the commandments. People obey to stay in, not to get in.

DOCUMENTED Sanders named his own exception. He did not claim that no Jewish text was legalistic. Of 4 Ezra he wrote: "In IV Ezra, in short, we see an instance in which covenantal nomism has collapsed. All that is left is legalistic perfectionism" (p. 409; review summary). That is a single exception, dated after 70 CE and the Temple's destruction.

DOCUMENTED What the texts themselves say supports the "not too hard" side. Deuteronomy 30:11: "Surely, this commandment that I am commanding you today is not too hard for you, nor is it too far away." Paul's own pre-Christian self-assessment: "as to righteousness under the law, blameless" (Phil 3:6). Observations §22.9 develops this point, including the Torah's built-in repair mechanisms (sacrifice, the Day of Atonement, repentance).

§3.3The pushback: "variegated nomism"

DOCUMENTED D.A. Carson, Peter O'Brien, and Mark Seifrid (eds.), Justification and Variegated Nomism, vol. 1: The Complexities of Second Temple Judaism (Mohr Siebeck, 2001), went through the same literature corpus by corpus. Their conclusion was that "covenantal nomism" is too broad a category: some texts fit it, and others weight final judgment by works more heavily than Sanders allowed (Mohr Siebeck).

DOCUMENTED Simon Gathercole, Where Is Boasting? Early Jewish Soteriology and Paul's Response in Romans 1–5 (Eerdmans, 2002), accepted that Jews saw election as grace. He argued that many texts still expected final vindication on the basis of obedience, and that this, not getting in, is what Paul attacks (Eerdmans).

INFERENCE Where things stand. The old Christian caricature, in which Jews tried to earn God's favour from scratch, is dead in the scholarship and has no serious defenders. What remains disputed is narrower: whether "staying in" and final judgment involved enough reliance on deeds that Paul's critique still lands on something real in Judaism, or whether Paul is objecting to something else (§5).

§4Paul on the Law: The Texts That Pull Apart

DOCUMENTED Paul says both kinds of thing, sometimes in the same letter:

Positive or continuing Negative or ending
"So the law is holy, and the commandment is holy and just and good" (Rom 7:12) "the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life" (2 Cor 3:6)
"Do we then overthrow the law through this faith? By no means! On the contrary, we uphold the law" (Rom 3:31) "Why then the law? It was added because of transgressions, until the offspring would come" (Gal 3:19)
"obeying the commandments of God is everything" (1 Cor 7:19) "every man who lets himself be circumcised… is obliged to obey the entire law" (Gal 5:3), said as a warning
"to them belong… the covenants, the giving of the law" (Rom 9:4) "the law was our disciplinarian until Christ came… now that faith has come, we are no longer subject to a disciplinarian" (Gal 3:24–25)

DOCUMENTED 1 Cor 7:19 is the sharpest case. "Circumcision is nothing… but obeying the commandments of God is everything." Yet circumcision is itself one of the commandments (Gen 17:10). Paul is drawing a line inside the Torah that the Torah doesn't draw.

§4.1Three ways to explain the tension

  1. DOCUMENTED Paul is inconsistent. Heikki Räisänen, Paul and the Law (WUNT 29, Mohr Siebeck, 1983; 2nd ed. 1987), argued that Paul's statements about the Torah contain real "tensions and self-contradictions" that no system resolves (Mohr Siebeck listing).
  2. DOCUMENTED Paul reasoned from solution to plight. E.P. Sanders, Paul, the Law, and the Jewish People (Fortress, 1983), argued that Paul did not start with a problem in Judaism and find Christ as the answer. He started from Christ and worked backward: if salvation is through Christ, the law cannot be the way (Fortress Press).
  3. DOCUMENTED Paul is coherent once the question is identified correctly. This covers everyone else in §5. Each camp argues that a different question (individual guilt, ethnic boundaries, Israel's story, or Gentile inclusion) makes the statements fit together.

§4.2Three texts that decide a lot

DOCUMENTED Romans 7: who is the "I"? "I do not do what I want, but I do the very thing I hate" (7:15).

  • The Greek Fathers read it as Paul before conversion.
  • Augustine first agreed, then changed his view during the Pelagian controversy to read it as Paul the Christian. Luther and Calvin followed that later view.
  • Werner Kümmel, Römer 7 und die Bekehrung des Paulus (1929), argued that the "I" is a rhetorical device, not autobiography. That became a major turning point in the passage's interpretation.
  • N.T. Wright reads the "I" as Israel under the Torah.
  • C.H. Dodd and G.K. Beale hear Adam in vv. 7–11 (Vox Reformata survey, 2018).
  • Stanley Stowers, A Rereading of Romans (Yale, 1994), reads 7:7–25 as prosopopoeia, speech-in-character, voicing a Gentile trying to master the passions through the law (review).
  • Krister Stendahl's "The Apostle Paul and the Introspective Conscience of the West" (HTR 56 (1963): 199–215) argued that reading Romans 7 as a guilty conscience is a Western, Augustinian–Lutheran projection onto a Paul who called himself "blameless" (Cambridge).

DOCUMENTED Galatians 3:24: paidagōgos. A paidagōgos was a household slave who escorted and supervised a boy until he came of age. He was not a teacher (Encyclopedia of the Bible, "Custodian"). Norman H. Young, "Paidagogos: The Social Setting of a Pauline Metaphor," Novum Testamentum 29 (1987), traced how the image stresses a temporary, restrictive role, not instruction leading to Christ (record). Translations encode the choice: KJV "schoolmaster" (teaching), ESV "guardian," NRSVue "disciplinarian" (restriction).

DOCUMENTED Romans 10:4: telos. NRSVue: "Christ is the culmination of the law." Greek telos can mean termination ("end," as in many traditional renderings) or goal. The NRSVue's "culmination" leans toward fulfilment over abolition. This is a live translation decision of the kind Translations §5 tracks.

DOCUMENTED Galatians 2:16: pistis Christou. The NRSVue main text reads "justified not by the works of the law but through the faith of Jesus Christ," with "faith in Jesus Christ" in a footnote (NRSVue at BibleGateway). That reverses the ESV and NIV, which put "faith in" in the text. Richard B. Hays, The Faith of Jesus Christ (1983), is the landmark argument for the subjective genitive: Christ's own faithfulness, not the believer's faith (profile).

INFERENCE This is the kind of committee choice Translations §5 tracks. Someone reading the NRSVue meets a contested reading of Galatians 2:16 without being told it is contested.

DOCUMENTED The New Perspective. Sanders laid the foundation (§3.2). N.T. Wright used the phrase "a new perspective" in his 1978 Tyndale New Testament Lecture: "a new way of looking at Paul which provides… not only an advance in the debate between Stendahl and Käsemann but also a new perspective on other related Pauline problems" ("The Paul of History and the Apostle of Faith," Tyndale Bulletin 29 (1978): 61–88; PDF). James D.G. Dunn made it the movement's name in his 1982 Manson Memorial Lecture, published as "The New Perspective on Paul" (BJRL 65 (1983): 95–122; text). Wright's best-known statement of the ecclesiological reading is that justification "wasn't so much about soteriology as about ecclesiology; not so much about salvation as about the church" (What Saint Paul Really Said, 1997; quoted at Ligonier). His main works on the topic are The Climax of the Covenant: Christ and the Law in Pauline Theology (T&T Clark, 1991; Fortress ed. 1992; St Andrews), Paul and the Faithfulness of God (Fortress, 2013; Publishers Weekly), and Galatians (Eerdmans, 2021; Eerdmans).

DOCUMENTED The critics.

  • Stephen Westerholm (McMaster University) wrote Israel's Law and the Church's Faith (Eerdmans, 1988; Eerdmans) and Perspectives Old and New on Paul: The "Lutheran" Paul and His Critics (Eerdmans, 2004). The 2004 book first surveys the "Lutheran" readers and their twentieth-century critics, then argues that the core of the "Lutheran" reading is substantially right, while taking some nuance from the NPP (Themelios review).
  • Thomas R. Schreiner argued against Sanders in "Is Perfect Obedience to the Law Possible? A Re-Examination of Galatians 3:10," JETS 27:2 (1984): 151–160 (Galaxie), and in The Law and Its Fulfillment: A Pauline Theology of Law (Baker, 1993). Study Notes §9.5.3 sets his reading of Gal 3:10 alongside Dunn's and Wright's.
  • Mark A. Seifrid wrote Justification by Faith: The Origin and Development of a Central Pauline Theme (Brill, 1992), co-edited Justification and Variegated Nomism, and wrote Christ, Our Righteousness (IVP, 2000; IVP).
  • Peter Stuhlmacher (Tübingen) wrote Revisiting Paul's Doctrine of Justification: A Challenge to the New Perspective (IVP, 2001), with an essay by Donald Hagner (Themelios review).
  • John Piper's The Future of Justification: A Response to N.T. Wright (Crossway, 2007) drew Wright's book-length reply, Justification: God's Plan and Paul's Vision (2009) (Banner of Truth review; IVP announcement).

DOCUMENTED Paul within Judaism. Mark D. Nanos and Magnus Zetterholm, eds., Paul within Judaism: Restoring the First-Century Context to the Apostle (Fortress, 2015; JTS review). Paula Fredriksen, Paul: The Pagans' Apostle (Yale, 2017; Yale UP). This school treats both the traditional reading and the NPP as Christian readings in which Paul leaves Judaism behind. It argues that Paul's polemic against "works of the law" is aimed at Gentiles taking up circumcision, not at Jews keeping Torah.

INFERENCE What the camps actually disagree about. All four agree that Paul thinks Gentiles in Christ shouldn't be circumcised. Their disagreement is about why, and whether the reason applies to Jews too. The traditional reading says the reason is universal: no one is justified by deeds. The NPP says it's ecclesial: God's people aren't defined by Jewish markers. Paul within Judaism says it's specific to Gentiles: Gentiles are saved as Gentiles. Study Notes §9.5.2–9.5.3 applies this to Galatians. Its observation that "the New Perspective" is not one reading still stands.

§6Continuity Between the Covenants: The Christian Frameworks

DOCUMENTED The earliest ruling. The Jerusalem decree in Acts 15:28–29 asks Gentile believers to "abstain from what has been sacrificed to idols and from blood and from what is strangled and from sexual immorality." It doesn't ask for circumcision. Markus Bockmuehl, Jewish Law in Gentile Churches (T&T Clark, 2000), reads the decree in light of Jewish halakhah for resident aliens, close to the Noahide logic in §1 (review). The Gospels add their own data point: Mark's narrator says of Jesus, "Thus he declared all foods clean" (Mark 7:19), while Matthew's Jesus says, "I have come not to abolish but to fulfill" (Matt 5:17).

Thomist three-way division The Old Law has moral precepts (natural law, still binding), ceremonial precepts (worship, fulfilled in Christ), and judicial precepts (Israel's civil law, expired) Aquinas, ST I–II q.99 a.4: "We must therefore distinguish three kinds of precept in the Old Law…" (CCEL)

Post-1965 Catholic teaching God's covenant with the Jewish people is irrevocable. The new covenant fulfils the earlier covenants rather than revoking them, and Christ remains the one Redeemer Nostra Aetate §4 (28 Oct 1965); "The Gifts and the Calling of God Are Irrevocable" (10 Dec 2015) §27, §36, §40; DDF and two other dicasteries, A Journey of Hope (16 Sep 2026) §13

DOCUMENTED The Catholic sequence, in its own words.

  • 1965. Nostra Aetate §4: "the Jews should not be spoken of as rejected or accursed as if this followed from holy scripture" (ADL backgrounder).
  • 2015. The Pontifical Commission for Religious Relations with the Jews said the new covenant "does not revoke the earlier covenants, but brings them to fulfilment" (§27). It said that how Jews share in salvation without explicitly confessing Christ "is and remains an unfathomable divine mystery" (§36). And it said the Church "neither conducts nor supports any specific institutional mission work directed towards Jews" (§40) (Vatican press dossier, Italian).
  • 2026. On 16 September 2026 the Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith, with the dicasteries for Christian Unity and for Interreligious Dialogue, issued A Journey of Hope: Sixty Years of Interreligious Dialogue and Fraternity in the Light of the Conciliar Declaration Nostra Aetate, approved by Pope Leo XIV. It says: "God's covenant with the Jewish people is irrevocable, and this continually challenges the Church." It adds that this "does not mean, for Christians, that there are two paths to salvation," and that "the religious life of contemporary Jews… is relevant to Christians" (§13) (Vatican).

DOCUMENTED Naming supersessionism. R. Kendall Soulen, The God of Israel and Christian Theology (Fortress, 1996), distinguishes three types (Jules Isaac Stichting summary):

  • Economic: Israel's preparatory role is complete once Christ comes.
  • Punitive: Israel is displaced as punishment for rejecting Jesus.
  • Structural: the standard Christian narrative (creation → fall → Christ → consummation) skips Israel, so Israel stops mattering to how Christians understand God.

INFERENCE Soulen's types help with reading the table. Most mainstream frameworks after 1965 have dropped punitive supersessionism explicitly. Many still hold an economic version without saying so: "fulfilment" can mean either fulfilled and continuing, or fulfilled and finished. The real question is which of those a given framework means.

DOCUMENTED The Jewish side of the question. Rabbinic Judaism has no "old covenant" in the Christian sense. Torah remains God's instruction to Israel, interpreted through the oral tradition, and Gentiles are judged by the Noahide standard (§1). In Jewish framing, the Christian question of whether the old covenant is still valid is a question about someone else's text.

§7Audits

Each audit takes a claim that is common in conversation, or one this corpus itself recorded earlier, and checks it against the sources above.

⚑First-Century Jews Earned Salvation by Keeping the LawCHECKED 20 OCT 2026Overstated▶
AS RECORDED

This is common in Christian preaching and in older scholarship: Judaism was a religion of works-righteousness, and Paul discovered grace in opposition to it.

STATUS

not supported as a description of Judaism in general. A narrower version survives as a live debate.

AUDIT

DOCUMENTED Sanders's survey of Palestinian Jewish literature from 200 BCE to 200 CE found the covenant consistently framed as coming before obedience, with atonement built in (§3.2). His one exception, 4 Ezra, comes after 70 CE. The Torah says of itself that it is "not too hard" (Deut 30:11), and Paul calls himself "blameless" under it (Phil 3:6). The critics (Justification and Variegated Nomism, Gathercole) did not bring the caricature back. They argued for a more varied picture, with more weight on final judgment by works in some texts (§3.3).

CORRECTED

As a blanket description of Second Temple Judaism, the claim fails. The narrower claim, that some Second Temple texts tie final vindication to obedience, is supported and still disputed. Anyone using the broad claim should be pointed to Sanders. Anyone saying "Sanders proved no Jew ever relied on works" should be pointed to Sanders's own exception and to Gathercole.

WHY IT LOOKED RIGHT

INFERENCE:** Paul's contrast between "works of the law" and faith (Gal 2:16) reads naturally as a contrast between two ways of salvation. For centuries Christian readers met Judaism only through Paul's polemic, not through Jewish sources.

⚑Christians Keep the Moral Law but Not the Ceremonial or Civil LawCHECKED 20 OCT 2026Overstated▶
AS RECORDED

This is the usual Christian answer to "Why keep Leviticus 18 but not Leviticus 19:19?"

STATUS

a later theological framework, presented as if the text stated it.

AUDIT

DOCUMENTED The three-way division is spelled out by Aquinas (ST I–II q.99 a.4) and made confessional in Westminster Confession 19.3–19.5 (§6). Aquinas grounds it in the wording of Deut 6:1 ("precepts… ceremonies… judgments"), which is a reading of that verse, not a classification the text announces.

DOCUMENTED The Torah doesn't sort its own commands this way. Leviticus 19 puts "you shall love your neighbor as yourself" (19:18) right next to the bans on mixed breeding, mixed seed, and mixed fabrics (19:19), with no change of category. Paul himself, in 1 Cor 7:19, sets circumcision against "the commandments of God," which is a cut inside the law that the law doesn't make (§4).

CORRECTED

The division has a long and serious pedigree, and it is coherent as Christian theology. It is not a distinction the Torah draws. Anyone presenting it as what the Bible says is presenting an interpretation as text. This connects to YOURS Study Notes §1.5 (selective law-keeping) and Observations §22.9.

WHY IT LOOKED RIGHT

INFERENCE:** Some commands do look universal (murder, theft) and others look Israel-specific (sacrifice, purity), so the sorting feels intuitive. The problem is where the line falls. Sexual and dietary rules sit side by side in the same holiness code.

Schreiner's works on the topic are the 1984 JETS article on Galatians 3:10 and The Law and Its Fulfillment (1993). The Cited Persons entry is corrected.

⚑The New Covenant Replaced God's Covenant with IsraelCHECKED 20 OCT 2026Overstated▶
AS RECORDED

This is the replacement (supersessionist) reading: the church is the new Israel, and the Sinai covenant is over.

STATUS

a documented, historically dominant Christian position, but not the only reading the New Testament supports.

AUDIT

DOCUMENTED Hebrews says it directly: "In speaking of a new covenant, he has made the first one obsolete, and what is obsolete and growing old will soon disappear" (Heb 8:13). That verse closes Hebrews' quotation of Jer 31:31–34 (Heb 8:8–12), often described as the longest Old Testament quotation in the New Testament (Reformed Homiletics).

DOCUMENTED Paul says the opposite about the people: "has God rejected his people? By no means!" (Rom 11:1), and "as regards election they are beloved for the sake of their ancestors, for the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable" (Rom 11:28–29). Jeremiah's own context ties the new covenant to Israel's permanence (Jer 31:35–37).

INFERENCE Hebrews talks about the covenant administration: priesthood, sanctuary, sacrifice. Romans 11 talks about the people's election. A reader can hold both: the Sinai cult is surpassed, and Israel's calling is not revoked. That is roughly where Catholic teaching has landed (1965, 2015, 2026; §6). The replacement reading puts the two claims together into one, and neither text makes that move.

CORRECTED

Hebrews supports "the first covenant is obsolete." Romans supports "Israel is not rejected." The Catholic Church has revised its own earlier teaching in official documents. Anyone presenting replacement as simply "what the Bible teaches" is leaving out Romans 9–11.

WHY IT LOOKED RIGHT

INFERENCE:** Hebrews 8:13 is short, quotable, and unambiguous on its own terms. Romans 9–11 is long and argued in steps, so it is quoted far less often.
